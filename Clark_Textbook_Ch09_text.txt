CHAPTER 9
Revolution
The War for Independence, 1776–1783
“A few more such victories would have shortly put an end to British dominion in America.”
— General Henry Clinton, on Bunker Hill, 1775
On July 1, 1776, Pennsylvania’s John Dickinson, who had argued the colonies’ case against Parliament for a decade and who would refuse to sign the Declaration, warned Congress that to declare independence unprepared meant to “brave the Storm in a Skiff made of Paper.” Dickinson understood the odds. Great Britain possessed overwhelming military and financial advantages, and the colonies remained fragmented and faced considerable internal opposition from those still loyal to the crown. The Patriots won through the combined efforts of colonial militias and the Continental Army, Washington’s leadership, the colonists’ determination to continue the fight, and crucial help from European allies. The diplomatic efforts of Benjamin Franklin and John Adams secured the crucial assistance of France, which contributed directly to the victory over Britain. In addition, women, Native Americans, and African Americans played a significant role in the war, and each group measured the promises of the Revolution against its results. Independence raised a larger question: who would enjoy the promises of the new nation, and who would decide? The men who had won the war largely retained that authority, determining whose claims to liberty and equality would carry weight in the new republic.

## Part I · An Army to Invent


### Context, 1776
Context, 1776: In 1754, Benjamin Franklin predicted that population growth in the colonies would necessitate them becoming self-governing entities by the mid-19th century. When the American population was larger than the British population, he predicted, each of the 13 colonies would peacefully become self-governing, each with its own independently operated government. The Crown would still be considered head of state, he thought, which would bind the colonies to the British empire and allow for a continuation of trade and military protection. However, the policies enacted upon the colonies in the aftermath of the French and Indian War soon soured relations between the two entities. Colonial leaders openly objected to Parliament’s treatment of the colonies, citing English political tradition, the English Bill of Rights, and several principles of the Enlightenment. Between 1763 and 1774, the Crown and the colonies exchanged several back-and-forth responses, each escalating in violence and intensity, with both attempting to impose their will upon the other.
In 1774, following the enactment of the punishing Intolerable Acts, representatives from the colonies gathered for the first time in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania to address the colonists’ grievances and called for a peaceful resolution of differences. Despite their efforts to remedy the conflict, tensions continued to escalate, culminating in the military skirmishes in the Spring of 1775 at Lexington, Concord, and Bunker Hill, Massachusetts. The congress that reassembled in May of 1775, after a war had begun, met under completely different circumstances, resulting in more aggressive measures designed to organize colonial governments, mobilize an army, choose a general, and to make themselves known to the world by declaring their sovereignty and independence from Great Britain. In July 1776, Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, drafted by Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson did not intend the document to be a revolutionary manifesto, but a legal brief that justified total separation and removal of all political ties to Great Britain. The Declaration begins with “self-evident” truths, or Natural Rights that are given to all men. More importantly, the Declaration of Independence became the foundation for something much more significant than independence for the colonies. As Thomas Paine said in, “Common Sense,” one of the most inspirational documents written in American history, “We have it in our power to begin the world over again.” Therefore, the American Revolution was about more than simply replacing the British government with a new regime; the founders believed they were creating a new model of government for the world. Whom that model would include, the Declaration did not say. The women, Native nations, and African Americans who close this chapter asked the question. All that remained was to prove the divinity of their cause on the battlefield, albeit against the most powerful fighting force on the face of the earth.

### The Continental Army
The Continental Army: A significant outcome of the 2nd Continental Congress was the unanimous appointing of George Washington as General of the newly commissioned Continental Army. Washington, a seasoned veteran of the French and Indian War, was undoubtedly the most qualified individual for the assignment. Once in command, Washington reorganized the army, secured arms and provisions, and began turning raw recruits into soldiers who could hold a line. As a field general, Washington was not the most skilled commander. Despite losing more battles than he won, Washington’s strong personality and sterling reputation garnered him the support and respect of American soldiers. By the time the Declaration of Independence was signed, the Continental Army, which was composed of state militias and members of the army, had successfully defeated the British in several battles, including the capture of Boston. Even the loss at Bunker Hill provided the Patriots with confidence and a belief that they were formidable opponents to the greatest professional army in the world.
Following the disastrous showing at Bunker Hill and with accusations of conflicting personal feelings (General Thomas Gage was married to colonist Margaret Gage) Gage was replaced as Commander in Chief over the colonies by William Howe. Howe, who reluctantly conceded Boston to Washington, regrouped his forces and coordinated a successful invasion of New York. Howe’s forces chased Washington and the retreating Continental Army across the East River, but failed to pursue the weakened army across the Delaware River, choosing instead to settle into winter quarters in New York and its New Jersey outposts.

### Crossing the Delaware
Crossing the Delaware: Following the retreat into Pennsylvania, Washington engaged the British in quick, strong strikes and then retreated as a means of overcoming the inadequate training of American forces and to boost morale. On Christmas night and morning of December 26, 1776, the American forces daringly crossed the icy Delaware River to engage the British. Consisting of primarily German Hessian mercenaries (Britain’s army was spread incredibly thin around the world, forcing Parliament to purchase the services of foreign soldiers), British forces were celebrating the holiday, drinking and enjoying themselves. Despite a warning from a scout regarding movements of Washington’s army, the Hessians failed to prepare, insisting such a move was highly unlikely. In a surprise attack at Trenton, New Jersey, the Patriots overran the Hessians in a dominant victory. However, the success was short lived.
Trenton mattered because of the arithmetic underneath it. The Patriots did not have to defeat the British Empire. They did not have to march on London or conquer anything at all. They only had to keep an army in the field and a government in being until the most powerful nation on earth tired of paying for the war. Britain had to win. America only had to avoid losing. Muhammad Ali gave the strategy its modern name in 1974, when he leaned back on the ropes against George Foreman, covered up, and let the bigger man punch himself into exhaustion (when Foreman’s arms came down in the eighth round, Ali knocked him out). Much like the rope-a-dope, Washington lost battle after battle and kept his army alive, while Britain won battle after battle three thousand miles from home and grew tired. Ironically, the Confederacy would try the same strategy against the Union, with the United States, this time, in Britain’s corner.

## Part II · The Turning


### The Plan That Broke in Half
The Plan That Broke in Half: As Washington remained in the Pennsylvania countryside, General Horatio Gates commanded colonial forces in upstate New York awaiting British forces from Canada. Coordinating the war effort from London, Lord George Germain instructed General John Burgoyne to march his forces south from Lake Champlain towards the Hudson River, while General Howe was expected to move north from New York to meet him. Howe went to Philadelphia instead. The successful union of the two armies was meant to divide New England from the rest of the colonies; isolating Washington from Gates. Already losing popular support in London, the war effort against the colonies was in desperate need for a quick and decisive victory.

### Saratoga
Saratoga: Hindered by a large procession and difficult terrain, Burgoyne’s army labored slowly, which allowed for Gates’ forces to fortify themselves and secure the area called Saratoga. However, Gates, who had secretly petitioned the Continental Congress to replace Washington as Commander of the Continental Army, was notoriously timid and desired to merely defend the location, fearing any military mistake would erase his chances to assume command. As Gates remained entrenched in his quarters, an impatient Major General, Benedict Arnold, requested the opportunity to engage the British. Gates reluctantly provided Arnold with a mere two thousand of the twelve thousand available and with a warning that should Arnold need reinforcements, they would not be available. Nonetheless, Arnold, with a regiment of colonial sharp shooters led by Daniel Morgan, readied themselves for attack. Positioned in treetops and out of range of British musket fire, Morgan’s snipers targeted Burgoyne’s officers and artillery. On horseback and dressed in elaborate uniforms, the British officers became easy targets for the accurate long rifles. The successful guerrilla-style tactics, considered cowardly by the British, wreaked havoc upon Burgoyne’s army. As the officers fell from Patriot marksmen, the British regulars suffered from a lack of organization and battlefield maneuvering. Capitalizing on the confusion of the British soldiers, Arnold charged into the chaos of devastating artillery and grape shot (a cannon-fired spray of iron balls). The Continentals overran Burgoyne’s army, forcing the surrender of nearly 6,000 soldiers. Considered the turning point of the war, the victory at Saratoga increased the opposition against the war in London and simultaneously convinced the French to officially enter the war as an American ally.
Arnold’s story did not end at Saratoga. He believed his achievements entitled him to advancement, and when the army and Congress failed to reward him as he expected, his resentment grew. Wounded, in financial trouble, and married to Peggy Shippen (whose Philadelphia family kept British sympathies and contacts), he opened a secret correspondence with the British in May 1779, while he commanded in Philadelphia. In 1780 he sought and won the command at West Point, the fortress that guarded the Hudson, and agreed to deliver it for money and a British commission. The plan collapsed when British Major John André fell into American hands carrying the papers that exposed it. Arnold escaped to the British lines, and the Patriots hanged André as a spy. Arnold’s next appearance in this war came in a British uniform, burning Richmond.
It is tempting to turn Arnold into a villain from the beginning, as if the traitor had always been hiding beneath the uniform. That makes the story simpler, and less revealing. The same ambition that drove him through the woods at Freeman’s Farm made him vulnerable to the promise of recognition from another power. That choice does not erase his earlier service. His earlier service does not excuse his betrayal. As I tell my students, especially when it comes to the actions of men in times of war, the difference between treason and patriotism is determined largely by the outcome. Our final judgment of their actions should include more than the final score. On the battlefield today, a monument marks the spot where Arnold’s leg was shattered at Breymann’s Redoubt — a carved boot — and his name appears nowhere on it. The victors decided whose names belonged in the story of their Revolution. The monument records that decision: the deed without the man.

### Valley Forge
Valley Forge: Without a suitable place to hold his army, Washington was forced to make winter camp in a desolate field near Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, while the British enjoyed the comforts of Philadelphia. The inferiority of the Continental Army, (funding, weapons, and supplies) became glaringly evident as Washington’s forces were forced to endure the harsh winter conditions. Enlistments in the Continental Army were from one to three years and with meager pay. Facing a shortage of food and inferior weapons, the army often had to scavenge to find supplies of food, clothing, and even firewood. Furthermore, disease, brought on by close confinement poor diet and sanitation, became a greater danger than the British Army. The most common camp killers were influenza, typhus, typhoid, and dysentery. The new Continental Army, faced with these challenges, limited their deadly effects through the work of dedicated surgeons, capable nurses, and a smallpox inoculation program. The women at Valley Forge, wives, widows, and camp followers alike, performed the essential work of cooking, washing, and tending to the sick, often on half rations. Black soldiers filled the ranks that winter as well, among them Salem Poor, a freedman whose conduct at Bunker Hill fourteen officers had petitioned the Massachusetts legislature to reward. Washington had barred them from serving in 1775, but when the enlistments ran out, he allowed free Black veterans to reenlist. The army accepted those willing to serve; the republic would take much longer to accept them as equals. Washington repeatedly demanded clothing, arms, and supplies from the Continental Congress, but to no avail. The congress, forced out of Philadelphia by the British army and fearing capture, lacked the authority and capability to fund the army.
Washington’s leadership abilities and skill for maintaining discipline proved instrumental at Valley Forge. Despite the horrifying conditions and human suffering, the Continental Army persevered with incredible resolve and the assistance of two European military leaders; Prussian Baron von Steuben and the Marquis de Lafayette of France. Baron Friedrich Wilhelm Augustus von Steuben (try fitting that on a driver’s license) had been recommended to Benjamin Franklin by the French Minister of War as someone who would be helpful in developing the American army. Steuben, who was rumored to have been discharged from the Prussian army for being gay, quickly became instrumental in the transformation of the Continental Army from a disorganized, unsanitary unit into a formidable fighting force. Upon his arrival in Valley Forge, Steuben reorganized the Patriot encampment and retrained the army by drilling critical maneuver and fire tactics of eighteenth century warfare. He created a military manual, which Alexander Hamilton, George Washington’s close aide, translated into English. Lafayette, too, helped make professionals of the soldiers who wintered at Valley Forge. Barely twenty, rich, well connected, and a true believer in the American cause, the Frenchman offered his sword to Washington and used his connections to pry loose French aid. Lafayette worked closely with Washington, helping throughout the American Revolution. Valley Forge essentially became a boot camp to develop the American soldiers into more knowledgeable and skilled fighters.

### The Other Battlefield
The Other Battlefield: Following his success as a printer, Benjamin Franklin desired to rise within English society as a gentleman and statesman. Known for his scientific experiments, literature, and novel inventions, Franklin’s politics and skills as a diplomat are largely ignored. In 1754, Franklin advocated for a political union among the British colonies in North America. A Democratic Republic, like the one observed among the Iroquois Confederacy, was his goal for the colonies. However, his Albany Plan of Union never materialized. During the French & Indian War, Franklin arrived in London as an official colonial agent for Pennsylvania. Franklin attended the coronation ceremony of King George III, and later proclaimed the British Isles had “the best Constitution and the best King any Nation was ever blest with.” In 1766, as tensions rose back in the colonies over the passage of the Stamp Act, Franklin was summoned to testify before the British House of Commons. Parliament decided to repeal the Stamp Act, which garnered Franklin incredible popularity among the colonies and equal disdain with British officials. Franklin even successfully lobbied the ministry to appoint his son, William, as Governor of New Jersey.
Tensions between England and Massachusetts reached new heights, resulting in the Boston Massacre and the dumping of nearly ten thousand pounds sterling of East India Company tea into the Boston Harbor. As relations worsened with the colonies, so did Franklin’s reputation in Parliament. Franklin, whose sole desire was to be recognized among the highest of British society, brought with him to England his own unique style. His fur hat and rustic-looking attire became quite fashionable in London. In 1774, Franklin had established himself as a distinguished member of both colonial and London society. In addition to serving as colonial agent for multiple colonies, Franklin held the Crown’s appointment as deputy postmaster general for North America. However, following the Boston Tea Party, many within the British government suspected Franklin to be complicit in the rebellious behavior in Boston. Franklin obtained highly sensitive letters from Massachusetts royal Governor, Thomas Hutchinson, in which Hutchinson admittedly desired to increase the tension and conflict between England and Massachusetts. Franklin secretly turned over these letters to Boston newspapers, which after their publication, led to the Massachusetts Assembly demanding Hutchinson’s resignation. As a result, Franklin was summoned to the Privy Council, where matters were debated before appearing before Parliament. Franklin, who thought of himself as British, and dressed in a new suit, appeared before the British officials in the “cockpit,” so named for the cock fighting which took place there during the reign of Henry VIII. For over an hour, Franklin was ridiculed and insulted. His character, reputation, and integrity had been destroyed, [he] “has forfeited all the respect of societies and of men.” “He will henceforth esteem it a libel to be called a man of letters,” a pun on Franklin’s position as postmaster. The personal attack was more than Franklin could bear. Furthermore, he was informed that he had been removed by King George as Colonial Postmaster General. That afternoon transformed a loyal British citizen into one of the most instrumental forces in the achievement of American independence.

### Ambassadors to France
Ambassadors to France: The second most important goal of the 2nd Continental Congress was the acquisition of European allies. Following his assistance with creating and passing the Declaration of Independence, Franklin went to France as a commissioner in late 1776 and became minister in 1778. Other diplomatic efforts include John Jay, appointed minister to Spain in 1779 and John Adams, appointed minister to Holland in 1780. Franklin arrived in France with a reputation already made. The French knew his electrical experiments and inventions, and admired the wit and unpretentious manner that made him such an effective representative. His French remained hesitant, and his grammar was often shaky. His hosts praised him anyway. In 1778 Congress named Franklin its minister plenipotentiary to France, and when he presented his credentials at the French court in 1779, he became the first American minister to a foreign government. His presence in Paris annoyed the British government, who sent several spies to monitor Franklin. As the War for Independence entered its third year, the Continental Army had suffered devastating defeats and Washington’s men remained on the verge of breaking at Valley Forge. A unification with France was paramount. Franklin effectively secured funds (secretly as not to inform England) from the French and even managed to recommend the Marquis de Lafayette and Baron von Steuben to assist General Washington. Yet despite spending more than a year among French officials and royalty, Franklin had yet to secure an official alliance. In January of 1778, the Continental Congress sent John Adams to assist Franklin in his negotiations. However, unbeknownst to Adams or the congress, the Patriot victory at Saratoga convinced the French to officially join the war as an American ally. Upon arriving in Paris, Adams quickly became irritated with Franklin’s lazy and lavish lifestyle. The author of “Poor Richard’s Almanac” and author of the proverb, “Early to bed, early to rise,” regularly failed to awake before ten in the morning. Adams wrote in his diary “These incessant dinners and dissipations were not the objects of my mission to France”. Adams missed the point of those evenings. In France, politics and pleasure went together in ways he had never encountered. Franklin moved easily through high society, trading jokes and flirting with women. Adams complained to the congress that, in addition to his ineffectiveness, Franklin spent frivolously and without any documentation of his expenses. Adams, who claimed to have developed a better ability to speak French than Franklin, remained unpopular in French courts and a burden upon Franklin’s agenda. Once the congress became aware of France’s allegiance, Adams was removed from Paris and sent to Amsterdam to acquire credit from the Dutch. Ultimately, the assistance of French military and money proved to be too much for Britain to overcome. Without French money and arms, American independence could not have been won.

## Part III · The War Moves South


### Clinton’s Gamble
Clinton’s Gamble: In addition to Virginia’s open rebellion, the failure to apprehend Washington’s debilitated army at Valley Forge and the loss at Saratoga resulted in the removal of General Howe, who was replaced by General Henry Clinton.
Clinton was assigned to oversee military operations in North America and to spare troops for the Caribbean. Unlike his predecessor, Clinton, took nothing off the table to ensure a British victory. Furthermore, Clinton opted to occupy the Southern colonies by laying siege to Charleston, South Carolina, and later raiding into Virginia. To assist him in this endeavor, Clinton utilized the unconventional and brutal services of Sir Banastre Tarleton. Clinton ordered Colonel Tarleton to stamp out Patriot resistance in the Carolinas. At Waxhaws, South Carolina, a cavalry charge by Tarleton’s men broke a column of some 350 Virginia Continentals. By Patriot account, Tarleton’s men cut down soldiers trying to surrender, a move that gave rise to the phrase “Tarleton’s Quarter,” the Patriots’ name for the brutal treatment they believed British forces had inflicted on their men. Along with Tarleton, General Charles Cornwallis orchestrated a series of devastating victories in the South.
That same year, 1779, Washington sent General John Sullivan into Iroquois country with orders for “the total destruction and devastation of their settlements.” Three years earlier, American expeditions had burned Cherokee towns across the southern frontier. The war Clinton prepared to carry into the Carolinas already had a second front along the frontier, one that most maps of the Revolution leave out.
Congress chose the general, and the choice proved disastrous at Camden. In June 1780, after the fall of Charleston, Congress appointed Horatio Gates to command the independent Southern Department. The victor of Saratoga arrived with a reputation that Benedict Arnold and Daniel Morgan had largely earned for him. On August 16, near Camden, Gates met Cornwallis with nearly 3,700 men against roughly 2,200 British. Gates placed his militia on the left and his Continentals on the right. The militia broke on the first British volley and fled, while the Maryland and Delaware Continentals under Johann de Kalb stood their ground and died where they stood. De Kalb received a mortal wound. American losses ran above a thousand, and British casualties amounted to a third of that number.
Gates left the field with the fleeing militia and kept riding. Three weeks later, Alexander Hamilton wrote that the general had covered 180 miles in three and a half days, a pace that “does admirable credit to the activity of a man at his time of life” but “disgraces the General and the Soldiers.” Congress had now lost two southern armies in four months. This time it allowed Washington to choose, and he chose Nathanael Greene.
The battle that followed had almost no British army in it. On October 7, 1780, on a rocky Carolina hilltop, roughly nine hundred Patriot militia from the far side of the Appalachians met more than a thousand Loyalist militia. The only British officer present, Major Patrick Ferguson, commanded the Loyalists. The Overmountain Men carried rifles that outranged the Loyalists’ muskets, attacked uphill from every side, and used the woods for cover. Ferguson remained mounted, took repeated hits, and his horse carried his body down the slope. The Loyalists surrendered shortly afterward. Patriot losses stayed under a hundred, while Loyalist casualties, including prisoners, exceeded a thousand.
Surrender flags went up and the firing continued. A week later, at Biggerstaff’s, the Patriot colonels tried thirty-six Loyalist prisoners. They condemned at least twelve and hanged nine from an oak that night before Isaac Shelby stopped the proceedings. The riflemen remembered the Waxhaws, and the story of denied quarter produced no order, only a willingness to keep shooting after the white flag appeared. Kings Mountain revealed the Revolution as a civil war, in which Americans settled scores with one another under the cover of a larger conflict.
That civil war had consequences for choosing sides, and it was the Revolution’s first decision about who belonged. Perhaps a fifth of white Americans sided with the Crown. Every state enacted laws against Loyalists: loyalty oaths, banishment, and confiscation of property. When the war ended, some sixty thousand Loyalists left the United States, white and Black, free and enslaved, for Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Quebec, Britain, and the Caribbean. The Treaty of Paris called for the restoration of confiscated Loyalist property, but many states chose to ignore the congressional request. The war had ended. The question of who belonged had yet to be answered.

### The Swamp Fox
The Swamp Fox: General Washington, who remained fixated on liberating New York, relied on his Southern militias, led by French and Indian War veteran, Francis Marion. Known as the “Swamp Fox,” Marion used guerrilla tactics to attack and avoid the British forces (See the movie, The Patriot).
Nathanael Greene never won a pitched battle in the South and still won the campaign. He took command of the Southern Department on December 3, 1780. Facing a larger British force, Greene divided his own army against every conventional rule. The main body moved east to the Pee Dee, while Daniel Morgan took the light troops west of the Catawba to threaten British posts and raise militia. As a result, Cornwallis had to divide as well, and he sent Tarleton after Morgan.
At Cowpens on January 17, 1781, Morgan asked his militia for only two volleys and then withdrew them behind the Continentals. The British charged what looked like a rout and ran into formed regulars. William Washington’s horsemen struck the British right as the militia re-formed on the left. The fighting ended by eight in the morning. American casualties stayed under 150, while British losses, including prisoners, approached 870.
Cornwallis burned his baggage train to move faster and still failed to catch Greene, who crossed the Dan into Virginia with every boat on the south bank. When Greene recrossed in late February, he brought roughly 4,400 men against a British force reduced by desertion and the loss of its supplies. At Guilford Courthouse on March 15, he repeated Morgan’s formation on a larger scale. Greene lost the field but inflicted casualties Cornwallis could not easily replace, roughly a quarter of the British army engaged. In the crisis of the fighting, British guns fired grapeshot into the melee and killed their own men.
Charles James Fox told the House of Commons, “Another such victory would ruin the British army.” Greene himself wrote, “We fight, get beat, rise and fight again.” Cornwallis pulled back to Wilmington, found himself still unable to face Greene, and turned north into Virginia. In essence, the general who never won a pitched battle had left the British holding little more than the coastal towns.

### Yorktown
Yorktown: In early 1781, Lord Germain sent commands from London to General Clinton to take the colony of Virginia. Led by the hero of Saratoga and recent traitor, Benedict Arnold, the British attacked and burned Richmond. Additional British forces led by General Cornwallis also left North Carolina for Virginia, hoping to cut off General Washington from the rest of the Continental Army. On Clinton’s instructions, Cornwallis chose to fortify his men in Yorktown, Virginia, a small tobacco port on the Chesapeake Bay, where he would await reinforcements from General Clinton. Understanding the importance of Virginia, Washington sent the Marquis de Lafayette to meet Cornwallis while he remained focused on the liberation of New York. However, once word arrived that the French navy was headed for the Chesapeake, Washington abandoned his desire to free New York, and marched his men down to Virginia. In September 1781, Washington’s Continentals and Rochambeau’s French regulars, some seventeen thousand men in all, joined Lafayette and laid siege to Cornwallis. As the Continentals slowly advanced towards Yorktown, Admiral de Grasse of the French Royal Navy, defeated a British fleet, successfully pinning down Cornwallis and simultaneously preventing the arrival of reinforcements. As the siege continued, the British soldiers began to suffer from malnutrition and malaria. Cornwallis expelled hundreds of Black refugees sick with smallpox from his lines, where they might infect the Patriot and French alliance, but to no avail. On October 17, 1781, suffering from a constant barrage of bombs, deadly diseases, and a lack of reinforcements, General Cornwallis raised the white flag. Two days later, the ceremonial surrender began. The colonial soldiers and the French soldiers stood in lines facing each other as the shamed and dismayed British soldiers walked between the lines. Too embarrassed to personally accept defeat, General Cornwallis sent his second in command to surrender his sabre and colors. Members of the world’s most professional army laid down their weapons in a pile (some angrily threw them down) as, tradition says, the British bands played an old British tune called, "The World Turned Upside Down." After six and a half years of fighting, the Revolutionary War was almost over.

## Part IV · Everyone’s War


### Everyone’s War
During the 18th century, the most common word invoked in legal and political literature was liberty. The second most common word used was slavery. The popularization of these words can be directly attributed to the arrival of the Great Awakening and Enlightenment philosophy to the colonies. Both movements sought to elevate human civilization by illustrating mankind’s rights on earth and his heavenly potential according to God’s divine plan. Thus, decades before the first shot was fired at Lexington, Massachusetts, millions of colonists, both male and female, had rededicated their physical and spiritual lives to the acquisition of freedom. The passionate and fiery sermons depicted mankind as treading upon an unsustainable path, doomed to fall into the pit of Hell for eternity, unless they received a true conversion. Furthermore, the Great Awakening was significantly more egalitarian, which empowered women, Africans, and Native Americans. Those who subscribed to this new evangelicalism desired a deep and direct connection with God, whereby their souls may be free from the torments of hell, lest they become slaves of the devil.
At the same time, the teachings of John Locke, Thomas Hobbes, and the scientific discoveries of Isaac Newton introduced a bold new theory about mankind’s natural state in the world. According to Enlightenment ideology, all mankind was born free to pursue any endeavor, education, or occupation, regardless of social class. Indeed, Locke’s Natural Rights specifically argued that liberty was God given and inalienable. Thus, by the time Britain imposed new tax laws and began restricting colonial governments, the vast majority of the colonists had already become keenly aware of liberty and its opposite state of being. Colonists widely embraced liberty, both as a sinless state and as a God-given natural right.

### Women and the Revolution
Women and the Revolution: Before the Revolution, women's lives centered largely on the household, though their work extended well beyond it. They ran households, bore children, and worked farms alongside their husbands. And under the doctrine of coverture in English Common Law, a married woman's legal rights and obligations were assumed by those of her husband. Under coverture, a married woman’s legal identity largely merged with her husband’s. He controlled her property and exercised legal authority over important decisions affecting her life. The words “to have and to hold” appeared in nearly every legal transaction between man and woman, from land deeds to marriage vows. And despite having more political autonomy than women in England, politics remained overwhelmingly a male realm. For a man, political freedom meant governing himself and exercising authority over his household. A woman’s relationship to the larger society was controlled by her husband. Women lacked the independent legal standing and property rights that political participation required. Many also had little control over the decisions that shaped their private lives, including whom they could court or marry. As late as the 1790s, many men still considered women naturally submissive and irrational, and therefore unfit for citizenship or the vote. Revolutionary leaders spoke of men's natural rights and political liberty. When they discussed women, they emphasized obligations to husbands, children, and households. The language of natural rights applied to men; women's place remained defined by the duties others expected them to perform.
However, as tensions with Great Britain began to escalate and unrest grew in the colonies, gender roles began to shift. Already familiar with Enlightenment philosophy and their duty to question unlawful authority, Patriot women became more politically aware and active in the struggle against Britain. They supported economic boycotts of British-made goods and produced materials for their families. Some attended public meetings against British rule; others raised money for the Patriot cause. With husbands and sons away in the militia or the Continental Army, women took on work traditionally assigned to men. They ran farms and shops, managed finances, and took on the responsibilities their absent husbands and sons had left behind. Married and unmarried women followed the Continental Army from camp to camp. Some followed their husbands; others sought the protection of the army. In camp, they washed clothes, mended uniforms, prepared meals, and nursed the sick and wounded.
Some women, like Deborah Sampson Gannett and the legendary Molly Pitcher, found less conventional ways to support the Patriot cause. Deborah Sampson Gannett disguised herself as a man and joined the Continental Army. The legend of Molly Pitcher tells of a woman who carried water onto the battlefield to cool the guns while her husband fought. Perhaps the most iconic woman to appear during the revolution was Abigail Adams. Abigail Adams frequently advised her husband, John, while he served in the Continental Congress. In addition, she oversaw management of the family home and other matters while her husband supported the war effort at home and overseas. During their courtship and marriage, Abigail and John exchanged more than 1,100 letters. Many of hers argued for greater rights and legal independence for women. In 1776, with her husband in Philadelphia, where he would help draft the Declaration of Independence, Abigail Adams encouraged him to "Remember the Ladies."
Through these widespread activities, women expanded traditional concepts of gender roles, proving to be highly capable and effective partners in the Revolution. By the end of the war, women’s sense of self-worth had grown immensely. The Constitution left voting qualifications to the states, and in New Jersey, eligible women could vote until 1807. After the war, women inherited a new civic assignment: raise sons fit for the republic, schooled in the virtue and Enlightenment principles that self-government required. Called Republican Motherhood, this responsibility reinforced traditional gender roles, giving women more control over the day to day activities within the home. It also inspired women to become more educated and politically active. Women were not openly clamoring for equal social status and rights, but only to have many of the restrictive laws lifted. Abigail Adams argued for greater legal independence for married women, whose rights to own property, enter contracts, and bring lawsuits remained sharply restricted under coverture. Even her own war bonds had to sit in a trust controlled by a male relative. Abigail warned John that men would become tyrants if they held unlimited power over women. Five weeks later, she returned to the contradiction: while the Patriots were “emancipating all nations,” they insisted on “retaining an absolute power over Wives.”
In her essay “On the Equality of the Sexes,” written in 1779 and published in 1790, Judith Sargent Murray held that women and men possessed equal powers of memory, while women had the stronger imagination. Murray acknowledged that women often appeared weaker than men in judgment and reasoning, but argued that their lack of education explained the difference: “We can only reason from what we know,” she wrote, because most women had been denied “the opportunity of acquiring knowledge.” The situation began to change in the 1790s, when Massachusetts's attorney general interpreted the state constitution as guaranteeing girls the same right to public schooling as boys. The Revolution provided women with valuable experience for the future, and also left them with new skills and more access to education. For many colonial women, the Revolution symbolized not just the separation from Britain, but also served as a reminder that they too were entitled to the same liberties, freedoms, and ideals they had helped fight for.

### Native Nations and the Revolution
Native Nations and the Revolution: Among the causes of the Revolution was the Proclamation of 1763, which restricted colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains. Colonists resented the boundary, but Native nations saw it as protection against further encroachment on their lands. However, by 1774, English encroachment upon native lands had become a serious concern for both Native Americans and the English. In 1775, Guy Johnson, the Superintendent of Indian Affairs, left for England with Chief Joseph Brant of the Iroquois Confederacy, to help convince the ministry to take greater measures of control. Brant was not only a mission-school-educated Mohawk Indian, but he was a communicant of the Church of England. Brant was considered by most Londoners to be the perfect example of the “Noble Savage.” Brant had grown up in both worlds and strived to both coexist with the English and maintain the traditions of his people. As the Revolutionary War began, both the colonies and England petitioned Native Americans for assistance. The Iroquois Confederacy attempted to maintain neutrality at the beginning the conflict, determining it was a “white man’s war.” As in the French and Indian War, Native Americans used the hostilities as an opportunity to profit by playing the English against one another. However, following a broken promise by the Continental Army, the Iroquois council met to decide their course of action. Four of the six nations opted to join the British, while the remaining two nations sided with the colonists. In the South, the Cherokee Nation joined the British, hoping to halt colonial encroachment. When the fighting ended, Britain and the United States drew up the Treaty of Paris without a single Native nation represented. Britain signed away everything from the Great Lakes to Florida and from the Atlantic to the Mississippi, land that belonged, by any measure but a European one, to the nations no one had invited. Allies or enemies, it made little difference: the Oneida and Tuscarora, who had fought beside the Patriots, lost their lands right along with the nations that had fought against them. Furthermore, the defeat of the British army proved to be the complete undoing of the Iroquois League, the Cherokee, and several other indigenous nations. For many Native Americans, the American Revolution marked the end for traditional Indian societies as England became powerless to prevent further incursions by white settlers into Indian Territory.

### African Americans and the Revolution
African Americans and the Revolution: By the beginning of the Revolution, the harsh and brutal conditions of the slave were widely known. Yet it was in this time that slavery assumed a central place in the language of colonial resistance. To some colonial elite, like James Otis and Samuel Adams, slaves were not some simple allegories, but real humans of flesh and blood, who were equally deserving of their freedom. But for others like John Dickinson and Edward Rutledge, the term slavery was merely a gross exaggeration of British rule. But it was because of the existence of slavery that made the comparison so powerful.
From the first protests against the new taxes, Patriot leaders described Parliament’s designs as slavery, and they made the comparison in colonies where actual slavery stood in plain view. When patriot leaders used the word slavery, they did so to illustrate their being denied the right to self-governance. For many Southern elite, it was their up close and personal connection with slavery that heightened their fears of losing their political and economic autonomy. But for more than half a million slaves living in the colonies, the revolutionary rhetoric used to justify the cause for liberty meant significantly more. In 1776, slavery was legal in every colony, and almost half of the enslaved lived in Virginia. The invocation of liberty and freedom echoed not only throughout cities and towns, but were also present among the numerous plantations of the Chesapeake and Carolinas. The hypocrisy of slave-owning patriots likening themselves to actual slaves was not only visible to those trapped in servitude, but was also recognized by Loyalists and British alike. Upon reading the Declaration of Independence, former Massachusetts governor Thomas Hutchinson asked the delegates of Maryland, Virginia, and the Carolinas in print “how their Constituents justify the depriving more than an hundred thousand Africans of their rights to liberty, and the pursuit of happiness, and in some degree to their lives, if these rights are so absolutely unalienable.” It was this exact duplicity of freedom that delegitimized the inflammatory rhetoric of Thomas Jefferson, who himself owned some two hundred people. In addition to British criticisms, many colonial anti-slavery organizations and religious leaders were quick to point out how those who kept other humans in bondage were making a mockery of the words “freedom” and “liberty.” Pamphlet after pamphlet placed freedom and slavery at opposite ends of human experience: one the summit of happiness, the other the bottom of misery. In private, Thomas Jefferson admitted the horrors of slavery, “one hour of which is fraught with more misery than ages of that which he rose in rebellion to oppose.” Such an analogy coupled with Enlightenment philosophy placed almost half of the Continental Congress in an ideological paradox. The inconsistency of slave owning in the midst of a revolution for liberty is perhaps the greatest irony in American history. Even so, a good many Americans walked out of the Revolution convinced that slavery would not survive long in a nation founded on liberty. Moreover, most of the enslaved in revolutionary America lived within a generation or two of Africa; they needed no pamphleteer to explain that they had been born free. Thus, if there were any participants in the Revolution that embodied the legitimacy of overthrowing oppression, it was the slave.
In November 1775 the royal governor of Virginia (Lord Dunmore) decided, as we saw in Chapter 8, to play upon the hypocrisy of colonial demands for liberty, when he promised freedom to any enslaved African who took up arms against the Patriots and fought for the British. An estimated 20,000 enslaved people fled to the British lines, and many fought alongside the British or aided the British army as nurses, cooks, and general laborers. The Continental Army did not want them, at least not at first: Washington and Congress both closed its ranks to Black men. Then the war ground on, enlistments ran out, the casualty lists grew, and Washington changed his mind. In January 1776 he allowed free Black men who had already served to reenlist, and within a year the requirement of prior service disappeared. In the end, more than 5,000 African Americans took up arms for the Patriot cause. New England’s militias took Black men from the start, and many of them stood on Breed’s Hill in June 1775, at the battle history misnamed for Bunker Hill. Across the North, Black and white soldiers shared the same regiments and drew the same pay, an integration the United States Army would not see again until the Korean War. The First Rhode Island Regiment, a mixed-race regiment, included several all-African units of soldiers. The First Rhode Island made the bargain plain: an enslaved man who enlisted walked free the day he passed muster. The state assembly declared, "Every slave so enlisting shall, upon his passing muster … be immediately discharged from the service of his master … and be absolutely free."
Commanded by white officers, the regiment fought at Newport in 1778 and stood in the lines at Yorktown in 1781. In March 1779, Congress urged South Carolina and Georgia to raise battalions of enslaved men, promising owners up to $1,000 for each one and the soldiers their freedom at war’s end. South Carolina and Georgia said no. Neither would trade its slaves for its independence. Once it became clear the new nation meant to keep them in bondage, thousands of African Americans chose the British and sailed away with them. The Patriot cause for “liberty” did not go unrecognized by African Americans, both enslaved and free. Many used the rhetoric of the revolution as justification for their emancipation and equal status in the newly independent nation. The irony of the War for Independence is that it widened the gap between free Americans and those remaining in bondage. Race, which had long been used to legalize social inequality, would emerge as a justification to perpetuate slavery; Life, Liberty, and “Property.”

### The World, Turned
The World, Turned: The Revolution was not won by just a few founding fathers. Victory came from people whose names rarely reach the history books: Stamp Act crowds in the streets, and Daughters of Liberty spinning homespun and policing the Townshend boycotts. Yet the Revolution left most of the republic’s inequalities standing, and for Native Americans it built new ones, enforced by new wars. In time, though, the Declaration’s promise that “all men are created equal” outgrew the men who wrote it, and later generations turned its words against the very inequalities the Revolution had spared.

## Then and Now

1. I ask my students to guess the war. One side was called the Rebels. Most of the fighting happened on their ground. They were outgunned, outmanned, and short of nearly everything, but they had the better generals, and their commander came from Virginia. They did not need to conquer their enemy, only to outlast him in a war of attrition. That describes the Revolution, and it describes the Confederacy. The same strategy won one war and lost the other. Nearly two centuries after Yorktown, Henry Kissinger put the arithmetic in a single line: “the guerrilla wins if he does not lose. The conventional army loses if it does not win.” In Vietnam and again in Afghanistan, the side that won the battles went home first.
2. Test it. Why did not losing win in 1781 and fail in 1865? Name what the Patriots had that the Confederacy never got. Or bring your own: find a war where the stronger side won the battles and lost the war, and say what the weaker side had to survive.
