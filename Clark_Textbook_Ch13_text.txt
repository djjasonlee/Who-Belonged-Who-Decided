CHAPTER 13
The Market Revolution
Money, Machines, and the Remaking of American Work, 1800–1840
“Business is the very soul of an American: he pursues it, not as a means of procuring for himself and his family the necessary comforts of life, but as the fountain of all human felicity.”
— Francis J. Grund, 1837
### The Question
How is it that in less than two centuries, the United States of America became a global superpower? How in such a short period of time did thirteen colonies, self-seeking and quarrelsome, come to possess both the world’s most productive economy and its most powerful military? I ask my students that question at the start of every year, and the first answers come easily. Freedom and democracy — but Switzerland was a republic of free citizens, and it never became a great power. Land and resources — but Brazil is larger than the forty-eight contiguous states. Capitalism — but the Dutch all but invented modern capitalism, led the world economy in the 1600s, and then faded. Then there is Argentina, with vast fertile plains, millions of European immigrants, and by 1913 one of the ten highest incomes per person in the world. Every one of those answers holds a piece of the truth, and not one of them carries the whole load. No single factor explains it. Democracy, personal liberty, capitalism, and immigration had to run together, the way a good offense needs every position doing its job, and that combination made the story uniquely American. A French visitor to the young republic reached for the same word the question invites. “The position of the Americans is therefore quite exceptional,” Alexis de Tocqueville wrote in 1840, “and it may be believed that no democratic people will ever be placed in a similar one.” He meant unlike any other, not better than every other, and this book uses the word in his sense. The combination began to work in the decades covered by this chapter, and the Market Revolution is where its first results appear. It also redrew the line between autonomy and dependency: who controlled their own work and who sold their labor for wages, who grew wealthy from cotton and who toiled in the fields. Employers wrote the contracts, courts defined the boundaries of economic freedom, and the market determined who prospered.
As the Colonial Period came to a close, the United States underwent an economic upheaval historians have named the Market Revolution. Beginning in the early nineteenth century, several innovations in transportation and communication, as well as a few key innovations in production, transformed the nation’s economy. Prior to this seismic change, farmers and artisans produced just enough to sustain themselves and for a small profit. In 1820, more than seven in ten American workers farmed, most of them to feed their own families. Farming was largely contained to small areas, and manufacturing continued to be done by hand. Families engaged in agriculture divided their labors among traditional gender roles, with the men doing most of the planting and harvesting, while the women tended to the livestock, produced many of their own supplies, and maintained the home. The transportation of goods depended almost entirely upon merchant ships that traveled along the Atlantic coast, flatboats that navigated the major rivers, and wagons that traversed rudimentary roads. Travel remained slow and difficult, as stagecoaches made long journeys on worn, dirt paths. Trade with Europe, mainly Britain, had greatly expanded since the Revolution, which provided access to more manufactured goods and other luxury items. But as more Americans settled west of the Appalachian Mountains, they became more isolated and removed from the larger markets. With little access to currency, most Americans living in rural areas utilized the barter system, exchanging goods and manufactured products as payment. As I tell my students: when you farm to feed your family, that is your job; there is nothing else to buy and no money to buy it with. When you work for a wage, you have cash, and cash can be spent on anything. A business class appeared — Americans who sold things to other Americans for a living — and with it, capitalism in action.
### The Independent Producer
The early republic believed in a connection between liberty and economic independence, and Jefferson believed in it most of all. “Those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God, if ever he had a chosen people,” he wrote; “Dependance begets subservience and venality, suffocates the germ of virtue, and prepares fit tools for the designs of ambition.” Ideally, an individual could support himself, control his labor, and avoid subordination to another person’s will — a principle Jefferson never applied to the more than six hundred people he enslaved over his lifetime. The independent farmer who owned land and controlled its production possessed a degree of autonomy: he could make decisions about his work, provide for his household, and participate in public life without depending on a wage or an employer. Not all could participate. Many farmers were indebted or tenant farmers; women performed essential labor while facing legal and economic constraints; and enslaved people were denied control over their labor and their lives. Nevertheless, the ideal shaped how Americans debated the relationship between property, citizenship, and freedom. The important belief was that controlling the means of making a living helped make a person independent. The Market Revolution complicated that assumption.
The Market Revolution changed how Americans traveled, communicated, and did business. So what? It changed who controlled the means of making a living, and with it the relationships among people, places, labor, capital, and power. The farmer shipping grain east, the farm daughter drawing a wage at Lowell, and the merchant borrowing to expand all held choices their parents had never had. But economic opportunity did not guarantee economic independence. As we will see, the same revolution stitched North and South into one economy while turning them into strangers, and the next three chapters, on the Age of Jackson, the Peculiar Institution, and the American Reformation, each answer, in part, what it set loose.
## Part I · The Arteries
### The Credit System
Towards the end of the eighteenth century, the rural subsistence farming economy gave way to a burgeoning, nationwide network of industry and commerce. Mechanization and new forms of transportation expanded consumer bases and connected distant regions of the country. Furthermore, the emergence of state and local banks increased the money supply to prospective farmers and entrepreneurs. During the Colonial Era, no colony chartered a bank, and British gold and silver rarely made its way into the colonies, which forced the colonists to exchange goods or services instead of paper money. In 1791, following the War for Independence, Federalists in Congress chartered the Bank of the United States, Hamilton’s bank, whose creation the New Republic chapter follows. By 1805, the bank had established branches in eight cities. The national bank generated handsome profits for investors as well as provided easy access to capital. The states chartered banks of their own, and by 1816, when Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States, they numbered more than two hundred, owned by tens of thousands of shareholders. Many of these state banks printed more paper money than their gold and silver could cover, and they lent it freely to farmers who paid too much for land. Such bad banking policies helped bring on the Panic of 1819 (similar to the financial crisis of 2008). In the crash, Americans met the business cycle for the first time: an unregulated market economy rises, collapses, and rises again. The Second Bank’s own story, and the war a president would wage on it, belong to the Age of Jackson.
### Roads and Rivers
Economic expansion also depended upon improvements in transportation. As with bank charters, state governments issued contracts for the construction of new roads and waterways. In addition to new roads, farmers, artisans, and merchants used natural waterways, specifically the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers, to access the larger markets along the Atlantic seaboard. In 1807, Robert Fulton piloted the first commercially successful American steamboat, the Clermont, up the Hudson River. Steamboats proved to be much cheaper and more efficient methods of transportation than the cumbersome flatboats. Steamboats significantly increased the flow of people and goods from the East Coast to the Ohio Valley by reducing both cost and travel time. The drastic increase in traffic along America’s waterways prompted land speculators to purchase property along the banks of the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers. Cincinnati, Louisville, and St. Louis quickly became riverboat hubs for exporting goods.
### The National Road
In the early republic, the federal government paid for its first road and called it the National Road. Authorized in 1806 and begun at Cumberland, Maryland, the road pushed west across the mountains toward the Ohio Valley and beyond. It was the clearest early statement that the new nation intended to use public power to overcome the geography that had kept so much of the interior isolated from the markets of the East.
What the National Road changed was the cost of distance. Western farmers could reach eastern markets more easily. Eastern merchants could reach western customers. Settlers could travel west with families and possessions instead of abandoning everything that could not be carried on a packhorse or flatboat. Towns along the route could attract businesses, inns, workshops, and the commercial traffic that followed the road. Political information, newspapers, mail, and ideas could circulate across a wider geographic area. In short, the road reduced one of the costs of geographic separation. That changed the economic possibilities of every place it touched.
More than that, the road redistributed opportunity across geography. A road, a canal, or a railroad can raise the value of land simply by making it reachable. It can attract investment, create commercial hubs, and turn a crossroads into a market town. It can also change the relative fortunes of towns and regions by redirecting the movement of people and goods. Places on the road rose; places left off it lagged. The distribution of economic opportunity is therefore partly a consequence of decisions about the distribution of infrastructure. The National Road was one of those decisions, made in Washington and paid for with money from federal land sales in Ohio.
At the same time, the road expanded opportunity for settlers while advancing westward dispossession. Every improvement that made it easier for American families to move west made it harder for Native nations to hold the land those families claimed. The same federal power that built the road also enforced the treaties, removals, and military presence that cleared the path. Opportunity and dispossession traveled together.
Finally, the National Road reveals that economic integration is not the same as national unity. Its name expressed a national ambition. Better transportation would connect eastern and western communities, strengthen commerce, and bind the country together. In that sense the road succeeded. Goods, people, and information moved more freely. Yet the very connections it created also changed who could prosper, where power and capital accumulated, and what Americans expected government to do. The federal government helped create economic opportunity by connecting the country. That connection, once made, raised new questions about who the connection served and who it left behind. Integration strengthened the market. It did not automatically produce a single people with a single set of interests. The economic integration paradox is this: the same improvements that bound the country into a single market also made its sections more different from one another, redistributed power unevenly, and created new political expectations and conflicts. As we will see, that paradox ran down every road, canal, and rail line of the Market Revolution.
### The Erie Canal
Even a good gravel road moved freight slowly and at great cost, and as farms and shops turned out more than local buyers could use, Americans needed a cheaper way to reach distant customers. They found it in water. State governments and private investors paid for canals on a scale, and at a cost, the country had never attempted. In 1817, New York passed legislation to fund a 363-mile man-made waterway connecting the Hudson River and Lake Erie, and construction began on July 4 of that year. Prior to the Erie Canal, America’s longest canal ran just twenty-seven miles, a reflection of the immense cost and lack of engineering expertise. Irish immigrants made up much of the workforce, and they moved earth by the millions of cubic yards, cut rock by the thousands of tons, and built the reservoirs that kept the channel full. The first significant engineering project of the United States, the Erie Canal transformed the ecology and economy of an entire region when it opened in 1825. Farms, shops, and cities sprouted up all along the canal, creating instantaneous financial success. Furthermore, shipping goods via the Erie Canal saved merchants and farmers roughly 90 percent. Copying New York’s achievement, other states invested in canal companies. The success of the Erie Canal even attracted British and Dutch investors to supplement the construction of other man-made waterways. Before the canal, grain had an expiration date, set by distance rather than spoilage: hauled far enough by wagon, the freight cost more than the crop could fetch. The crucial change was not simply that a shipment arrived sooner. A larger range of economic activities became profitable that previously had not been. The Market Revolution therefore expanded the sources of wealth while changing the assets and relationships through which wealth was accumulated. The United States gained productive capacity and new avenues for economic advancement. But the benefits depended increasingly on access to land, machinery, credit, transportation, and markets — resources that were distributed unequally.
### The Telegraph
At the turn of the nineteenth century, postal carriers on horseback remained the quickest form of communication across long distances. But for decades, inventors familiar with the properties of electricity imagined a faster, electronic communication system. European scientists experimented with various methods of using electrical impulses to send messages, but they struggled to create a representation of the alphabet. In 1837 and 1838, Samuel Morse devised a code for transmitting letters and numbers along a single wire, using a contact key. In 1843, Congress paid for a telegraph line between Washington, D.C., and Baltimore, and on May 24, 1844, Morse tapped out its first official message: “What hath God wrought.” A year later, investors formed the Magnetic Telegraph Company to string the country’s first network of wires. By 1856, Western Union had taken its name. And in October 1861, the first transcontinental telegraph line was completed, connecting San Francisco with the East. With access to the telegraph, farmers and merchants could expand their consumer base and conduct business transactions across long distances. In addition, electronic communication helped those new to the trans-Appalachian West communicate with loved ones, helped immigrants locate family, and revolutionized the communication of news, culture, and politics.
### Railways
In the 1850s, another innovation in transportation, railroads, joined canals and highways as vital components of the Market Revolution. Investors in Boston, New York, and London won charters from the states and put heavy money into new track, and by 1860 the railroads carried more freight overland from the Midwest to the Northeast than any other carrier, though most of the wheat still went by lake and canal. As the railways moved more goods between Northern producers and their markets, new lines also tied Southern cotton plantations to the textile mills of the Northeast. Cotton made white Southerners rich, but the trade with the North did little to build industry in the South. The South had few cities, few factories, and few skilled workers, and so it stayed a farming region, while the trade in wheat, corn, and livestock gave the Northeast and Midwest more varied economies. In essence, the rails bound northern industry and southern agriculture closer together than ever.
### The Market Revolution and the Supreme Court
The National Road solved a physical problem: how to connect distant places. Two Supreme Court decisions solved a related one — what legal rules would govern economic activity across those connections, and how much freedom private enterprise would have to operate within them. Roads, canals, steamboats, and factories could expand opportunity. They could not, by themselves, create a national market. Businesses also needed predictable rules about interstate commerce, corporate authority, contracts, and the limits of state power. Without those rules, every state line risked becoming a barrier, and every successful enterprise risked becoming a target for local politics.
A pattern runs through the Market Revolution, and it holds here. A bottleneck caps what a system can produce; an invention loosens the bottleneck; output climbs; and the gains flow to whoever stands in position to catch them. The new market soon hit two bottlenecks no machine could break, because the law had built both of them. The first: the state line. The second: the legislature.
### The State Line
Robert Fulton and Robert Livingston had secured from New York a monopoly over steamboat navigation in the state’s waters — the monopoly Fulton’s success had won him. Their arrangement gave them substantial control over an emerging transportation technology at the very moment steamboats were transforming the rivers. Thomas Gibbons, operating under a federal coastal-trade license, ran steamboats between New Jersey and New York. Aaron Ogden, who held rights under the New York monopoly, challenged Gibbons’s right to compete. The two men had once run a steam ferry together, and the case that settled the constitutional law of interstate commerce began as a feud between former partners who had come to despise each other. On the water, Gibbons’s boat ran under a young Cornelius Vanderbilt, who kept making the run in open defiance of New York’s injunctions and slipped past every officer sent to arrest him.
The dispute reached the Supreme Court. Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that interstate navigation fell within Congress’s constitutional power to regulate commerce among the states. Because New York’s monopoly conflicted with valid federal law governing coastal trade, the state restriction could not stand. Marshall’s decision strengthened the principle that interstate commerce could not be governed by conflicting state restrictions when those restrictions collided with federal law.
The decision reached well beyond steamboats. Competition gained a constitutional foothold: a state-granted monopoly could not automatically exclude a federally licensed operator from interstate navigation. Market integration advanced: commercial activity that crossed state lines was subject to federal authority rather than being controlled entirely by individual states. Businesses operating across those lines gained a stronger basis for challenging certain barriers. And the federal government acquired clearer authority to establish rules for an economy that was increasingly national in scale. The ruling did not abolish monopolies, guarantee fair competition, or hand the federal government unlimited power over every local business. It established a crucial principle about interstate commerce and federal supremacy at the exact moment the Market Revolution was knitting the country together commercially.
### The Legislature
Dartmouth College had operated since 1769 under a charter granted in the name of King George III. The quarrel that produced the case was local and personal before it was constitutional: the trustees forced out the college’s president, John Wheelock, son of its founder, and New Hampshire’s legislature took Wheelock’s side. In 1816 it moved to take the college over, renaming the school Dartmouth University, enlarging its board, and putting it under state-appointed overseers. The college’s own secretary and treasurer, William H. Woodward, sided with the state and kept the records, which is why the case carries his name. The old trustees went to court, and in 1819 the Supreme Court sided with them. Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the charter was a contract, and the Constitution forbids a state to impair the obligation of contracts. New Hampshire could not rewrite the college’s charter on its own. A republic that had fought a war against the Crown had just protected a royal grant against its own elected legislature.
Daniel Webster argued for the college, and finished, as one listener remembered it, by telling the Court: “It is, Sir, as I have said, a small college. And yet there are those who love it.” The case was about a small college; its consequences were about everything else.
The Market Revolution depended increasingly on organizations that could hold property, enter contracts, raise capital, and keep operating beyond the lives of their founders. A corporation could pool resources and organize work on a scale no one person could sustain. But a canal company or a textile mill needed what Dartmouth’s trustees had wanted: confidence that the next legislature would not rewrite the rules after the money was spent. Dartmouth gave them that. Private corporations gained security against a state rewriting their charters, and investors gained confidence that long-term commitments would hold. The ruling had limits — it did not forbid states to regulate business, and it did not make every business arrangement immune from government action — but the decision became an important precedent in the development of American corporate law.
### The Paradox of Progress
Where Gibbons opened the routes of commerce, Dartmouth protected the organizations that would travel them. Neither decision dug a canal or invented a machine. Their contribution was institutional: the rules a market needs before investors will commit to it. But a rule that protects is a rule that protects somebody in particular. The same shield that kept a legislature from rewriting an investor’s charter could keep a competitor from ever being granted one, and the same principle that opened a river to a federally licensed newcomer left untouched the advantages of everyone already on it. Protection for the established is exclusion for the newcomer. A state could use its authority to shelter an existing interest from competition, which was the problem Gibbons exposed; a court could do the same thing by enforcing a charter, which was the problem Dartmouth created. Justice Joseph Story saw it from the bench and wrote the way around it into his concurrence: a state could reserve, in the charters it granted thereafter, the right to amend them. States soon did.
### Charles River Bridge
The problem Dartmouth created soon reached the Supreme Court from the opposite direction. In 1785, Massachusetts granted a group of investors a charter to build a toll bridge connecting Boston and Charlestown. The charter eventually gave the proprietors seventy years to collect tolls. Four decades later, the legislature authorized a second bridge, the Warren Bridge, just a few hundred feet away. Its investors could collect tolls while recovering the cost of construction; afterward, the bridge would become free. The proprietors of the Charles River Bridge sued. Their argument followed Dartmouth: a charter was a contract, and the state could not destroy the value of the rights it had granted. A competing bridge, they argued, would do precisely that.
By 1837, John Marshall had left the Court and Roger B. Taney occupied his seat. In a 4–3 decision, the Court ruled against the original proprietors. A charter granted only the privileges it expressly contained; it did not confer a monopoly simply because competition might diminish the value of the investment. The original charter had promised the right to operate a toll bridge, not the exclusive right to cross the river. Chief Justice Taney framed the choice in terms of the public interest: “the object and end of all government is to promote the happiness and prosperity of the community.” Justice Story dissented, but the new bridge stood. Once the Warren Bridge became free, the old bridge could no longer compete for customers, and its toll-collecting business soon collapsed.
Dartmouth had protected a charter from the legislature. Charles River Bridge refused to turn that protection into a guarantee against competition. The distinction mattered. A state could honor its contracts without surrendering its authority to serve the public, and an investor could rely on the rights actually granted without claiming rights the charter never promised. In this case, the established interest lost and the newcomer won. The Court had reopened the question Dartmouth left behind: whether the law existed to preserve the advantages of those already in business or to leave room for those who wanted to enter. The decision still left the legislature in control of who received a charter. It limited the privileges an existing charter could imply; it did not eliminate the political gatekeeping involved in granting one. Charles River Bridge made competition legally possible. General incorporation would make entry less dependent on legislative permission.
### General Incorporation
General incorporation marked the shift from favor to form. For most of the early republic a corporate charter was a privilege, granted one enterprise at a time by special act of the legislature. The process invited lobbying, favoritism, and the suspicion that incorporation was a political gift rather than a neutral legal status. Dartmouth College had protected existing charters against unilateral state alteration; it had not made new ones easy to obtain.
States gradually changed the rules. New York moved first, on March 22, 1811, with an act that let any five or more men form a company to manufacture textiles or work metal without asking the legislature at all — capital capped at $100,000, a corporate life of twenty years. Instead of requiring a separate statute for each company, states enacted general incorporation laws under which any group meeting the statutory requirements could form a corporation as a matter of right. The corporate form ceased to be a special favor and became a standardized legal instrument. Capital could be pooled, liability limited, and enterprises continued beyond the lives of their founders with far less dependence on legislative grace.
The change did not eliminate political influence or public regulation. Justice Story’s concurrence in the Dartmouth case had already noted the state’s reserved power to amend. General incorporation simply altered the baseline. What had once been dispensed as privilege was now available to anyone who filed the papers. And here, as everywhere in this chapter, the paradox held: the reform that opened the corporation to ordinary business also gave firms the power to pool capital and limit risk. The companies that grew from it would dwarf anything a legislature had ever chartered by name. The opportunity was open to all. The outcomes were not.
Neither Dartmouth College nor Gibbons guaranteed that an ordinary merchant would succeed. Neither eliminated unequal access to capital or economic power. But both helped shape the rules within which an expanding commercial economy could operate. The Market Revolution was not simply a transformation in how Americans produced and moved goods. It was also a transformation in the relationship between government, private enterprise, and the national market. The question worth debating is the same one that runs through the entire chapter: did these decisions expand economic freedom, or did they strengthen the institutions and commercial interests best positioned to exercise economic power?
## Part II · The Machines
### The Reaper and the Plow
The story of agricultural mechanization is not simply about a new way to harvest grain or a better way to plow. It is about how technology changed the relationship between land, labor, and capital. Every system of production has a bottleneck, the one step that limits how much the whole can turn out, and American grain farming had two. The first was the harvest. Grain has a narrow window for reaping, and weather, insects, and time can all disrupt a process that was traditionally slow and done by hand, with scythes and sickles; the bottleneck was the speed at which a harvest could be brought in. Cyrus McCormick’s reaper, first demonstrated in 1831 and patented in 1834, helped solve that problem by rapidly speeding the reaping process. The second bottleneck was particular to the Midwest, where the soil was often sticky and dense, and farmers had to stop again and again to scrape off their plows. John Deere’s steel plow of 1837 used polished steel that let the soil slide easily off the blade. It was not just a better tool; it was an improvement in the way farmers could extend the range and size of their farms. The reaper increased the speed of harvesting, and the steel plow improved the efficiency of preparing the soil. Together they helped reduce two of the largest bottlenecks in agriculture.
Higher productivity did not guarantee higher profits. If farmers increased production simultaneously, grain prices could fall. Farmers might also incur debt to purchase equipment or acquire land. Profitability depended on the relationship between production, selling prices, operating costs, financing, and access to markets. This is one reason agricultural mechanization was inseparable from the transportation revolution: a farmer who could harvest twice as much grain but could not move it to market might realize little economic benefit. And like the cotton gin, which the Peculiar Institution chapter follows, the new machines cut the labor a single task required while raising the demand that called for more labor overall.
Agricultural mechanization demonstrates a recurring pattern in economic history. A constraint limits production. An innovation reduces that constraint. Production expands, and land that was previously impractical to farm becomes profitable. New markets develop and supporting industries are created. Competition, land policy, and investment determine who captures the gains. And unintended consequences emerge, including economic displacement and environmental damage. The reaper reduced the labor bottleneck in harvesting. The steel plow reduced the difficulty of cultivating heavy prairie soils. Transportation networks helped farmers sell their additional output. Federal land policies and westward expansion made more territory available to settlers, often resulting in the dispossession of Native Americans. The key point is not that two machines single-handedly revolutionized American agriculture. It is that they helped unleash a larger transformation in which technology, markets, capital, geography, and government policy reinforced one another.
### The American Industrial Revolution
For centuries, spinning and weaving, whether wool, cotton, linen, or silk, were duties performed in the home, using primitive methods that were slow to evolve. Steam- and water-powered machines revolutionized textile production by replacing the tedious work of hand-spinning thread and yarn into usable materials. Britain guarded its lead: Parliament made it a crime to send the machines abroad, and a crime for the men who built them to leave. But the promise of opportunity and higher wages drew thousands of skilled machinists across the Atlantic anyway. In 1789, Samuel Slater, who had apprenticed to Jedediah Strutt, partner of the chief inventor of British spinning machinery, came to America and reproduced British innovations from memory, a story the Peculiar Institution chapter tells. Falling water supplied the power. Rivers running hard out of the Appalachian foothills turned the machines, and textile mills rose along their banks from Massachusetts to Delaware. Even with British technology in hand, American mills could not match British prices. British merchants commanded a shipping network that bought raw cotton cheaply, and British cities held thousands of workers with no land and no choice but to take low pay. Congress tried to close the gap with protective tariffs in 1816, 1824, and 1828, the last of them the Tariff of Abominations. American industrialists and merchants found two additional ways to compete with British textile manufacturers. First, American producers made several improvements to British textile machinery. The American businessman Francis Cabot Lowell, while touring textile mills in England in 1810–12, reportedly committed the workings of the British power loom to memory. Back home, the mechanic Paul Moody built and improved the machinery, and Lowell’s Boston Manufacturing Company, at Waltham, built more powerful looms, which operated at higher speeds and required less labor, and put spinning and weaving under one roof for the first time. The second strategy was to employ cheaper labor.
### Interchangeable Parts
Interchangeable parts made interchangeable workers. The idea is usually credited to Eli Whitney. In January 1801, before President Adams and President-elect Jefferson, Whitney demonstrated the principle with muskets whose parts, he claimed, could be freely substituted for one another. The performance was impressive. Later scholarship has been less so. Merritt Roe Smith concluded that Whitney almost certainly staged the demonstration with specimens specially prepared for the occasion. The real advances came from others who did the sustained work Whitney took credit for: Simeon North on federal pistol contracts at Staddle Hill in Middletown, Connecticut; John H. Hall at Harpers Ferry; and Roswell Lee at Springfield. In 1827 three armorers appointed by the Ordnance Department took a hundred of Hall’s rifles, made three years before, stripped them, mixed the parts, and rebuilt them on new stocks. They could find no part that failed to fit: “By no other process known to us,” they reported, “could arms be made so exactly alike as to interchange and require no marks on the different parts.”
What mattered was not the legend but the logic. Once parts could be made to a standard, the skilled artisan who filed and fitted each piece by hand lost his advantage. The work could be broken into narrower tasks and performed by less skilled hands. The machine that produced interchangeable parts also produced a more interchangeable labor force. The same principle that standardized the product standardized the producer.
### Clock Time
The factory changed more than how Americans made things; it changed how they measured their days. Farm life still ran on daylight and the seasons, and the colonial artisan had worked in bursts, breaking for a drink at the tavern or an argument about politics when the work allowed. In the factory, the workshop, and even the kitchen of a household with servants, work began and ended at fixed hours, and the clock, not the task, set the pace. The artisan’s pay had been his price, tied to the goods he made; the factory hand’s pay was a wage, set by the hour or the day. The railroads, running on timetables, taught the rest of the country the same discipline. To many Americans, tending a machine for a span of hours set by someone else’s clock looked like the opposite of the independence that freedom was supposed to mean — and so, at first, few native-born men would take the work.
### The Lowell Girls
The mill owners turned instead to the people with the fewest other ways to earn money, and found them on New England’s farms. In the 1820s the Boston Manufacturing Company began recruiting young, single women from farm families, and after Lowell’s death in 1817 his associates built the nation’s first planned factory town and named it for him: Lowell, Massachusetts. Under the “Lowell System,” which replaced the family-run “Rhode Island System” of the Slater mills, raw cotton became finished cloth inside a single mill, and the workers lived in company boardinghouses under close watch. Parents worried about sending their daughters away, so the owners set curfews, banned liquor, made churchgoing mandatory, and offered evening lectures and other cultural activities. By 1840 Lowell’s mills employed more than eight thousand workers, most of them young women, some as young as ten, usually hired on one-year contracts. For the women, the bargain was real. A girl who had risen before dawn to milk the cows on her father’s farm now drew a wage, more than housework had ever paid, and with it a little bit of independence. The “Lowell girls,” as the country came to call them, sent their pay home to clear their fathers’ mortgages, paid for their brothers’ and sisters’ schooling, and saved for their own dowries, and many discovered that earning their own money changed what they believed a woman could be. Money does not buy happiness. It buys choices, and for a farm daughter in the 1830s, choices meant freedom. The Lowell girls traded a life that looked settled from birth for a wage, and they made the trade willingly, though it cost them twelve hours or more a day, six days a week. They judged it the better bargain. Harriet Hanson Robinson, who went into the mills as a child of ten, remembered what the wage meant to the women around her: “they could earn money, and spend it as they pleased; and could gratify their tastes and desires without restraint, and without rendering an account to anybody.” The women’s movement that a later chapter follows to Seneca Falls had one of its roots here.
### Two Kinds of Freedom
Wage labor created a new relationship between freedom and dependence. Compare a farmer who owns his land with a factory worker who earns a wage. The farmer may have considerable control over how the work is organized, although weather, debt, prices, and markets can constrain his choices. The factory worker, by contrast, generally labors under an employer who controls the workplace and sets the conditions of employment and the wages. The worker is legally free to leave the job in a way that an enslaved person is not. That distinction is fundamental and should never be erased. Yet the worker may have limited practical freedom if leaving means losing the income needed to pay rent and buy food. The result is a distinction between two kinds of freedom. Legal freedom asks what choices the law permits a person to make; economic independence asks how much practical control a person possesses over the resources and conditions necessary to live. The two can overlap, but they are not identical. A wage worker might have more purchasing power than a subsistence farmer, and access to goods that were previously unavailable. Yet the worker might also have less control over the pace of work, the organization of the working day, and the immediate conditions of production.
### The Price of the Mill
The independence came at a price, and it rose. A Lowell workday ran from about five in the morning to seven at night, six days a week, in rooms kept shut against the breezes that would disturb the fibers, sweltering in summer and freezing in winter, lit after dark by smoking oil lamps. The noise was deafening and the air thick with lint. Cotton bales and machine oil made fire a constant danger; a hand or an arm caught in the machinery could be crushed or torn away, and a worker who survived an injury usually lost her job, and her income, along with it. Over the decades conditions grew worse rather than better. Owners assigned each worker more machines and ran them faster, cut wages, and moved many hands from hourly pay to piecework, paid by the yard rather than the hour.
### The Labor Question
The men who did enter the shops fought back. After the Revolution, artisans and craftsmen had embraced an ideology of independent production — small producers, equal to one another, free because self-sufficient. The factory system turned many of them into wage earners under a supervisor’s eye, and unlike the young women who welcomed the mill as an escape from parental control and domestic service, the men bridled at it. To assert their independence they refused the old terms master and servant and borrowed a Dutch word for their employer: boss. Skilled workers, carpenters and stonemasons among them, used their leverage to form unions and bargain for pay and hours, and ran straight into the law: under English and American common law, unions were illegal “combinations,” disrupting a master’s authority over his servants, and most courts treated strikers as mobs. Labor’s defenders answered that the great capitalists regarded their employees much as southern planters regarded their slaves. The fight over the ten-hour day was a fight over what freedom meant. By the mid-1840s the Lowell mills averaged twelve hours and ten minutes a day. In rooms lit by scores of lamps — 293 small and 61 large in one recorded case — operatives worked from before dawn until after dark. Lowell workers, Sarah Bagley among the most outspoken, petitioned the Massachusetts legislature for a law that ten hours “shall constitute a day’s work.” The committee that answered them in 1845, chaired by William Schouler, did not deny the long hours or the hardship. It simply refused to act. “Labor is intelligent enough to make its own bargains, and look out for its own interests without any interference from us,” the report declared. The committee even granted the abuses — too many hours, too little air — before concluding: “We acknowledge all this, but we say, the remedy is not with us.” Under free education and free constitutions, the committee insisted, labor stood on an equality with capital and in some sense controlled it. A Massachusetts-only statute, it warned, would “close the gate of every mill in the State” while neighboring states remained unrestricted. New Hampshire enacted a ten-hour law in 1847. The larger pattern remained. The workers’ demand for a legal limit on the working day exposed a deeper disagreement about freedom itself: whether freedom meant the right to bargain individually, even under unequal conditions, or the right to collective protection against those conditions. The legislature chose the first definition and left the second unanswered. In 1842 the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts, in Commonwealth v. Hunt, overturned the old precedent and upheld the right of workers to organize and to strike. The workers had their own name for the condition they were resisting — “wage slavery.” Within a generation the language of slavery would become the common tongue of every American reform.
## Part III · One Economy, Two Societies
### The Cotton Complex
In 1800, the economy of the United States remained predominantly local and agrarian, with manufacturing still in its infancy. The Market Revolution and westward expansion reached the North and the South at the same time, yet the two sections built drastically different economies, and the difference deepened the divide between them. The rise of the South’s Cotton Kingdom created both economic and social divisions between the North and South. As northern mills adopted water-powered spinning and weaving machinery, their appetite for southern cotton grew. The bottleneck lay in the seeds. Short-staple cotton held its seeds so tightly that cleaning it by hand took hours of labor, and until 1793 that labor kept cotton a small crop. In that year Eli Whitney, a Yale graduate staying as a guest on a Georgia plantation, built a simple machine of rollers and brushes that pulled the fiber away from the seed, and with it planters could grow cotton for the world market. In Virginia’s tobacco country, slavery had been paying planters less and less; cotton, planted farther south and west, reversed the trend, and planters spent their new profits on more enslaved people. The cotton then traveled north, to the mills and to the merchants and bankers who shipped it overseas, and northern insurers and exporters grew rich on the trade. What that partnership cost, and who paid for it, is the subject of the Peculiar Institution chapter. That partnership challenges the simple story in which American innovation and economic development replaced an older, less advanced system. Industrialization and slavery could expand together. Greater productive capacity did not automatically produce greater human freedom.
### A New America
By the middle of the nineteenth century the United States had experienced monumental changes to its primary institutions, shifting away from the economic and social norms of the Colonial Period. In essence, Thomas Jefferson’s agrarian vision for America was giving way to his greatest fears. New innovations in transportation, communication, agriculture, and business connected the nation in unprecedented ways. These advancements in the production and facilitation of goods had a profound effect, not just upon commerce but upon society at large. The building of new highways and canals opened up new markets for rural American farms and businesses, which led to more production and lower prices. With access to more consumers and credit, American farmers began shifting away from subsistence farming and towards commercial farming. In addition, the development of factories and the mechanization of production led to an increased demand for labor, providing new opportunities for men and women to earn a living. This new division of labor resulted in less specialization and artisan work, as well as a new independence away from family-centered farms and businesses, as thousands of Americans relocated to urban areas. Consequently, massive fortunes were amassed, a middle class emerged, and more men and women could pursue economic independence and new social freedoms. As American cities rapidly grew in population and production, American society began experiencing new family dynamics, gender roles, and attitudes towards capitalism. Ultimately, the rapid congregation of Americans into urban areas inspired new social dynamics, as people from diverse backgrounds, including European immigrants, were forced to coexist in a new America.
In that sense, the country grew more connected. Yet integration did not produce unity. It produced differentiation and tension.
• Uneven development. The same arteries that carried cotton north and manufactures south strengthened two increasingly distinct regional economies — one industrializing and commercially diversified, the other intensifying its reliance on slave-grown cash crops. The cotton gin tightened the South’s grip on slave labor, and the Cotton Kingdom grew into a regional power in its own right, economic and political, fighting northern merchants and manufacturers over tariffs and the price of foreign goods. Connection made the sections economically interdependent and culturally more aware of their differences at the same time.
• Redistribution of opportunity and power. Capital, population, and political influence concentrated along the new routes. Places and people with access to the market prospered; those without it did not. Geographic integration therefore created new hierarchies of place.
• Changed expectations of government. Once the federal government had demonstrated that it could, and would, spend public money to overcome distance, Americans began to argue over how far that power should reach, who should pay, and who should benefit. The National Road itself became both a symbol of national ambition and a source of constitutional and sectional dispute.
• Liberty and its limits. Economic integration expanded commercial freedom for many white Americans while tightening the grip of slavery in the Cotton Kingdom and accelerating Native dispossession. Although the North had washed its hands of slavery, its factories fed on slave-grown cotton and its banks financed the plantations that grew it. The market that promised independence to the farmer or the wage earner also bound the nation more tightly to an institution that denied independence to millions.
Each step forward in the economy dragged the country somewhere new, and by the end the Market Revolution had left the United States bound, paradoxically, to economic liberty and to slavery at once. Economic growth was neither universally beneficial nor inherently harmful; greater productive capacity and greater human freedom produced a variety of historical outcomes, and one did not guarantee the other. The United States could become more innovative, productive, and economically powerful while maintaining slavery, exploiting labor, and dispossessing Native peoples, and while creating new class structures within society. In short, the paradox is that knitting the country together commercially did not automatically produce a single political community or a shared set of interests. Integration lowered the barriers of space; it raised the stakes of the differences that remained. The National Road, like the Erie Canal and the early railroads, made the market larger and more efficient. It did not make Americans one people with one destiny. That was the tension the Market Revolution left for the Age of Jackson, the debates over slavery, and ultimately the crisis of the Union.
Economic progress and individual independence were no longer bound together. A country could become wealthier, more productive, and more connected without ensuring that its people did too.

## Then and Now
The Panic of 1819 was similar to the financial crisis of 2008. Both times the mechanism ran in the same three steps. Banks issued paper they could not cover. They lent freely against land whose price everyone involved expected to keep climbing. And when the climbing stopped, the loss landed on the people holding the land rather than on the people who had written the paper. The Panic introduced Americans to the business cycle and taught them, for the first time, that an unregulated market economy does not rise forever, a lesson the country has since had to learn again.
Test it. Does the comparison hold, or do two centuries of banking law and deposit insurance make these two different animals wearing the same coat? Or set it aside and bring your own: name something being bought right now on the assumption that its price cannot fall, and say who will be holding it when the bill comes.
