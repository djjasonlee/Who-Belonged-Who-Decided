CHAPTER 16
The Peculiar Institution
The Cotton Kingdom and the Making of a People, 1793–1860
“Where yesterday the wilderness darkened over the land with her wild forests, today the cotton plantations whitened the earth.”
— William Henry Sparks
The opening chapters asked us to see the indigenous inhabitants of this country not as a stage upon which other nations performed, but as actors shaping the historical drama of America. This chapter makes the same request, in perhaps the most difficult case that exists in the American record. Between the invention of a machine in 1793 and the eve of the Civil War, the South built the largest system of forced labor in the Western hemisphere. That story must be told, and Part I tells it quickly. But the argument of the chapter develops later. Inside that machine, under deliberate policies designed to prevent exactly this, nearly four million men, women, and children forged themselves into a people: a common language where the masters had engineered Babel, a kinship architecture built to outlast the auction block, a faith that carried Moses into the fields, and a repertoire of resistance that ran from the broken tool to insurrection. The economics is the setting. The making of a people is the story. In the Cotton Kingdom, the market did the deciding. No legislature had to pass a law directing the movement of cotton or the people who produced it. Supply and demand did that work. The invisible hand set the price of cotton, and the price of cotton set the price of the people who grew it. Planters treated both as commodities, bought and sold by the same calculations of cost, demand, and profit. But they traveled in opposite directions. Cotton moved north and across the Atlantic, feeding the mills that turned raw fiber into cloth. Enslaved people moved south. Nearly a million men, women, and children endured this Second Middle Passage, and many had been raised for no purpose other than sale. The market joined these two movements, making human bondage an essential component of the machinery of industrial growth. Nature had helped determine who would supply the labor. The law had made that labor hereditary. The market decided how profitable the system could be, and profit gave it reason to expand. But neither the market nor the law could govern every part of an enslaved person’s life. Out of the master’s sight and beyond the reach of the overseer’s lash, enslaved people continued to make decisions of their own: who counted as family, what each member owed the others, and how to care for those the auctioneer’s gavel had torn from their own. Beyond family and kinship, enslaved people made decisions that gave their communities structure, continuity, and identity. They chose their own leaders and looked after one another, gathered to worship and to settle disputes, and passed knowledge and news from one plantation to the next. They preserved elements of African cultural and spiritual traditions while adapting them to new circumstances, forging a culture that was neither simply inherited nor imposed. These institutions helped communities survive, but they also gave enslaved people a measure of control over their own lives. They made those decisions under conditions they had never chosen, but the decisions were theirs. The law decided what they were. They decided who they were, and who they would become.
Ironically, the same system that connected communities and cultivated a cultural bond also created environments rife with enmity and division — house and field, lighter and darker, favored and driven — fault lines laid down on purpose, whose argument over who belonged did not end when the institution did.
### Cultural Legacies — A Note on Method
One claim runs under everything that follows, and it earns its place only if it survives a test: cultural legacies are real. What happened to a people generations ago still shows up in how their descendants behave, often without their knowing it. The claim is easiest to test on a population no one is arguing about. The Scots-Irish borderers who settled the Blue Ridge, the western Carolinas, Kentucky, and Tennessee brought with them, on one influential reading, a herdsman’s culture of honor, in which a man’s reputation for answering an insult was his protection. In a study published in 1996, psychologists at the University of Michigan put that inheritance in a hallway. A confederate bumped each young man in a narrow corridor and muttered an insult as he passed; a few minutes later a second confederate, six feet three and 250 pounds, walked straight at him down the same hall. Men raised in the South who had not been insulted stepped aside at about nine feet. Men raised in the South who had just been insulted held their line to about three. Men raised in the North behaved the same either way. Courtesy and the readiness to collide turned out to be one inheritance, and a single insult decided which one showed. The honor-culture reading is contested, and this chapter does not rest on it; the evidence for slavery’s legacies is in the chapters that follow. But if a code carried out of the borderlands could still steer young men in a Michigan hallway two centuries later, the same principle applies to slavery, where it is harder to hear: a culture forged under slavery, still running in the present tense, should be no surprise at all. But a legacy explains; it does not command. Cultural legacies can account for a stereotype, and they can become convenient vehicles for justifying present conditions. They are not predestination orders, and they do not mandate behavior; the people who inherit them still choose.[[1]]
## Part I · The Kingdom of Cotton
### Context, 1800 — The Deathbed
At the turn of the nineteenth century, informed Americans believed slavery was dying, and the evidence was on their side. The Revolution had tormented many Americans with the distance between the ideals they fought for and the human property they held, and upon independence the North moved: Vermont’s 1777 constitution barred adult slavery before Vermont was a state, Pennsylvania passed the first gradual-abolition statute of any state legislature in 1780, and in Massachusetts the institution collapsed in the courts, where an enslaved man named Quock Walker sued for his freedom and the chief justice charged the jury that perpetual servitude could no longer be tolerated under the new constitution.[[2]] Virginia and North Carolina loosened their manumission laws. Pennsylvania Quakers organized abolition societies and campaigned openly against the South’s claim to human property, and in February 1790 their petitions reached the floor of the First Congress above the most famous signature in America after Washington’s — a confrontation told in Chapter 10.[[3]] The economics seemed to agree with the morality: tobacco was fading, the profitability of slavery visibly declining with it, and the Northwest Ordinance had closed the territory north of the Ohio — enough for Jefferson to proclaim the institution’s undeniable demise. The world seemed to agree with both. Britain planted a colony of former slaves at Sierra Leone in 1787; the enslaved of Saint-Domingue rose in 1791 and ended slavery in the richest colony on earth; and Britain, which had already set the Royal Navy — the same navy that had carried the trade — to hunting slave ships on the Atlantic after banning the trade in 1807, would outlaw the institution across its empire in 1833. At home, the American Colonization Society organized to resettle free Black Americans in a West African territory it named Liberia, while evangelical revivalism was spawning the reform energies the previous chapter described. By every visible measure — moral, economic, political, global — slavery in America was on a path toward death. The obituary was written. Instead, with one machine, a fresh set of justifying philosophies, and the constitutional armistice already described in Chapter 10, the institution did not merely survive. It became the engine of the American economy.
### White Gold
The machine came first. In Britain, the spinning jenny and its successors had revolutionized textiles — cloth could now be woven faster than cotton could be cleaned, and the mills were starving. In 1793 a Northern inventor, Eli Whitney, supplied the missing piece: the cotton gin, which stripped the sticky seeds from raw cotton and broke the bottleneck that had kept the crop marginal.[[4]] The cost of production collapsed, the demand exploded, and the South had its next cash crop — “white gold,” nearly as valuable as sugar and far more profitable than tobacco. The numbers tell the scale of what followed. Cotton exports, a modest 150,000 bales in 1815, topped three million by 1859; by 1832, 88 of the 106 American corporations worth more than one hundred thousand dollars made textiles; and the Census Bureau itself would single out cotton manufacturing as the most striking feature of fifty years of American industry.[[5]] And the people who grew the crop were the largest asset of all: by 1860 the market value of the enslaved exceeded the invested value of all the nation’s railroads and factories combined.[[6]] And mark where those mills stood, because they did not stand in the South. The water-powered factories of England and New England ran on Southern cotton; Northern insurers underwrote the crop, Northern financiers moved it, Northern ships carried it. And New England’s mills began with a theft. In 1789 Samuel Slater, a young mill hand trained in Jedediah Strutt’s Arkwright-pattern mills at Belper, in Derbyshire, sailed for New York with the design of the water frame in his head, because Britain forbade the export of the machines and the emigration of the men who knew them; on December 20, 1790, his water-powered spinning began at Pawtucket, Rhode Island. Some of the people of Belper called him Slater the Traitor. The circle then closed on itself: the North bought the South’s cotton, spun and wove it, and sold the finished goods back to a region that manufactured comparatively little of what it consumed. Free-labor North and slave-labor South grew up as partners in a single enterprise — a partnership whose bill comes due in the chapters ahead.[[7]] The gin did not revive a declining institution so much as resurrect it, and send it west — across Alabama and Mississippi, to the river and beyond. The march west had an engine in the ground itself. Cotton planted on the same fields season after season stripped the soil of its nutrients, and with cheap land on the frontier, the planter’s easiest answer was not to restore a worn-out field but to leave it for a new one. The Cotton Kingdom was built to expand or decline — more land, which meant more labor, which meant more slavery — and a later chapter will follow that hunger to Texas and the war with Mexico.[[8]]
### The Second Middle Passage
The Kingdom needed hands, and after January 1, 1808, it could not import them: Congress had closed the Atlantic trade on the first day the Constitution allowed, the final rung of the legislative ladder Chapter 10 climbed.[[9]] The global arithmetic frames everything that follows: of roughly 12.5 million Africans embarked in the Atlantic trade across its whole history, about 388,000 disembarked in mainland North America — a fraction of the hemisphere’s total.[[10]] The nearly four million people enslaved in the South by 1860 were not imported; they were born. Shut the door on a supply and the price of everything already inside goes up: stop importing bananas tomorrow, and every banana in the country is worth more by the weekend. When the Atlantic door closed in 1808, the same arithmetic applied to human beings, and a whole new door opened. And when the cotton frontier demanded labor the Atlantic could no longer supply, the South built a market in its own people. In the Upper South — Virginia and Maryland, where tobacco had faded — enslaved populations exceeded the dying crop’s needs, and planters found that their most profitable export walked on two feet. By 1860, something approaching a million men, women, and children had been moved from the Upper South into the cotton states — much of it through markets of unprecedented American scale, the rest carried along by relocating owners.[[11]] The visible end of the trade ran through the coastal cities — Baltimore, Charleston, New Orleans — where the auctions drew abolitionist eyes and abolitionist pamphlets. Much of it moved overland and out of sight, traveling merchants walking their coffles toward the inland cotton country along roads where nobody printed anything.[[12]] Prices climbed as the supply closed; the fortunes of the old tobacco elite were sustained by selling human beings to the new cotton elite; and the enslaved population, 700,000 at the first census in 1790, approached 1.2 million by 1810 and stood near four million by 1860.[[13]] The courts sold people too, and on a scale few Americans noticed. When an owner died in debt or went bankrupt, the law required his property, human beings included, to be auctioned to the highest bidder, and sheriffs, executors, and trustees conducted the sales. The historian Thomas D. Russell, working through South Carolina’s records, found that courts handled half of all slave sales there, and that the risk of breaking up a family may have run nearly three times higher at a court sale than at a commercial one. No one had to choose the separation. The law chose it. “The law itself not unfrequently performs the most cruel separations of families,” the Southern critic Daniel R. Goodloe wrote, “almost without the intervention of individual agency.” A sale ordered by a court had no seller anyone could name, and that was the point.[[14]] The numbers are the setting, but one number inside them carries into Part III: the interstate trade alone ended one Upper South marriage in five, and about one in three once an owner’s other interventions are counted.[[15]]
### The Pyramid
Who owned these four million? Far fewer Southerners than the word “South” implies. Roughly three of every four Southern white families owned no slaves at all; of the minority who did, most held a handful and labored beside them, dreaming of the planter class above.[[16]] The great planters — the mansions, the horse races, the European furnishings — were a thin crust at the top of the pyramid, yet the whole structure was organized around their interest, and the yeoman defended a system that impoverished him because the law gave even the poorest white man complete dominion over his household and his “property,” and because the alternative — a South without the color line — was the one thing the region’s rhetoric had taught him to fear since Bacon’s Rebellion.[[17]] The institution’s spokesmen said as much to the yeoman’s face. In December 1860, two weeks before South Carolina seceded, J. D. B. De Bow — who as superintendent of the United States Census had ordered the count of the South’s slaveholders, and cited it in the same pamphlet — told the non-slaveholders in an open letter addressed to them that “the interest of the poorest non-slaveholder among us, is to make common cause with, and die in the last trenches in defence of, the slave property of his more favored neighbor.” The man who counted the slaveholders wrote the pamphlet telling everyone else to die for them.[[18]] And the man it was pitched to? Walk down the ladder — from the planter with hundreds, to the farmer with twenty, to the farmer with one, to the three families in four with none — and at the bottom stands a white farmer whose one distinction in the whole structure was that he had not been born Black. That was what he was being asked to die for. He was still broke. The pyramid had a capstone in Washington. Twelve of the first sixteen presidential elections were won by men who owned slaves while serving as president; even the Hero of Democracy, champion of the common man, submitted to his own convictions of white male superiority and extended none of the new democracy’s freedoms to people of color, while his vice president pressed him to treat any federal gesture against slavery as an attack on liberty itself — the liberty of whites to own slaves.[[19]] The widest franchise in the world, the previous chapter concluded, and the most precisely racialized one. Here is what that franchise protected.
## Part II · The Argument
### Slavery Justified
Aside from utilizing legal means to restrict abolition efforts, the South embarked on an ideological campaign to justify White supremacy and slavery with religious arguments. Using the Bible, the Southern elite endorsed a Scriptural argument for Black inferiority, citing God’s curse, the mark of Cain, for slaying his brother Abel. In addition, Africans were affected by Noah’s punishing declaration that his grandson, Canaan, son of Ham and all his posterity, would serve his brothers as the lowest of slaves. Southern theologians also embraced these writings as both evidence for Africans’ physical differences and as justification for slavery. Furthermore, Southern authors and academics claimed that slavery gave structure and purpose to a people, who without the benevolent guidance of their masters, would surely devolve into a primitive and barbaric state, much like their African ancestors. In 1837, John C. Calhoun, who had served as Vice President under Andrew Jackson and was now Senator from South Carolina, delivered a speech before the Senate in which he rejected the older Southern defense of slavery as a “necessary evil,” choosing instead to argue that it was a “positive good.” Calhoun declared, “Compare his condition with the tenants of the poor houses in the more civilized portions of Europe, look at the sick, and the old and infirm slave, on one hand, in the midst of his family and friends, under the kind superintending care of his master and mistress…”[[20]] In essence, slavery had saved the Africans from a savage life by showing them a civilized society.
In addition to paternalistic claims, a new argument emerged based on racial superiority. According to Southern academics and religious leaders, Africans were culturally and genetically inferior, and thus placed at the bottom of the human hierarchy. Africans, argued men like George Fitzhugh across his books of the 1850s, were perpetual children: “the Negro is but a grown up child, and must be governed as a child, not as a lunatic or criminal.” Taking the view that blacks simply did not have the mental capacity to function as adults, Fitzhugh argued that it was the responsibility of white slave-owners to act as de facto parents. A precursor to the theory and “science” of eugenics, pro-slavery intellectuals declared the African race to be mentally inferior and destined to be controlled. Fitzhugh proclaimed, “We would remind those who deprecate and sympathize with Negro slavery, that his slavery here relieves him from a far more cruel slavery in Africa, or from idolatry and cannibalism, and every brutal vice and crime that can disgrace humanity.” Even as abolitionist voices rang louder, slavery sympathizers juxtaposed the state of African Americans against the condition of the “wage slaves” who labored in Northern factories. According to men like Fitzhugh, the system of free labor practiced in the North was an unhappy and hardscrabble existence running directly counter to humankind’s natural impulse for social cooperation, “It (slavery) Christianizes, protects, supports and civilizes him; that it governs him far better than free laborers at the North are governed,” argued Fitzhugh.[[21]] Furthermore, as the economic divide between small farmers and large plantations expanded, the “slaveocracy” of the South repeated the rhetoric made famous following Bacon’s Rebellion to suppress the frustrations of poor, White farmers.
The defense ran in three registers, each reaching for a higher authority than the last. The social register was paternal: the master as guardian of a people cast as children, charged to civilize and Christianize them. The religious register was Scripture, which excused the master if it was read the right way — and the South’s favorite proof text had to be misread twice to serve. Genesis 9:25 reads “Cursed be Canaan”: Noah curses Ham’s son, not Ham, and the text says nothing of skin, color, or Africa; Canaan’s line, in the text, is the peoples of the Levant. The Cain version fared no better with Black readers of the same Bible. In 1829 David Walker, a free Black clothing dealer in Boston, answered it in his Appeal: “I have searched the Bible as well as they, if I am not as well learned as they are, and have never seen a verse which testifies whether we are the seed of Cain or of Abel.”[[22]] The pseudoscientific register, which held that the races descended from separate Adams, measured skulls[[23]] — the Philadelphia physician Samuel George Morton ranked the races by cranial capacity in Crania Americana (1839), and Southern writers such as Josiah Nott put the measurements to proslavery use. And the defense came with a counterpunch: the Northern factory hand, fresh off the boat, working for starvation wages with no safety and no recourse, was the real slave. The audience is the point. The argument was not written for the North. It was written for the majority of the white South, who owned no one.[[24]] The defense concedes two things. First, every clause of it is paternal — the plantation as household, the master as parent, the enslaved as perpetual child. The argument for the institution was an argument about family, and Part III will show what that metaphor licensed and what it hid. Second, the defense was doing domestic work inside the white South itself: as the gap between planter and poor farmer widened, the slaveocracy reached for the rhetoric first invented after Bacon’s Rebellion — whatever the white farmer lacked, he was not at the bottom. Chapter 5 watched that wage invented; here it is paid again, every time the pyramid trembled. The South, in short, did not merely practice its institution. It published.
## Part III · The Making of a People
The argument of this chapter begins where the chapters on Native America began: there was no single people here to start with. The men and women carried into North American bondage shared a continent of origin and, in their captors’ eyes, a color — and beyond that, almost nothing. What follows is an account of ethnogenesis: the deliberate policy of engineered strangeness, and its complete failure; the family rebuilt wider than the law could efficiently break; the labor system and the hierarchy it installed; and the faith that preserved Africa inside the masters’ own religion. A people assembled itself from forbidden materials. The inventory follows.
### Slave Culture
For nearly two hundred years, hundreds of thousands of slaves imported to North America were sold and sent to labor alongside each other. Aside from their continent of origin and skin color, the vast majority of slaves possessed no other commonalities. Slaves were as diverse in culture and language as their European masters.
The separation began before the ships sailed. Captives who shared a language, let alone a family, were seldom kept together; no one shipped a village whole to a single plantation, and over a century people of dozens of languages were thrown together in the quarters, each culture given almost no chance of surviving intact. Anyone who doubts what happens next should consider the claim, heard often enough, that there is no American culture. Rock and roll, Hollywood, the cowboy who learned his trade from the Mexican vaquero — and country and bluegrass, grown from a mountain music that put the Scots-Irish fiddle, the German settlers’ dulcimer, and the banjo, an African instrument carried into slavery, on the same porch. Americans are a mash of peoples and places that came together without planning to, and look what came of it. Run the same mechanism at a smaller and crueler scale, on people who had no say in it at all, and the result is the culture this section describes. The separation was going to force a fusion.[[25]]
Slave owners purposefully segregated their property according to their native background to prevent any organized resistance. However, by the early 1800s, decades of compulsory coexistence had fused many of those ethnic and linguistic differences into a coalescent slave culture. Over time, the diverse array of African languages and dialects slowly adapted English vernacular to form a universal slave language and grammar. In order to retain a connection to their ancestral lands and preserve their history, storytelling was commonly practiced during family and communal gatherings. Older slaves, who were usually too feeble to labor in the fields, spent their time looking after young children and often told stories and shared African proverbs like “Br’er Rabbit.” Slave communities also developed unique recipes and dishes, which often featured rice, okra, squash, greens, and distinct seasonings.
### The Life of a Slave
The South’s recommitment to slavery forced millions of slaves to find ways to both endure the strenuous, physical demands of plantation work and preserve the traditions of their homeland. In order to survive the brutal mental and physical abuse, doled out by vicious slave drivers, slaves across the South coalesced to form new support structures, which provided ways to cope with the horrors of slavery. Slaves utilized folklore, music, and religion to help manage the physical and emotional stress of their existence. Because slave families and marriages lived in constant fear of being separated, extended family relationships became imperative. Uncles, aunts, and grandparents were extremely important in the lives of children. Where a spouse, a child, or a parent could be sold away on any day, the family adapted by widening: the grandmother’s role grew, and brother and sister became words for anyone who had stood in that place — because, at some point, anybody was a brother or a sister, and it did not have to be biological. The usage outlived the institution. Despite the frequency with which slave couples became separated, marriage ceremonies were still common.[[26]] Many slave owners recognized the value in retaining families intact, as this largely resulted in more efficient labor. While slave marriages were not legally recognized, wedding ceremonies continued to be performed with unique rituals like “jumping the broom,” and vows included the phrase, “until death or distance do you part.”[[27]] And to guarantee that any offspring remained legally enslaved, all children born to slave couples retained the status of their mother. The law could claim the body. The soul and the spirit, the quarters insisted, remained their own.
### Plantation Labor
Prior to the American Revolution, slaves in the rice and indigo fields of the Carolina lowcountry labored primarily under the “task system,” in which specific tasks were assigned daily. Once the task was accomplished, slaves could rest or spend the remainder of the day as they wished. However, the emergence of the cotton industry demanded a maximizing of labor in order to satisfy an ever increasing demand. The result was a more regimented work day, expedited by “gang labor.” More so than the lash was the fear of losing a spouse or family member to another plantation. Generally, male slaves were relegated to the fields, where they existed upon minimal food rations and lived in poorly constructed quarters that provided minimal protection from the elements. Furthermore, the task of maximizing efficiency was given to the plantation “overseer,” who was in charge of the day to day operations. Overseers usually employed “slave drivers,” who often brandished a whip, using it to compel the slaves to work faster and more diligently. It was also common for slaves to have a daily quota of picked cotton that was weighed on a scale at the end of the day. Those who failed to meet these quotas faced physical punishment, commonly in the form of whippings and beatings. Slave drivers were mostly White men, although Black men were also assigned to the role. Slaves who were particularly defiant or troublesome were sent to a “slave breaker.” Breakers specialized in the infliction of sadistic punishments designed to “break” the will of the rebellious slave. The most famous breaker in American letters is Edward Covey of Talbot County, Maryland, to whom a sixteen-year-old Frederick Douglass was hired out in 1834 precisely to be broken. After months of beatings, Douglass fought back — the struggle lasted, by his account, nearly two hours — and Covey never laid a hand on him in anger again; a breaker who could not break a teenager, Douglass reasoned, had a reputation to protect, and kept the defeat quiet. “You have seen how a man was made a slave,” Douglass wrote of that day; “you shall see how a slave was made a man.”[[28]] A slave with particular value or skill was often treated better and considered to be part of the family. These particular slaves usually labored within the master’s house.
### House Slaves
The day to day chores of maintaining plantation residences were also performed by slaves, where men served as the master’s personal servants and women provided domestic duties and reared the children of the master. Domestic or house slaves washed clothing, cooked meals, and cleaned regularly. Their duties also included the raising of the master’s children and waiting on the family and guests. House slaves were generally women of all ages and elderly men deemed too weak to labor in the fields. They also tended to be of lighter complexion and familiar with Southern etiquette. As the primary care givers, female slaves tended to every need of the children, which allowed the woman of the home to engage in her social duties. It was also common for each member of the master’s family to have a personal slave. Female domestics faced higher frequencies of sexual assault and abuse than their counterparts in the field.
### The House and the Field
Two details are among slavery’s longest-lived legacies: house slaves “tended to be of lighter complexion and familiar with Southern etiquette,” and a slave of particular value was “often treated better and considered to be part of the family.” Both describe a power structure. Proximity, complexion, and privilege were not accidents of assignment; they were the currency of a hierarchy, and the hierarchy did what hierarchies installed inside subject populations are meant to do — the mechanism we saw at Bacon’s Rebellion, when the planter elite answered a united underclass by splitting it and paying the upper fraction in status. Chapter 5 watched that wage paid in race, white servant divided from Black slave; here it was paid in shade and station, house divided from field — division as administration, not accident. And the fault line held. Who had stood nearer the big house, who had lightened, who had learned the parlor’s manners — the quarters argued over what those things meant, over favor and loyalty and belonging, and that argument ran alongside every bond this Part describes. It kept running long after the plantation itself was gone.
### Slave Religion
The culture that developed within slave society was neither entirely African nor entirely European. It emerged from the forced coexistence of people carrying different traditions, languages, beliefs, and experiences. Enslaved Africans preserved customs from their homelands while adapting to the religious and cultural practices imposed upon them. The result was not the abandonment of one culture for another, but the creation of something new.
On many plantations, Sunday provided the only regular opportunity for enslaved people to gather beyond the immediate demands of their labor. Owners permitted the day of rest in part because a rested workforce remained a productive one. What the enslaved did with that time, however, extended well beyond the purposes of the plantation. They congregated to worship, sing, dance, comfort one another, and preserve the histories and customs of their ancestors. As the religious fervor of the Second Great Awakening spread across North America, Baptist and Methodist preachers carried evangelical Christianity into the South, while some slaveholders organized religious services in an effort to “civilize” the people they held in bondage. Enslaved people encountered Christianity through these services, but they did not simply accept it on their masters’ terms. They brought their own experiences and traditions to its teachings, finding familiar themes of deliverance, suffering, judgment, and hope.
The story of Moses became especially important. The biblical account of the Israelites escaping bondage in Egypt offered a language through which enslaved people could understand their own condition and imagine its end. The God who had delivered the Israelites, they believed, could deliver them too. Spirituals carried these beliefs beyond the church meeting, preserving religious teachings, expressing grief, strengthening community, and sometimes communicating meanings that slaveholders did not recognize. Religion became a preservation of self — a way to maintain a connection to African roots while developing a shared spiritual identity in America. The institution could dictate where people worked and how they lived, but it could not entirely determine what they believed.
### The Contraband Curriculum
If religion provided a language of deliverance, literacy offered a means of understanding the world that kept enslaved people in bondage. Slaveholders understood the danger. Across the South, lawmakers increasingly restricted the education of enslaved people, fearing that the ability to read and write would encourage resistance, facilitate communication, and weaken the authority of slaveholders. South Carolina’s 1740 slave code prohibited teaching enslaved people to write. In 1800, the state broadened its restrictions to include gatherings for “mental instruction.” Virginia enacted restrictions in 1819 and strengthened them in April 1831. North Carolina prohibited teaching enslaved people to read or write in 1830–31. Other states, including Georgia, Louisiana, and Alabama, adopted similar measures. The wording varied, but the underlying fear was consistent: knowledge could make slavery harder to maintain.[[29]]
Frederick Douglass learned that lesson as a boy in Baltimore. When he went to live with Hugh and Sophia Auld, Sophia began teaching him the alphabet and helping him spell short words. Her husband discovered the lessons and ordered her to stop. In his 1845 Narrative, Douglass recalled that Auld warned his wife that education would make an enslaved person impossible to control. If Douglass learned to read, Auld explained, it would “forever unfit him to be a slave.” The lesson was unintended, but Douglass understood its meaning immediately. Auld had revealed the connection between ignorance and obedience, and between knowledge and freedom. Douglass later wrote, “From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to freedom.”
Sophia stopped teaching him, but Douglass did not stop learning. He made friends with white boys in the neighborhood and persuaded them to give him reading lessons. He traded bread for instruction, carried books on errands, and used whatever opportunity he could find to learn. The classroom had disappeared; the education continued in the streets. Douglass eventually recognized that the very people who feared his education had explained why he needed it. As he put it, “What he most dreaded, that I most desired.”[[30]]
The struggle over literacy also connected directly to the religious culture of the quarters. Enslaved people had heard the stories of Moses and the Exodus in sermons and spirituals; learning to read offered another way to encounter those stories and interpret their meaning. The Bible that slaveholders used to preach obedience also contained the story of a people delivered from bondage. The contradiction was difficult to miss. The prohibition itself revealed what slaveholders understood: a person who could read might begin questioning the authority of the people who claimed to own him.
Most slaveholding states prohibited or severely restricted the education of enslaved people, particularly instruction in reading and writing. Despite these laws and the constant threat of discovery and punishment, enslaved people continued to seek knowledge. They longed to free their bodies from bondage, but through literacy they could begin liberating what their masters could never fully control: their minds. They learned from sympathetic individuals, one another, religious gatherings, and whatever books or printed materials they could obtain. Literacy was never universal, and the opportunities to acquire it varied greatly, but the desire to learn persisted. Reading gave them a means to question the world that held them captive and imagine one beyond it. When emancipation finally came, that desire did not have to be created. Freedpeople organized schools, raised money, sought teachers, and pressed for an education that slavery had denied them. The schoolhouses of Reconstruction would become one of freedom’s most visible expressions.
The results were measurable. In 1870, roughly 20 percent of African Americans could read; by 1910, that figure had climbed to approximately 70 percent. The transformation took place within a generation and a half, despite poverty, segregation, and organized resistance to Black education. W. E. B. Du Bois recognized the significance of that achievement in Black Reconstruction, describing the public school as “a Negro idea.” Education was not simply something African Americans received after emancipation. It was something they demanded, built, financed, and defended.[[31]]
And the argument over education did not end there. Generations later, accusations that a Black student was “acting white” raised a different question: what happens when an institution once denied to a people becomes associated with a culture from which some of them feel separated? The circumstances were obviously different from slavery, but the question of who defines education, identity, and belonging remained. The effort to learn had begun long before the law permitted it. What freedom would make possible was the effort to decide what that education meant.
## Part IV · Free and Unfree
### Free Black Communities
No single state can claim a tidy “first”: Vermont’s constitution barred adult slavery in 1777, Pennsylvania passed the first state abolition statute in 1780, Massachusetts ended the institution through its courts — and most Northern states embraced a policy of “gradual emancipation.” Faster methods to freedom included escape and voluntary manumission by slave owners. Freedom arrived slowly, but it arrived. The free Black population grew from fewer than 60,000 in 1790 to more than 186,000 by 1810, and the communities behind those numbers fought for their own civil rights and for the freedom of family members still held in the South. In several New England cities, free Black men voted and free Black children sat in public classrooms, and most northern states let Black residents own property and guaranteed them trial by jury. The same decades took the vote back where it had existed: New York’s constitution of 1821 kept the ballot for white men on easy terms and required Black men to hold $250 in property, and Pennsylvania’s of 1838 restricted the franchise to “white freemen” and ended Black voting outright. The widest franchise in the world drew its line by color in the North as well as the South. In the South too, free Black families bought land, opened businesses, built churches and mutual aid societies, ran schools, and put words into print, and in North Carolina and Tennessee free Black men cast ballots into the 1830s. Although they were legally free, most African Americans living in the North had limited personal liberties and were forced to carry legal documents proving they were free.
As the Abolition Movement of the 1830s gained momentum, more slaves attempted to run away, resulting in greater efforts made by slave owners to reclaim their fugitive property. Consequently, free Blacks lived in constant fear of being mistaken for runaways and captured by bounty hunters, and thousands were wrongfully enslaved. Solomon Northup, a free-born African American from New York, had been a landowner and musician. In 1841, he was offered a traveling musician’s job and went to Washington, D.C. to perform. There he was drugged, kidnapped, and sold as a slave. He was shipped to New Orleans, purchased by a planter, and held as a slave for 12 years. He remained a slave until he met Samuel Bass, a Canadian working on his plantation who helped get word to New York, where state law provided aid to free New York citizens who had been kidnapped and sold into slavery. His family and friends enlisted the aid of the Governor of New York, Washington Hunt, and Northup regained his freedom on January 3, 1853. Northup published his autobiography, “Twelve Years a Slave,” featuring the story of his kidnapping, enslavement, and the cruelty he experienced. His book fueled Northern abolitionist efforts to bring more awareness to the brutal existence of slaves.[[32]]
### Institutions of the Free
With what little margin they had, the free communities built institutions. Two kinds matter most here, because both run straight out of Part III. The first is the church. In Philadelphia, weeks before the Constitutional Convention opened a few blocks away, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones founded the Free African Society — mutual aid, organized by free Black Philadelphians for themselves — and in the early 1790s, after the indignity of being pulled from prayer in a white congregation’s gallery, Allen’s people built Mother Bethel, dedicated in 1794; in 1816, Bethel and her sister congregations organized the African Methodist Episcopal Church, often called the first independent Black denomination in America. The invisible church of the quarters now had a visible, chartered, self-governing descendant — a straight institutional line from the Sunday gatherings of Part III to a denomination that stands today, built without anyone’s permission.[[33]] The second institution is less comfortable: the free communities also inherited the fault line. In Charleston in 1790, five free men of color founded the Brown Fellowship Society — fifty members, a fifty-dollar fee, three votes to admit; its written rules screened by class and wealth, its membership, in practice, overwhelmingly drawn from the lighter-skinned elite. In 1843 the men it did not admit organized their own society, the Humane Brotherhood, “free dark men” by their own constitution’s words — answering, the best scholarship suggests, the Fellowship’s class exclusiveness as much as its color line, the two already impossible to untangle.[[34]] The hierarchy of the quarters, in other words, did not dissolve at the edge of freedom; it incorporated. Unity built the church; division chartered two benevolent societies where one would have done. Both inheritances were carried forward together, and we will see each again.
## Part V · The Spectrum of Resistance
### Slave Revolts
During the post-Revolutionary era, the emotional rhetoric of freedom and liberty for all continued to resonate among African American communities. And with the spread of religious and secular notions of democracy and equality made popular during the Second Great Awakening, the demand for ending slavery became louder and more militant. No longer were Black Americans, as well as White abolitionists, willing to patiently wait for gradual emancipation. In 1800, Gabriel, an enslaved blacksmith in Henrico County, Virginia, was inspired by the slave revolt in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (Haiti), and secretly planned his own insurrection. With the help of other slaves, Gabriel devised a plan to seize control of Richmond by killing all of the Whites. Gabriel recruited hundreds of supporters and organized them into military units, who were to be armed with swords and pikes made from farm tools by slave blacksmiths. Gabriel planned to initiate the insurrection on the night of August 30, 1800. However, earlier in the day two slaves revealed the plot to Virginia authorities. Governor James Monroe alerted the militia. Realizing their plan had been discovered, Gabriel and many of his followers dispersed into the countryside. Gabriel escaped to Norfolk, where he was betrayed by fellow slaves who claimed the reward for his capture. He was found guilty and hanged on October 10, 1800, one of twenty-six men executed for the plot.[[35]]
In 1822, Denmark Vesey, a free Black carpenter and Methodist leader, used his position to organize Blacks, who were especially angry about the recent decision to suppress their African Church. After one loyal slave told his master about a plot to seize the city of Charleston, South Carolina and kill all the whites, local authorities exposed the most comprehensive slave plot in the history of the United States. More than 1,000 free and enslaved Blacks intended to be a part of the uprising. South Carolina authorities moved swiftly once the plot was uncovered and Vesey and thirty-four others were hanged after a dubious trial.[[36]]
Then in 1831, Nat Turner, a Virginia slave who became deeply affected by liberating and egalitarian rhetoric of the Second Great Awakening, believed he had been chosen by God to lead the slaves out of bondage and destroy the sinful institution of slavery. Turner saw himself as a modern day Moses, called by the Almighty to deliver his people. After months of planning, in the first hours of August 22, Turner and six fellow conspirators, broke into their master’s house, murdering him, his wife, and his two children. By the afternoon, nearly 60 slaves had joined Turner, going from plantation to plantation for the purpose of liberating their fellow slaves and killing their oppressors. The violence lasted over two days and took the lives of some fifty-five Whites.[[37]] This brutal slave insurrection, known as Turner’s Rebellion, initially inspired Virginia lawmakers to seek possible ways to gradually emancipate their slaves. At first, this proposal appeared to be a common sense solution to many of the smaller plantations who were losing money to the larger plantations. However, Virginia quickly reversed course, opting instead to implement harsher Slave Codes that increase punishments, organized night patrols, and established new militias. And because most plantation owners blamed the insurrection on Northern agitators, new measures were taken to censor the distribution of all abolition literature and religious teachings that encouraged slaves to rebel. Laws were also passed that criminalized the public criticism of slavery.
### Slave Resistance
Violent uprisings like the Stono Rebellion, Nat Turner’s Rebellion and those attempted by Gabriel and Denmark Vesey, were extremely rare. Rather, slaves resisted their masters in much more subtle ways. As far back as the first tobacco plantations, it was much more common for slaves to work slowly, fake illness, break farm tools, and even poison the mules and other beasts of burden as excuses to not labor in the harsh fields. For domestic slaves, spitting in the master’s coffee was a simple way to resist and maintain some degree of dignity. It dismantled nothing, and it was not meant to; it let the person who did it stand up, for a moment, and feel it. A more aggressive form of resistance involved running away. Slaves who dared to escape desperately attempted to reach the sanctuary of a swamp, a dense forest, or a northern state, which were commonly used as refuges away from the plantation. Fugitive slaves that were caught by slave catchers or bounty hunters faced significant punishments, either physical or the loss of a family member who would be sold “down river” to another plantation. Common physical punishments included the severing of the Achilles tendon, torture, and branding of the letter “R” on the face with a red-hot iron. Repeat offenders were commonly fixed with large metal collars or chains, making escape nearly impossible. Some of this was law before it was custom: South Carolina’s slave code of 1712 laid out a ladder for repeated flight — whipping, branding with an R, the loss of an ear, castration, and at the fifth escape the cutting of “the cord of one of [his] legs … above the heel,” or death. And the collars could be fitted with iron horns and bells, so that a runaway in the brush could be heard, and caught.[[38]]
Sometimes the two measures met in a single act. Edward Covey still had a defeat to hide. A slave who struck a white man could be sent to the public whipping post, and Covey had every legal means to send him. He did not, and Douglass explained why: Covey “enjoyed the most unbounded reputation for being a first-rate overseer and negro-breaker. … That reputation was at stake; and had he sent me — a boy about sixteen years old — to the public whipping-post, his reputation would have been lost; so, to save his reputation, he suffered me to go unpunished.” The system’s enforcer declined to use the system, because using it would have cost him his standing. Douglass called the fight “the turning-point in my career as a slave. It rekindled the few expiring embers of freedom, and revived within me a sense of my own manhood”; and though he remained a slave four more years, he was, in his words, “never again what might be called fairly whipped.” A dignity preserved and a piece of the machine disabled, in the same act: the question this Part asks of every form of resistance, answered by the man who lived it.[[39]]
### A Million Acts to One
The spectacular revolts were extremely rare, and the daily resistance was constant; the slowed hoe and the broken tool outnumber the uprisings by a million acts to one, and the unit of measure, in the source’s own phrase, was dignity. The deepest resistance the institution faced was not the pike or the torch but the thing Part III inventoried: a people’s decision, renewed daily for generations, to remain a people — to marry without law, to mother other women’s children, to preserve Africa inside the masters’ religion, and to keep an argument alive about their own deliverance. Part III and Part V are one story, not two: people have to feel a sense of self-worth in order to fight back, and a people without it never rises. Whether the system drove the enslaved toward having nothing left to lose, or rationed them just enough to keep them from it, remains an open question — the person with nothing left to lose is the most dangerous one in any system. The institution understood this, which is why it legislated against the culture as vigilantly as against the weapon. And the culture outlived it. So did the fault line. Zora Neale Hurston put it in her autobiography, Dust Tracks on a Road: “My skinfolks but not my kinfolks.”[[40]] And a century after emancipation, in a Detroit church in November 1963, Malcolm X could reach for a figure every listener recognized without a footnote — the house Negro and the field Negro, a frame he credited to a speaker earlier that same evening, closing “I’m a field Negro. The masses are the field Negroes” — and the quarters’ oldest argument, who belonged and who decided, was running again in the present tense.[[41]] Whether that argument is slavery’s longest shadow or a free people’s ordinary politics is a question left open on purpose, and we will return to it. The people forged in this chapter now take their argument into the national one: the abolition crusade, the fugitives, the crisis of the Union — the chapters ahead belong to them.
## Questions for Consideration
How did enslaved African Americans develop a sense of group identity and resist the institution of slavery? Build your answer from the chapter’s inventory — language, kinship, religion, and the spectrum of resistance — and weigh the proportion the chapter ends on: the rare revolt against the constant, quiet refusal.
How did economic, political, social, and ethnic factors shape the formation of a Southern identity — and why did a white majority that owned no slaves defend the institution that impoverished it? Use the pyramid, the dominion laws, and the Bacon’s Rebellion mechanism from Chapter 5.
How and why did Southern planters and politicians justify slavery? Trace the argument’s evolution from “necessary evil” to “positive good,” and explain what the shift reveals about the institution’s economic trajectory.
Slavery left two inheritances at once: the unity the quarters built, and the divisions the hierarchy installed. Trace each through the evidence — the coalescent culture and the invisible church against the house/field line and the Charleston societies — and make an argument about which inheritance proved more durable, and why.
What did the system fear more: literacy, or violence? The institution legislated against teaching as vigilantly as against weapons. What does a law against literacy concede about where power actually lay — and what does the speed of the freedpeople’s school-building after emancipation (a later chapter’s subject) suggest about what had been built in secret before it?
## Then and Now
1. [Two institutions, two hierarchies] Two institutions came out of the same generation of free Black Americans, and both are still legible. The invisible church of the quarters became Mother Bethel in 1794 and then, in 1816, the African Methodist Episcopal Church. It is often called the first independent Black denomination in America: chartered, self-governing, built without anyone’s permission, and still standing. The second is less comfortable. In Charleston in 1790, five free men of color founded the Brown Fellowship Society, whose written rules screened members by class and by wealth. The same generation that built an institution to get out from under one hierarchy built another that kept a hierarchy of its own.[[42]]
2. [Who counts as one of us?] The argument between those two institutions never closed; it moved inside. In 1963 Malcolm X could reach for the house Negro and the field Negro in a Detroit church and be understood without explanation. The vocabulary has kept coming since: “Uncle Tom,” “acting white,” “keeping it real,” “skinfolk ain’t kinfolk.” Each one is a border check on the same question: who counts as one of us, and who decides? ¶ The question now runs through the ballot box too. Black voters gave Joe Biden 92 percent of their votes in 2020 and Kamala Harris 83 percent in 2024. Test it. Is that policing a hierarchy carried out of the quarters, or what any group under pressure does to hold together? Does a vote that lopsided show a shared interest, or the cost of leaving? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a community that polices its own membership, and judge whether that border protects the people inside it or controls them.[[43]]

The 1942 printed text has nearly the opposite: “My kinfolks, and my ‘skinfolks’ are dearly loved.” The aphorism “all my skinfolk ain’t kinfolk” is a later vernacular reshaping. No documentary evidence supports a pre-twentieth-century origin, so the phrase stands here as a twentieth-century echo: evidence that the argument kept running, not of what anyone said in 1850.
