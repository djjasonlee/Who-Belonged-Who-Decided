CHAPTER 12
The Jeffersonian Republic
From the Revolution of 1800 to the Monroe Doctrine, 1800–1823
“A wise and frugal government, which shall restrain men from injuring one another, shall leave them otherwise free to regulate their own pursuits of industry and improvement, and shall not take from the mouth of labor the bread it has earned.”
— Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1801
The last chapter ended on the night the Federalists lost power and the morning they handed it over — the moment the republic proved it could change hands without breaking. This chapter follows the people who received it. The Republicans came to power in 1801 as the party of the small government, the strict reading of the Constitution, the independent farmer, and the suspicion of everything Hamilton had built: the bank, the debt, the standing army, the navy, the centralized state. They meant to take it apart, and for a while they did. And then the world refused to cooperate. Over the next two decades the party of strict construction would double the size of the nation by a purchase its own president believed the Constitution did not permit, fight its first foreign war on the far side of the Atlantic, lay the heaviest federal hand on American commerce that any government had yet attempted, and blunder into a second war with Britain that nearly broke the republic in half. By the time the last of the Virginia presidents sent his annual message to Congress in 1823, the Federalist Party was dead — and its victors were chartering Hamilton’s bank and warning the empires of Europe away from an entire hemisphere. Ironically, the founders’ great quarrel ended with the Republicans holding the government alone and running it by Hamilton’s design — becoming, in power, much of what they had opposed. And every acre the republic gained came at the expense of Native nations, whose homelands were surveyed, divided, and offered for sale. The republic widened the circle of settler ownership while narrowing, treaty by treaty, the lands Native nations could call their own. Washington, D.C., helped set the terms of both.
## Part I · The Revolution of 1800
### Jefferson and Liberty
By the time those eligible to vote cast their ballots, there was little doubt that the Federalists’ time in power was over. John Adams’s support for the Alien and Sedition Acts and their subsequent jailing of a number of Republican newspaper editors thrust individual liberties and the freedom of the press to the center of the presidential campaign. In denouncing Adams and his measures, Thomas Jefferson developed a campaign slogan of “Jefferson and Liberty,” suggesting that a vote for him was not merely to side with the Republicans, but with the triumph of American freedom. Indeed, Jefferson referred to his election as the “Revolution of 1800.”[[1]]
### The Tie
The only question that remained was which Democratic Republican would take over. Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr had an equal number of electoral votes, which placed the fate of the executive office in the hands of the House of Representatives. Burr, who ran as Jefferson’s Vice President, was a native New Yorker and the grandson of Jonathan Edwards, the popular New England preacher of the First Great Awakening. Burr, a veteran of the Revolutionary War, had spent most of his time after the war serving in public office. However, having made powerful friends on both sides of the political spectrum, Burr’s true political ideology remained a mystery, something that provoked criticism from Alexander Hamilton. For six days, the House of Representatives voted over 30 times to decide on the next president, each time failing to reach the nine-state majority.[[2]] Rumors began circulating that Burr was involved in a secret campaign with the Federalists of the northern states, who offered their help in exchange for his loyalty to the party.
However, it was perhaps the most influential Federalist of all, Alexander Hamilton, who is often credited for swaying several key votes in the House for Jefferson. On the 36th ballot, Delaware’s lone congressman, James Bayard, cast a blank ballot, as did the Federalists of Maryland and Vermont, and Jefferson carried ten states, breaking the political impasse.[[3]]
### The Repair
The near-disaster exposed the flaw a second time, and the republic fixed it. The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, required electors to cast separate ballots for president and vice president, ending the arrangement that had yoked rivals together in 1796 and nearly elevated the wrong man in 1800.[[4]] It is a small amendment and an important precedent: the founders’ machine, discovering a defect in operation, was repaired by the procedure the machine itself provided. Washington’s worry in the Farewell had been that parties would break the republic. In 1804 the republic bent its own rules to accommodate them instead — an admission, written into the Constitution, that the party system was now permanent.
### Jefferson Takes the Oath
On March 4, 1801, Jefferson took the oath in Washington, D.C., the first president sworn in at the new capital. The capital itself looked more like a construction site than a seat of government: mud for streets, poverty in the neighborhoods, and half-built public buildings. Jefferson’s inaugural address was conciliatory toward his opponents. “Every difference of opinion,” he declared, “is not a difference of principle. . .We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists.”[[5]] He then laid out a program that was the Federalist decade in reverse: economy in government, free trade, freedom of religion and the press, “peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations, entangling alliances with none.” Leave Americans “free to regulate their own pursuits,” he argued, keep the government lean, and the country would prosper on its own. His goal: dismantle the Federalist machinery, piece by piece. Early in his presidency he pardoned every man still jailed under the Sedition Act. Across two terms he trimmed the federal workforce and cut the army and navy to the bone. He repealed every internal tax, the hated whiskey tax among them, kept only the tariff, and paid down part of the national debt.[[6]] Hamilton had pictured a strong central state on the European model; Jefferson spent his presidency keeping Washington small and out of the marketplace so that picture never came true. In addition, Adams’s lastminute appointment of multiple circuit judges caused Jefferson to distrust the unelected judiciary, favoring instead the belief in an elected, local self-government.
### Marbury v. Madison
One of the aforementioned judges was Supreme Court Chief Justice, John Marshall, who would serve during the entirety of Jefferson’s presidency and for many years thereafter. Adams had made Marshall, his own secretary of state, chief justice in the final weeks before Jefferson’s inauguration.[[7]] A firm nationalist, Marshall built a Court that claimed authority to judge the laws of Congress and the states alike. The first great test came in 1803, in Marbury v. Madison. In his final hours as president, Adams had appointed a slate of local magistrates to serve the capital, and James Madison, now Jefferson’s secretary of state, refused to hand over their commissions, the documents that made the appointments official, to these “midnight judges.” Four of the would-be justices sued for their posts, William Marbury first among them. Marshall ruled that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting the courts order executive officials to deliver commissions went beyond the powers the Constitution gave Congress, and so it had no force. Put plainly, Marbury deserved his commission, yet the Court lacked the constitutional authority to order Madison to give it to him. On the narrow question before the Court, Jefferson’s side won. As Jefferson saw it, though, the victory came at a high price. In winning, though, the Court had claimed the right to void any act of Congress it found unconstitutional, a power that came to be called “judicial review.”[[8]]
### The Interview at Weehawken
The election of 1800 claimed one more casualty, and it took four years to collect. By the summer of 1804 Aaron Burr had little left to lose: the Republicans had dropped him from the ticket in the first election under the Twelfth Amendment, and New York voters had rejected him for governor that spring. Then he read, in a letter published in an Albany newspaper, that Hamilton — having already called him a dangerous man — had privately expressed “a still more despicable opinion” of him, and he demanded satisfaction.[[9]] By the code of honor that governed such affairs among gentlemen, the challenged party chose the weapons, and Hamilton chose pistols. Each man brought a second, a trusted friend sworn to see the rules kept and the wounded tended — Nathaniel Pendleton for Hamilton, William Van Ness for Burr. Dueling was illegal in most states, which is why the arrangements were built for deniability. At around five in the morning on July 11, 1804, the two men were rowed in separate boats across the Hudson from New York City to a secluded ledge on the cliffs near Weehawken, New Jersey. The oarsmen and the physician, David Hosack, stayed below with the boats, so that they could swear honestly that they had witnessed nothing; only the seconds watched. Illegal or not, duels were far from rare, and wealthy men in particular believed that when honor was seriously attacked, there was no other way to defend it. The duelists stood ten paces apart. Moments after the word “present,” shots rang out. Hamilton was struck in his right side and died the next day.[[10]]
### Two Seconds, Two Stories
What happened in the instant after “present” has been argued over for two centuries, and the argument began within a week. The two seconds published a joint account of the duel that agreed on nearly everything except the one thing that mattered: who fired first. Van Ness, Burr’s second, claimed “full & perfect recollection.” In his telling, Hamilton took his pistol, raised it as if to test the light, and lowered it again — “I beg pardon for delaying you but the direction of the light renders it necessary” — then drew his spectacles from his pocket and put them on. At the word “present,” both men took aim. Hamilton’s pistol went off first, and Burr fired after, “only five or six seconds of time intervening.” Van Ness had been watching his principal at the first shot and saw a slight motion in his body, as if Burr had been hit; rowing back, Burr blamed a small stone under his foot, and said the smoke from Hamilton’s pistol had hidden his opponent from him for a moment.[[11]] If Hamilton fired first, the logic of the field held, then he had fired at Burr.
### In His Own Hand
Hamilton’s second answered on July 19 with a statement of his own. Pendleton was confident that Hamilton had not fired first — and had not fired at Burr at all — and he called it “a sacred duty” to Hamilton’s memory, to his country, and to his friends to publish the facts that had convinced him. He began with evidence already in print: the testimony of Bishop Benjamin Moore, who attended Hamilton as he died, and a paper in Hamilton’s own hand, sealed with his will in a packet that was not to be delivered unless he was killed. The Evening Post had published it three days earlier. In it Hamilton listed every reason he had to avoid the interview: his “religious and moral principles,” which were “strongly opposed to the practice of Duelling”; a wife and children to whom his life was “of the utmost importance”; creditors who would suffer if his property had to be sold; no ill will toward Burr “distinct from political opposition”; and the plain arithmetic that he would “hazard much, and can possibly gain nothing.” Then he recorded what he meant to do:[[12]]
“I have resolved, if our interview is conducted in the usual manner, and it pleases God to give me the opportunity, to reserve and throw away my first fire, and I have thoughts even of reserving my second fire — and thus giving a double opportunity to Col Burr to pause and to reflect.”
— Alexander Hamilton, Statement on Impending Duel with Aaron Burr, written June 28–July 10, 1804
Why go at all, then? Because, he wrote, his position in public and private life, and “all the considerations which constitute what men of the world denominate honor,” laid on him “a peculiar necessity not to decline the call.” His ability to be useful in the national crises he saw coming “would probably be inseparable from a conformity with public prejudice in this particular.”[[13]]
### The Same Pistols
Hamilton went to Weehawken knowing exactly what the code could cost. On July 4, 1801, a young Republican lawyer named George Eacker had delivered an oration in New York that savaged the Federalists — and the financial and military policies that were largely Hamilton’s. That November, Hamilton’s eldest son, Philip, nineteen years old, went to the Park Theater with a friend named Price, and the two young men mocked the speech loudly near Eacker’s box. Insults followed outside the theater, then challenges. Price’s duel with Eacker ended with no one hurt. Philip’s did not. When Hamilton learned of the meeting, one of Philip’s college classmates recalled, he ordered his son to hold his fire until Eacker had shot, and then to discharge his pistol into the air. On the afternoon of November 23, 1801, at Paulus Hook, New Jersey, across the Hudson from the city, Eacker shot Philip, who died the next morning — his “agonized father” on one side of the bed and his “distracted mother” on the other. The pistols that afternoon were probably the ones owned by Philip’s uncle, John Barker Church: the same pair Hamilton carried to Weehawken. Few events in Hamilton’s life struck him harder. It did not lead him to refuse Burr. Three years later, the father crossed the same river with the same pistols and the same plan he had given his son.[[14]]
### Pendleton’s Answer
Then Pendleton laid out what he had seen and heard himself, point by point. At least ten days before the duel, Hamilton had told him he was weighing whether to receive Burr’s first fire and not return it. Pendleton argued against it — the plan was dangerous to Hamilton himself, and unnecessary when every settlement short of humiliation had already been offered and refused — and Hamilton promised to deliberate. On the evening before the duel he gave his answer: he would not fire at Burr the first time, but would receive his fire and fire in the air. When Pendleton pressed him once more, Hamilton closed the subject in words that, Pendleton wrote, made an impression on his mind “which can never be effaced”: “My friend, it is the effect of a RELIGIOUS SCRUPLE, and does not admit of reasoning, it is useless to say more on the subject, as my purpose is definitely fixed.” On the ground, the purpose had not changed. Asked whether he wanted the hair spring of his pistol set — a hair trigger that fired at the lightest touch — he answered, “Not this time.” Lying wounded in the boat, his first words, spoken to a gentleman Pendleton had told of the plan that very morning, were these: “Pendleton knows I did not mean to fire at Col. Burr the first time.” When one of the boatmen picked up Hamilton’s pistol, which lay loose in its open case, Hamilton warned him: “Take care of that pistol — it is cocked. It may go off and do mischief.” That, Pendleton argued, showed that Hamilton “was not sensible of having fired at all”: had he fired before he was hit, he would have known the pistol could not go off; if it went off afterward, it was “the effect of an involuntary exertion of the muscles produced by a mortal wound.”
Last came the physical evidence. Pendleton was so certain that a first shot from Hamilton could not have escaped his attention — “all his anxiety being alive for the effect of the first fire” — and so willing to grant that Van Ness sincerely believed the opposite, that he went looking for proof. The day after Hamilton died, he returned to the ledge with a friend and found it: Hamilton’s ball had passed through the limb of a cedar tree about twelve and a half feet off the ground, thirteen or fourteen feet from where Hamilton stood, and about four feet wide of the line between the two men, on the right — Hamilton had fallen to the left. The limb was cut off, brought back to the city, and placed in the keeping of John Barker Church. And there Pendleton stopped: “No inferences are pointed out as resulting from these facts, nor will any comments be made. They are left to the candid judgment and feelings of the public.”[[15]]
Nearly two centuries later, the historian Joseph Ellis weighed both seconds’ testimony and offered a reconstruction that reconciles them: Hamilton fired first, as Van Ness insisted, but deliberately wasted his shot in the trees above Burr’s head, as the cedar limb suggests; Burr, with no way of knowing Hamilton’s intention, heard the ball pass overhead and fired back, and his shot struck home.[[16]] It is a reconstruction, not a verdict. The two seconds left accounts that still disagree, and the evidence is all on the table — the sealed statement, the spectacles, the five or six seconds, the stone underfoot, the religious scruple, the cocked pistol, the cedar limb. Pendleton’s closing instruction still stands: the facts are left to the candid judgment of the reader.
The sitting vice president of the United States, forty-eight years old, had killed the first secretary of the Treasury, the architect of the financial system the Republicans had spent three years taking apart. Burr was indicted for murder in New Jersey and for dueling in New York, never stood trial, and returned to Washington to finish his term presiding over the Senate.[[17]] The two men who had decided the election of 1800 between them were both finished — one in the grave, the other in public life. Burr never held office again.
## Part II · An Empire of Liberty
### The Louisiana Purchase
Perhaps the greatest accomplishment of Thomas Jefferson’s presidency, the acquisition of the Louisiana Territory, is also the most ironic. As a staunch opponent of excessive federal power, Jefferson most assuredly experienced some cognitive dissonance when presented with the opportunity to expand the size of the nation.
When a massive slave rebellion in the French colony of Saint Domingue (Haiti) forced Napoleon Bonaparte to concentrate his military and resources in Europe, Jefferson saw an opportunity to solidify American interests.[[18]] New Orleans had worried Jefferson for years. Western farmers could not live without the port, and Spain had guaranteed American use of it in the Treaty of San Lorenzo, also called Pinckney’s Treaty, in 1795. France, far stronger than Spain, might not honor that promise. He dispatched an envoy to France offering to purchase the city for $10 million. Napoleon needed cash for his European wars, and the Haitian Revolution had killed his dream of a New World empire, so he put the whole of Louisiana on the table for $15 million, roughly three cents an acre, the real estate deal of the century.[[19]]
### The Strict Constructionist’s Dilemma
The Louisiana Purchase forced Jefferson to reconsider his strict interpretation of the Constitution. For years he had insisted the federal government could do only what the Constitution spelled out, and the Constitution said nothing about buying land. Thus, Jefferson pragmatically (some say conveniently) adopted a loose interpretation of the Constitution and used its treaty-making powers, outlined in Article II, to complete the deal with France. One signature doubled the size of the country and ended France’s hold on the North American mainland. Federalists were dismayed. “We are to give money, of which we have too little,” one declared, “for land, of which we already have too much.” Jefferson admitted that he had “done an act beyond the Constitution.” Still, he decided the prize outweighed the constitutional foul. Farmers, Jefferson had written, were “the chosen people of God,” and the country would remain “virtuous” as long as it was “chiefly agricultural.” Citing the Federalists’ (Hamilton) loose interpretation of the “Necessary and Proper Clause” and the Federalist Papers (Federalist no. 10), Jefferson ironically justified his decision to utilize federal power. Madison, in fact, had argued that a bigger republic would govern itself better, not worse: “extend the sphere,” he advised. All that land, Jefferson believed, guaranteed his republic of farmers, and its stability, for generations to come.
### Jefferson and the Native Nations
The new western lands, wrote Jefferson, would be “the means of tempting all our Indians on the East side of the Mississippi to remove to the West.”[[20]] Once again, we see the complexity of a man who expressed multiple views regarding Native Americans. As a boy, Jefferson had only a few encounters with America’s indigenous, which sparked his curiosity and admiration. Thomas Jefferson believed Native American peoples to be a “noble race” who were "in body and mind equal to the white man,” and were endowed with an innate moral sense and a high capacity for reason. Jefferson even maintained a large collection of indigenous artifacts in his home. But like many of his land-owning contemporaries, Jefferson also saw Native Americans as obstacles to American expansion into the West. He even included his contempt for native occupation in the Declaration of Independence saying, that King George III had “endeavoured to bring on the inhabitants of our frontiers, the merciless Indian Savages, whose known rule of warfare, is an undistinguished destruction of all ages, sexes and conditions.” While Jefferson never authorized federal power to remove any indigenous people, he did not envision both societies living together.[[21]]
### The Corps of Discovery
A year after the purchase, Jefferson sent two Virginians, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, both veterans of the Ohio Valley Indian wars, to lead an expedition west. Their mission was to explore and map the new territory, establish relations with Native Americans. The mission carried scientific and commercial orders as well: record the plants, the animals, and the land, and report how American farmers and merchants might put them to use. Jefferson also wanted trade with the western nations and a river road to the Pacific, with the markets of Asia waiting at the other end. Lewis and Clark pushed off from St. Louis in the spring of 1804 with roughly fifty men, the “corps of discovery,” on what would become the most famous expedition in American history.[[22]] In 1805, after passing the winter on the Great Plains, they received critical help from a Shoshone teenager living among the Hidatsa, named Sacagawea, the wife of a French fur trader. Sacagawea served as both an interpreter and a diplomat for the expedition, and she recognized landmarks that helped lead them through treacherous lands. In one critical encounter with the Shoshone, Sacagawea helped the captains secure the horses they needed after she recognized her brother. Originally born as a member of the Shoshone tribe, Sacagawea was captured as a young girl by the Hidatsa. And much like a scene from a Hollywood movie, the chief of the very band the captains had come looking for turned out to be her brother.[[23]] Over the Rockies and down the Columbia, the expedition reached the Pacific in November 1805, in present-day Oregon, well past the edge of the new purchase. When Lewis and Clark came back to St. Louis in September 1806, they carried journals full of geography, wildlife, and Native cultures, along with crates of plant and animal specimens. They never found a commercial water route to Asia, but they proved that Americans could cross the continent by land. They also found that the western nations already traded with Europeans and already belonged to a global market. Their success fed a growing belief that the United States would one day stretch to the Pacific.
### Complex Characters: Sacagawea
Lewis and Clark needed someone who spoke Shoshone, the language of the people whose horses they would need to cross the Rocky Mountains. They hired Toussaint Charbonneau, a French-Canadian trader at least twenty years older than his wife, because his wife spoke it. Sacagawea, then about sixteen or seventeen, had been born among the Lemhi Shoshone. Around the age of twelve, a Hidatsa raiding party captured her and carried her some four hundred miles east to the Knife River villages, where Charbonneau later took her as a wife. On February 11, 1805, at Fort Mandan, she gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste. Less than two months later she left with the expedition, the infant on her back.
Her work went well beyond words, though the words alone took four people to carry: Sacagawea spoke to Charbonneau in Hidatsa, Charbonneau translated into French, and an expedition member carried the French into English. When a squall nearly capsized a boat on the Missouri in May 1805, she pulled journals, instruments, and medicine from the water, and Lewis credited her with “equal fortitude and resolution, with any person onboard.”[[24]] In August she recognized Beaverhead Rock and told the captains they neared her people’s country. At the council of August 17 she recognized the Shoshone leader, Cameahwait, as her brother, and with her interpreting and the captains’ promises of trade, the expedition came away with the horses that carried it over the Bitterroots. Clark also understood what her presence meant to the nations they met: “a woman with a party of men is a token of peace.”[[25]]
Sacagawea did not guide the expedition across the continent. Hired guides such as Old Toby led the party through the mountains, and much of the country lay as new to her as to the captains. On the return journey in July 1806, however, she pointed Clark toward the gap now called Bozeman Pass, and he wrote that she had “been of great Service to me as a pilot through this Country.”[[26]]
The journals record her, usually through someone else. At the mouth of the Columbia on November 24, 1805, Clark polled the party on where to spend the winter. He counted York’s vote and noted Sacagawea’s preference for a place with plenty of wapato root, but did not tally hers.[[27]] Six weeks later, when a whale washed ashore, she insisted on going to see it. Clark wrote that “She had traveled a long way with us to See the great waters, and that now that monstrous fish was also to be Seen, She thought it verry hard that She Could not be permitted to See either.”[[28]] He took her. The entry preserves something the journals rarely record: what she wanted.
When the expedition returned to the Mandan villages in August 1806, Charbonneau received $500.33 in pay, and a 320-acre land grant followed. Sacagawea received nothing. York, who also made the entire journey, received nothing and remained enslaved. On August 20, Clark wrote to Charbonneau that “your woman who accompanied you that long dangerous and fatigueing rout to the Pacific Ocian and back diserved a greater reward for her attention and services on that rout than we had in our power to give her.”[[29]] Clark backed the words with more than ink. He offered to raise and educate her son, and in 1813 he became the legal guardian of Jean Baptiste and his younger sister, Lisette, and paid for the boy’s schooling in St. Louis.[[30]]
On December 20, 1812, a fur-company clerk at Fort Manuel recorded the death of Charbonneau’s wife, calling her “a good and the best Women in the fort.” Clark’s list of expedition members from the late 1820s marks her as dead. A Shoshone tradition holds that she returned to her people and lived until 1884 on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming. The documents favor 1812, and the tradition belongs to the history of how her people remembered her.[[31]]
Fame came nearly a century later. Eva Emery Dye’s 1902 novel, The Conquest, made her a Shoshone princess who led the explorers west, and suffragists adopted her as proof of women’s capacity to lead. A statue unveiled in Portland in 1905 placed her at the front of the march. No portrait of Sacagawea survives. When the United States Mint put her on the dollar coin in 2000, a Shoshone-Bannock college student, Randy’L Hedow Teton, posed in her place.[[32]]
Between the legend and the ledger stands a captive girl taken as a wife, recruited for her language, who carried her son to the Pacific and back and helped the captains find the people and the horses they needed.
## Part III · The World Will Not Leave Us Alone
### The Shores of Tripoli
The Louisiana Purchase made one thing plain: whatever Americans said about their distance from the Old World, their fortunes still rode on what happened across the Atlantic. American farmers still sold much of their crop in British markets, so every European quarrel put the living of farmers, merchants, and artisans at risk. As president, Jefferson hoped to avoid being drawn in by foreign disputes, but he soon found it impossible. He wanted a lean national government, but the world kept forcing him to grow one, and it pulled the country into its first war abroad.
Only a few months after being sworn in as president, Jefferson again demonstrated his ability to fluctuate between political ideologies when he sent into battle the very navy he had attacked John Adams for building.
Beginning in 1785, as the United States struggled to establish their identity in the world, the Barbary states of North Africa — Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, nominally provinces of the Ottoman Empire, along with independent Morocco — began attacking American merchant ships in the Mediterranean and Atlantic. These Muslim pirates captured American sailors and their cargo, often selling the crew members as slaves. Between 1785 and 1796, Barbary corsairs took thirteen American vessels and kept over a hundred seamen as “slaves,” and American shipping in the Mediterranean all but stopped. Washington and then Adams followed the advice of Spanish diplomats: they bought back captives at a cost running to hundreds of thousands of dollars and pledged yearly tribute to keep the corsairs off American ships.[[33]] As the American diplomat to France, Jefferson became familiar with the Barbary pirates and formed a relationship with several Muslim envoys. In 1801, after the Pasha of Tripoli increased the ransom amount, Jefferson refused, and asked Congress to provide funding for additional naval ships and to send a military force to Northern Africa. From 1801 to 1805, the United States Navy, and later Marines, fought against the Barbary states, until a decisive victory was achieved in Tripoli. In 1805, Marine Lieutenant Presley O’Bannon was presented with a Mameluke sabre by Hamet Karamanli, the Pasha’s brother, after the march on Derna; the sword is still a part of the Marine officer’s uniform.[[34]] In the Barbary Wars the young republic met the Islamic world for the first time, and it met it in battle. Seeking peace, the federal government had even declared, in the Treaty of Tripoli of 1797, that the government of the United States “is not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion.”[[35]] The wars also left Americans with a lasting habit of picturing Muslims as exotic outsiders, a people whose customs lay beyond the bounds of the West.
### Impressment and the Chesapeake
The war between Britain and France did far more damage to the United States. Under international law, a neutral country could sell goods other than weapons to nations at war. However, by 1806, both the French and the British sought to deny each other all trade with America. Britain revived impressment and seized more than 6,000 American seamen, on the claim that they belonged to Britain as subjects and deserters. In 1807 the British frigate Leopard even fired on the American warship Chesapeake off the Virginia coast, boarded her, and seized four of her crew.[[36]] In addition, Napoleon Bonaparte also sanctioned the capture of American vessels in the Caribbean.
### The Embargo
Free trade, in Jefferson’s view, sat at the heart of American prosperity, and no foreign government had any business interfering with it. American farmers lived on sales to Europe and the West Indies. He made trade his weapon, much as the patriots had done against Parliament in the 1760s and 1770s. Congress, pressed by Jefferson, passed the Embargo Act in December 1807, barring American ships from sailing to any foreign port.[[37]] For a champion of limited government, it amounted to a stunning display of federal power. He hoped it would force Britain and France to leave American ships alone and give the Royal Navy fewer chances to impress American sailors.
However, much like the ineffectiveness of the colonies’ non-importation acts, the Embargo of 1807 had little effect upon the British. Conversely, in 1808, American exports plummeted, paralyzing the economy. The embargo did real harm at home, knocking about 5 percent off the gross national product and dragging down the whole economy. Exports collapsed by four-fifths in a single year, from $108 million to $22 million, and farmers took the hit along with the merchants. Before the embargo, one visitor to New York City remembered, “All was noise and bustle”; afterward the town sat shuttered as if “a malignant fever was raging in the place.”[[38]] Britain and France, each locked in a fight to the death, scarcely noticed. At home, the embargo gutted the economies of the port cities. Ultimately, Jefferson left office at the lowest point in his political career.
### A Bundle of Contradictions
The third President of the United States of America can aptly be summarized in one word; complex. Thomas Jefferson was a man of both principle and impulse, of mind and heart. From his immortalized words, “All Men are Created Equal,” written as an owner of human property, to his hatred of a standing army, all while expanding the might of the American military, Thomas Jefferson is perhaps the greatest conundrum of a man. As a passionate consumer of biological and cultural artifacts, and a genius in the art of politics, Jefferson was brilliant and constantly thirsted for knowledge. His monumental contributions to the foundation of America stand in stark contrast to his deep, human flaws. “A bundle of contradictions,” as many historians label him.[[39]] As president, Jefferson did his best to both fulfill his vision for an agrarian America, and wield federal power to achieve his objectives. He strived to both expand the nation and natural rights, often struggling to balance the use of power and liberty, of slavery and freedom. He doubled the size of the nation, expanded access to democracy and opportunities to own land, shrank the size of the federal government, cut the national debt by a third, and solidified religious freedom.
### Complex Characters: Thomas Jefferson
Most politicians contradict themselves opportunistically and never quite notice. Jefferson is different. In nearly every case, he is the person who wrote the most rigorous version of the principle he later violated.
The cleanest example is Louisiana. He spent the 1790s as the philosophical anchor of the “no implied powers” reading of the Constitution; that reading is the entire case against Hamilton’s Bank. Then the Louisiana Purchase landed in his lap in 1803, and there is no enumerated power to acquire and incorporate foreign territory. He drafted a constitutional amendment to authorize it, then quietly shelved the amendment and did the deal anyway.[[40]] He knew exactly what he was violating. That is not drift. It is a man overriding his own theory in real time, honest with himself about it in private correspondence while staying silent in public.
The Kentucky Resolutions make it sharper, because they came in 1798, five years before Louisiana. Jefferson secretly wrote the most radical states’-rights nullification argument of the founding era.[[41]] As president he became the one wielding the expansive federal power — Louisiana, the Embargo of 1807 — that a Kentucky Resolutions Jefferson should have found terrifying coming from anyone else.
The press is a more personal contradiction. “Were it left to me to decide whether we should have a government without newspapers, or newspapers without a government, I should not hesitate a moment to prefer the latter” is Jefferson in 1787. In 1803, President Jefferson suggested to Pennsylvania’s governor that “a few prosecutions of the most eminent offenders” among the Federalist editors, brought in state courts, “would have a wholsome effect.”[[42]] The man who had placed newspapers above government now considered using government to discipline newspapers, deciding the principle does not bind him if the mechanism is technically different. The distinction between federal and state authority gave him a way to defend the principle while weakening its protection.
He despised faction and party spirit as a threat to republican unity — “we are all Republicans, we are all Federalists” — while being, functionally, the single most effective party organizer of the founding generation. He built the apparatus he claimed to fear.
What ties it together: Jefferson was the era’s best systematizer of political theory, which meant he was also the person most exposed to the gap between theory and the actual exercise of power. Hamilton mostly wanted more federal power and got it. Adams was consistent to the point of being politically miserable. Jefferson kept building the sharpest possible case against the exact thing he would eventually do.
The Deism makes the whole pattern legible, because it is the same operation he ran on politics, applied to scripture first. The Jefferson Bible (a first cut in 1804, the full compilation around 1820) is the method in miniature.[[43]] He took a razor to the Gospels and cut out every miracle, every resurrection, every supernatural claim, keeping only the ethical teaching of Jesus, because he trusted reason to separate the authentic moral core from what he considered priestly corruption layered on top over centuries. That is the same move as stripping away monarchy, aristocracy, and inherited privilege to find the natural rights underneath. He approached both texts as corrupted originals waiting for a rational editor.
That makes the political contradictions less like hypocrisy and more like a structural risk of the method itself. A man who believes he can rationally purify any inherited system down to its true core — a testament, a constitution — will occasionally decide in the moment that the pure principle does not apply to the emergency in front of him, because he has already trained himself to treat the received version as provisional. Strict construction was the corrupted text he had purified down to enumerated powers only. The Louisiana Purchase was the moment he decided the emergency justified editing his own edit.
The science fits the same frame. His fight with the French naturalist Buffon over American degeneracy — Buffon claimed New World species were smaller, weaker, degenerate versions of Old World ones — was not vanity. Jefferson spent years assembling mastodon bones and moose specimens to out-empiricize a European naturalist on his own turf.[[44]] Notes on the State of Virginia is partly a data table built to win an argument. Same instinct as the Bible, same instinct as the Constitution: do not just assert the position; build the evidentiary system that forces the conclusion.
## Part IV · The Second War of Independence
### Little Jemmy
The embargo nearly cost the Republicans the presidency. Federalists, revived by the economic wreckage, accused the Virginia Dynasty of wrecking northern commerce to serve southern interests, and they mocked the Republican nominee, James Madison, for his slight build and sickly look — “Little Jemmy,” all five feet four of him. Some Republicans, sensing defeat, considered jumping ship to share in a Federalist victory; Jefferson held them in line. When the electoral votes were counted, Madison had beaten the Federalist Charles Cotesworth Pinckney 122 to 47.[[45]] But victory did not make the problem go away. The embargo was repealed as Jefferson left office and replaced by more limited restrictions, and in 1810 Madison tried a different lever: the United States would reopen trade with both belligerents, and if either one stopped harassing American ships, America would cut off trade with the other. Napoleon, seeing his chance to turn the United States against Britain, agreed — at least on paper. Britain, needing sailors more than ever, kept seizing ships and impressing crews, and in 1811 trade with Britain was cut off again.[[46]] The two nations were drifting toward war, and the drift was accelerated by events a thousand miles from the sea.
### Trouble in the West
The Treaty of Paris of 1783, which ended the Revolution, had ceded Britain’s lands west of the Appalachians to the United States, and settlers rushed across the mountains to claim them. The treaty overlooked one detail: the land had not been Britain’s to give. It belonged to the Native nations who had lived on it for generations, and they fought back in the long Northwest Indian War of the 1780s and 1790s. In November 1791 a confederacy of Miami and Shawnee warriors, led by Little Turtle and Blue Jacket respectively, and joined by Delaware and Potawatomi allies, overran General Arthur St. Clair’s army near the headwaters of the Wabash, killing more than six hundred soldiers in the worst defeat Native forces ever inflicted on the United States Army. The war ended in their defeat at Fallen Timbers in 1794 and the land cessions of the Treaty of Greenville in 1795.[[47]] Under Jefferson, as Part II showed, the pressure for cessions only grew — through purchase, through debt, through relentless negotiation. Some nations sold and moved; others resisted. And the British, watching from across the border in Canada, were glad to help them resist, supplying guns and ammunition to the Shawnee and other nations from their posts on the Canadian side of the Great Lakes. To westerners, every frontier raid looked like a British raid. The war that was coming would be fought on two fronts, as the Revolution had been — against Britain on the sea and along the border, and against the Native nations of the interior.
### The Prophet and the Chief
What the Native nations of the Ohio Valley faced follows from the colonial pattern: for two centuries, Native nations had survived by playing empire against empire, French against British, Spanish against both, holding the balance of the continent through diplomacy. That world — the “middle ground,” in the historians’ phrase — was gone. With France expelled and Spain fading, the nations of the Ohio Valley faced a single expanding power with no rival to bargain against, and they knew it. The years from 1800 to 1812 were an age of prophecy among them, as leaders searched for new ways to survive. Some chose accommodation: Cornplanter of the Seneca, who had made peace with the United States after the Revolution, urged his people to preserve themselves, ironically, by adopting American farming and schooling. Others chose revival. Among the Shawnee, a prophet named Tenskwatawa preached that whites were the source of the world’s evils and that Native peoples must renounce American alcohol, clothing, food, and goods and return to the old ways; his followers gathered at Prophetstown on the Wabash River in Indiana. His brother Tecumseh, a chief who would not put his name to the Treaty of Greenville, carried the message south and west, building a pan-Indian alliance across nations that had often been rivals — the same dream Neolin and Pontiac had pursued half a century earlier. What set this uprising apart was its scale. With few exceptions, Native conflicts with Europeans stayed regional, drawing in no more than a handful of nations at a time, because Native America had never been the single, unified people so often portrayed (see Chapter 1). Its nations held as much diversity as the kingdoms of Africa, each with its own language, religion, vision, and goals, and a war in one region placed no obligation on nations in another to take up arms, any more than a war between France and Prussia required Spain to choose a side. Native nations weakened by disease, bound by dependency on European goods, and divided by their own diversity of needs and goals had never built a pan-Indian alliance capable of defeating European power. The alternative to unity, the brothers warned, was extinction. “Where today are the Pequot?” Tecumseh asked. “Where are the Narragansett, the Mohican, the Pokanoket, and many other once powerful tribes of our people? They have vanished before the avarice and oppression of the white man, as snow before a summer sun.” And to chiefs who had sold land to the federal government, he answered: “Sell a country! Why not sell the air, the great sea, as well as the earth?”[[48]] In November 1811, while Tecumseh was away recruiting, the governor of Indiana Territory, William Henry Harrison, marched on Prophetstown and destroyed it at the Battle of Tippecanoe. Tecumseh led what remained of his confederacy north, to the British.
### The War Hawks
News that the British were arming Tecumseh poured fuel on a fire already burning in Congress. A new generation of Republicans from the South and West — the war hawks, led by Henry Clay of Kentucky, the young Speaker of the House, and John C. Calhoun, a rising congressman from South Carolina — demanded war, and saw in it an opportunity to seize British Canada and Spanish Florida and drive every foreign power from the continent’s edges. In June 1812, with Britain still seizing ships and impressing sailors, Madison asked Congress for a declaration of war. The vote laid the country’s divisions bare: members from New Jersey northward, where the nation’s commerce and capital were concentrated, voted overwhelmingly against; the South and West voted for. The declaration passed the House 79 to 49 and the Senate 19 to 13 — the narrowest margin of any declaration of war in American history.[[49]] The nation entering the war was unprepared, poorly armed, and deeply divided, and it was nearly broke: Republicans had let the charter of Hamilton’s Bank of the United States expire in 1811, and Federalist bankers and merchants refused to lend money for a war they opposed. Before it was over, the government was close to bankruptcy. Its one great advantage was that Britain was fighting it with one hand, the other locked around Napoleon’s throat.
### The War
It began in disaster. Two American invasions of Canada collapsed, and the Royal Navy clamped a blockade on the coast that all but destroyed American commerce. New England governors refused to send their militias to fight. American privateers captured scores of British merchant vessels early on, but by 1813 British warships were raiding the coast at will. In August 1814 a British force sailed up the Chesapeake Bay, routed the militia defending the capital, and — in retaliation for the American burning of York, the capital of Upper Canada — set fire to the Capitol and the White House as the government fled. Dolley Madison stayed long enough to rescue art and relics from the president’s house before escaping herself. Then the tide turned at Baltimore. On the night of September 13–14, 1814, the garrison of Fort McHenry at the mouth of the harbor endured a British naval bombardment through the night and was still standing at dawn, its flag still flying. Watching from a ship in the British fleet, where he had been detained during a prisoner negotiation, a young Maryland lawyer named Francis Scott Key wrote the poem that became “The StarSpangled Banner.”[[50]]
### Two Fronts, One Verdict
Like the Revolution, the War of 1812 was fought against two enemies at once, and on the second front its consequences were decisive. In October 1813, at the Battle of the Thames in Upper Canada, an American army under Harrison defeated a British and Native force, and Tecumseh was killed in the fighting; with him died the last great attempt at a pan-Indian confederacy east of the Mississippi. In March 1814, at Horseshoe Bend in Alabama, an American force with Cherokee and Creek allies, led by a Tennessee general named Andrew Jackson, crushed the Red Stick Creeks — a battle and a cession the Jackson chapter recounts in full.[[51]] On the Canadian front the war ground to a stalemate, and in December 1814, at Ghent in Belgium, diplomats from two exhausted nations signed a treaty that restored the prewar status quo and said nothing about impressment or neutral rights. The ships carrying the news had not yet reached America when Jackson won his famous victory at New Orleans in January 1815. Americans did not care that the treaty had come first. They called the conflict the Second War of Independence, and they had a point: the republic had fought the world’s greatest power to a draw, survived its own bitter divisions without collapsing, and never again faced a serious British threat on its own soil. For the Native nations, the war meant something else. It broke their power in the Northwest and stripped those of the South of millions of acres. Never again would they be able to stop American expansion east of the Mississippi. The Second War of Independence was, for them, the war that ended theirs.
## Part V · The Era of Good Feelings
### Hartford
As the war dragged on, New England’s Federalists, out of power for three straight presidential elections and convinced the war was ruining them, gathered in convention at Hartford, Connecticut, in December 1814, “to lay the foundation for a radical reform in the National Compact.” A few delegates whispered about secession; most wanted to rewrite the Constitution to break the power of the South and the Virginia Dynasty. They proposed amendments to limit presidents to a single term and forbid two in a row from the same state; to require a two-thirds vote of Congress to admit new states, declare war, or restrict trade; and — most pointedly — to abolish the three-fifths clause, the provision of the constitutional armistice, recounted in Chapter 10, that had inflated southern power in Congress and the Electoral College for a generation.[[52]] The timing could not have been worse. The Hartford delegates had barely adjourned when news arrived of Jackson’s triumph at New Orleans, followed by word of the peace. In speeches and sermons across the country, Americans proclaimed that a divine hand had guided the nation to victory — and the Federalists, who had opposed the war and talked of secession while American soldiers fought, could never shake the charge that they had lacked patriotism in the nation’s hour of need. Within a few years the Federalist Party had vanished from national politics. Hartford was the occasion of its death, not the whole cause: the urban commercial and financial interests it championed were a small minority in an expanding agricultural nation, and its distrust of popular government put it ever more at odds with the democratic temper of the age.
### The Republicans Become Hamiltonians
The Federalists died; their ideas did not. The war had taught the victors hard lessons — about a nation that could not finance a war without a national bank, defend itself without a navy, or move an army without roads — and the Republican Party split along the lessons. A nationalist wing led by Henry Clay, the faction that would later call itself the National Republicans, now championed what Clay would call an “American System” of federal economic development. In 1816, with Clay’s backing from the Speaker’s chair, Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States, and James Madison — who as a congressman in 1791 had argued that the first bank was unconstitutional, and who now asked for a second — signed it into law.[[53]] The next year, a bill to create a national fund for roads and canals reached the president’s desk, sponsored by the most ardent nationalist in the House: John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, the same Calhoun the Jackson chapter will meet as the architect of nullification. On March 3, 1817, his last full day in office, Madison vetoed Calhoun’s Bonus Bill, reaffirming the old Republican principle that the federal government lacked the constitutional authority to fund internal improvements.[[54]] It was the last stand of strict construction, and it could not hold back the tide. The country was on the edge of an economic transformation — canals, steamboats, factories, a market reaching into every farmhouse — that would demand exactly the kind of federal energy the Federalists had welcomed and the Republicans had feared. One irony carries forward: in 1817 Calhoun wanted more federal power than the president would permit. Fifteen years later he would argue that a state could declare federal law void.
### The Last of the Virginia Dynasty
In 1816 James Monroe, the last of the Revolutionary generation to hold the presidency and the fourth Virginian in five, defeated the Federalist Rufus King with ease. By 1820 the Federalists could not field a candidate against him, and Monroe won reelection with all but one electoral vote. A Boston newspaper named the years of his presidency the Era of Good Feelings, and for the first and only time, the United States was effectively governed by a single party.[[55]] But the good feelings were thinner than they looked. Without two parties to organize the nation’s quarrels, politics did not disappear; it fractured along sectional lines, North against South against West, and the arguments that had once been contained by party discipline now ran raw. That fracture is the inheritance the Age of Jackson receives, when the single party splits four ways in 1824. Meanwhile, beyond the nation’s borders, a revolution was sweeping the hemisphere. Between 1810 and 1822 Spain’s American colonies rose in rebellion and established independent republics — Mexico, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, and others — until by 1825 Spain’s vast American empire had shrunk to Cuba and Puerto Rico. Americans saw their own revolution reflected in these, and in 1822 the Monroe administration became among the first governments in the world to recognize the new Latin American republics.[[56]]
### The Monroe Doctrine
The man who turned that sympathy into policy was Monroe’s secretary of state, John Quincy Adams, the son of the second president and the most experienced diplomat of his generation — he had traveled Europe as a boy on his father’s missions and had served as the American minister to the Netherlands, Prussia, and Russia. Adams feared that the reactionary monarchies of Europe might help Spain reconquer its lost colonies, or seize them for themselves — France had designs on Cuba, and Russia was pressing down the Pacific coast from Alaska. Adams drafted the key passage of Monroe’s annual message to Congress in December 1823, and it became known as the Monroe Doctrine.[[57]] Its premise was that the Old World and the New were fundamentally separate, and it laid down three principles: the Western Hemisphere was closed to further European colonization; the United States would stay out of Europe’s wars; and any European attempt to interfere with the independent nations of the Americas would be treated as a threat to the United States itself. In the words that open the doctrine:
“The American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers.”
— President James Monroe, Annual Message to Congress, December 2, 1823
At the time, the United States had no navy capable of enforcing a word of it; it was the British fleet, for its own reasons, that kept Europe out of the Americas. But the doctrine announced an identity. It has been called America’s diplomatic declaration of independence, and it completed the foreign policy Washington had sketched in his Farewell: no entanglements in Europe, and now, no European entanglements in America. Like Washington’s counsel, it would remain a cornerstone of American foreign policy for generations.[[58]]
### The Quarrel Ends
The distance from 1801 to 1823 was enormous. In 1801 the Republicans took power to undo the Federalist state: to pay off Hamilton’s debt, shrink Hamilton’s army and navy, abolish Hamilton’s taxes, and read the Constitution as narrowly as its words allowed. By 1823 they had doubled the nation by a constitutionally doubtful purchase, fought wars in the Mediterranean and on the continent, imposed the most sweeping economic regulation in the nation’s history, chartered Hamilton’s bank anew, and warned the empires of Europe off an entire hemisphere. The Federalist Party was dead. Its program — the strong national state Hamilton had imagined — was alive and in Republican hands. Neither founder won the argument outright; the nation absorbed both of them, and the result would have surprised each. Jefferson purchased the Louisiana Territory for $15 million, but it was the Native nations of the interior who paid the highest price. Their last united stand died with Tecumseh at the Thames, and Jefferson had already mapped their removal. And the one-party calm of the Era of Good Feelings was a lid on a boiling pot — sectional, economic, and over the institution that the Hartford delegates had tried, and failed, to put on the table. The founders’ generation was passing. In the next chapter, a new kind of politics and a new kind of hero would take the lid off.[[59]]
## Questions for Consideration
The chapter argues that the Republicans governed by “becoming, in power, much of what they had opposed.” Test that claim against the evidence — the Louisiana Purchase, the Barbary War, the Embargo, the Second Bank — and decide whether Jefferson and Madison abandoned their principles or discovered their limits.
Marshall’s decision in Marbury v. Madison gave Jefferson the outcome he wanted and the Supreme Court the power it wanted. Explain how both could be true, and why judicial review mattered more than the commission Marbury never received.
Jefferson called Native peoples “in body and mind equal to the white man” and planned their removal west of the Mississippi. Using the chapter’s evidence, explain how he held both positions — and evaluate whether the absence of a forced removal under his presidency changes the moral judgment of his policy.
Tecumseh and Cornplanter offered opposite strategies for Native survival in the same years. Compare them, and explain why the disappearance of the colonial “middle ground” made both strategies harder than any their grandparents had faced.
Van Ness and Pendleton watched the same duel and published accounts that contradict each other, and Hamilton left his own sealed statement of what he intended. Weigh all three against each other and against Ellis’s reconstruction: what does each second’s evidence prove, what does it only suggest, and what more would you need to know to decide who fired first — and why?
Americans called the War of 1812 the “Second War of Independence.” For whom was that label accurate, and for whom was it the opposite? Build your answer from both the nation’s gains and what they cost.
## Then and Now
The Republicans ran in 1800 against the jailing of editors, and the chapter’s opening page says so: the Alien and Sedition Acts had put freedom of the press at the center of the campaign. Then they won. In 1804 a Federalist editor named Harry Croswell stood trial in New York for printing the charge that President Jefferson had paid James Callender to attack Washington and Adams. The prosecution was a state one rather than a federal one, which is the whole point: the mechanism was different, so the principle was held not to apply. Alexander Hamilton argued the appeal on Croswell’s behalf, and the conviction was technically left standing. The man who had written in 1787 that he would rather have newspapers without a government than a government without newspapers was, as President, content to let a state do what he had denounced the federal government for doing.[[60]]
Test it. Is that hypocrisy, or is it what power does to anyone who holds it — and does the distinction between a federal prosecution and a state one carry the weight Jefferson rested on it? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a principle being defended right now by whoever happens to be out of power, and say what you would expect them to do with it once they are in.

St. Clair’s defeat, November 4, 1791, near present-day Fort Recovery, Ohio: of some 920 soldiers engaged, 632 died and 264 fell wounded, against perhaps sixty Native casualties; Little Turtle (Miami), Blue Jacket (Shawnee) and Buckongahelas (Delaware) led.
