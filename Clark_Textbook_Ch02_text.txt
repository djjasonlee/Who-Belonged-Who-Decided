CHAPTER 2
Contact and Exchange
Columbus, Conquest, and the Columbian Exchange, 1492–1650
“They could not move; they could not stir. … Covered, mantled with pustules, very many people died of them.”
— Nahua account of the smallpox epidemic at Tenochtitlán, 1520
This chapter sets the stage for the Age of Exploration and follows the consequences of Columbus’s voyages across two hemispheres. It asks what caused the Columbian Exchange and how it affected Europe and the Americas; how the growth of the Spanish Empire shaped the social and economic structures of the New World; and how European and Native American perceptions of one another shaped their strategies for negotiation, cooperation, and competition. Four claims organize it. All four answer a single question: who belonged in the world Spain was making, and who got to decide. The Columbian Exchange brought new crops, animals, and diseases to the New World, and it stimulated European social and economic growth, hastening the change from feudalism to capitalism. Spanish exploration and conquest were accompanied and enabled by epidemic disease that decimated indigenous populations, while the introduction of foreign crops and animals devastated native environments. The rapid death of native peoples forced European traders to seek an alternative supplier of human labor, which led to a partnership with West African kingdoms to bring enslaved Africans to the Caribbean and South America for plantation agriculture and mining. And the Spanish built a caste system to classify and stratify a population of European, African, and Native American peoples. That hierarchy of identity determined where each person stood and what power they held. The chapter closes where the historiography does: with the nations that survived the exchange and dictated terms to the empires that followed.
European nation-states — feudal, plague-scarred, and hardened by the centuries of brutal warfare and religious intolerance — are described in the Unit I opener. This chapter begins where it ends, at the landfall of October 1492.

### First Contact
First Contact: On October 12, 1492, the Santa María landed on San Salvador Island in the Bahamas, and the Spanish soon encountered the native Taíno, who embraced their visitors in the custom of exchanging gifts to establish amicable relationships. Columbus, unfamiliar with Native American customs of exchange and reciprocity, simply inquired of the Taíno where he could find more gold. His expedition continued to other Caribbean islands in search of Indian commodities until December 25, 1492, when his flagship hit a reef and began to take on water. Taíno onlookers rushed and canoed to the aid of the Spanish, ferrying men and supplies to Hispaniola, where Columbus’s crew built a temporary fort, aptly named La Navidad. Columbus returned to Spain with the remaining ships and crew, Taíno gold, exotic birds, and ten captive natives, leaving thirty-nine men behind. In his letter to the Spanish monarchs, he bragged of the quality of the land and the character of its people — and received, in return, funding for more men and ships.
Eleven months after leaving La Navidad, Columbus came back with 1,500 men — conquistadors and clergy — with livestock, and with sugarcane to farm. He found the settlement destroyed and the men gone. The Taíno explained that the Spanish had raped and murdered several of their people, and that multiple Taíno communities had aligned to vanquish them. Columbus settled a different part of the island, naming it La Isabela: two hundred small living quarters, gardens, quarters for the admiral, and fences. He returned again to Spain, leaving his younger brother in command, and in his absence the Spanish continued to abuse the Taíno, who combined forces again to retaliate. The Spanish had placed themselves in a paradox of survival: choosing to kill the people who sustained them.
Columbus was an excellent sailor, but an inept governor. In an attempt to satisfy Spanish desires for wealth and to fulfill his promise of converting native souls, he instituted a tribute and forced-labor regime that the Crown formalized under his successor: a royal cedula of December 20, 1503, authorized Governor Nicolás de Ovando to divide the Indians among the settlers, the repartimiento that became the encomienda — a form of tyranny that enslaved the Taíno and divided them among the conquistadors, forcing them to farm and to mine for gold. Brutal working conditions, disease, and Spanish cruelty devastated the Taíno; by 1518, smallpox had begun to ravage native villages. Conquistadors, animals (the pig and the sheep were especially destructive to the native environment), insects, and the spread of disease reduced the Taíno from a pre-contact population that scholars have put anywhere from a few hundred thousand to several million — the width of the range is itself a measure of how little the record allows — to some 60,000 by 1508 and about 32,000 by 1514: a collapse of eighty to ninety percent within a single generation. The impact was devastating to the indigenous people and their ecosystems, and extremely profitable for Spain, whose sugar and gold quickly transformed a struggling kingdom into a world power.

### From Exploration to Colonization
From Exploration to Colonization: What began as a Portuguese trading post on the West African coast would ultimately change the course of human civilization, as other European nations launched their own quests for economic and political supremacy. The Portuguese tried to keep their new sources of wealth hidden, but by the 1450s the quantities of African slaves and gold trickling into Mediterranean ports, along with reports from Genoese captains, leaked out. Castilian mariners found where the Portuguese were obtaining their riches, and several caravels sailed for the West African coast. In 1454, upon their return from Ghana, Portuguese pirates attacked the convoy and captured a vessel; King Enrique IV of Castile sent a scathing threat to the Portuguese monarch, claiming Castile’s “ancient and exclusive right to the Seas of Guinea.” By the principle of prior discovery, the Portuguese were legally entitled to the Guinea trade, but the dispute did not merit all-out war with Castile.
King Afonso V of Portugal wrote instead to Pope Nicholas V for the moral authority to monopolize the trade, and in the 1450s the ideological justification for these novel trading and slaving ventures came into full force. In 1455 Nicholas issued the bull Romanus Pontifex, declaring both Portugal’s divine right to its maritime monopoly and its legal authority over all Catholics within the territory granted. In essence, no other Catholic nation could sail toward the West African settlements for any reason, on pain of excommunication. With papal backing Portugal retained its monopoly of African slaves, sugar, and gold, while Spain remained engulfed in internal wars and conflict with Islam. Then, in 1492, Columbus’s voyage sent shockwaves through Europe, and through Portugal in particular: the tables had turned. In March 1493 Columbus wrote to the King and Queen of Spain informing them of the new lands, people, and goods he had discovered. Portugal’s King John II petitioned Pope Alexander VI to intervene on his behalf — but the pope, a native of Valencia and a friend of Spain’s King Ferdinand, responded with a bull of his own.

### Inter Caetera and the Doctrine of Discovery
Inter Caetera and the Doctrine of Discovery: Issued May 4, 1493, Inter Caetera supported Spain’s strategy to secure exclusive right to the lands Columbus had found. It established a demarcation line one hundred leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde Islands and assigned Spain the exclusive right to acquire territory and to trade in all lands west of that line; all others were forbidden to approach without a license from the rulers of Spain. This gave Spain a monopoly on the lands of the New World, and it became the religious and ideological justification for colonizing and converting all indigenous people. The bull declared that any land not inhabited by Christians was available to be “discovered,” claimed, and exploited by Christian rulers, “that the Catholic faith and the Christian religion be exalted and be everywhere increased and spread, that the health of souls be cared for and that barbarous nations be overthrown and brought to the faith itself.” Inter Caetera became the foundation of what later jurists would call the Doctrine of Discovery — the principle, written into American law by the Supreme Court in 1823, that Christian nations acquired title to lands held by non-Christians. In other words, the bull served to justify what would become a series of brutal acts upon the peoples of the New World, as Spanish, French, and Portuguese colonizers carried out colonization’s trinity of goals: gold, glory, and God.
The contention between Portugal and Spain was resolved the following year. On June 7, 1494, the two crowns signed the Treaty of Tordesillas, settling the possession of the newly discovered lands of the non-Christian world between them; the treaty moved the demarcation line to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, running pole to pole. The division gave the New World to Spain and Africa and India to Portugal — and, because the line cut through the eastern shoulder of South America, it gave Portugal Brazil.

### Europe Unsettled
Europe Unsettled: While Spain and Portugal plundered the New World, unrest plagued Europe. The Protestant Reformation threw England and France, the two powers capable of contesting Spain, into turmoil. Long and expensive conflicts drained time, resources, and lives; millions died from religious violence in France alone. As the violence diminished in Europe, religious and political rivalries continued in the New World. Spain’s exploitation of New Spain’s riches inspired other monarchs to invest in exploration and conquest, and reports of Spanish atrocities spread through Europe, providing a humanitarian justification for rival colonization. An English reprint of the writings of Bartolomé de las Casas bore the sensational title:
Popery Truly Display’d in its Bloody Colours: Or, a Faithful Narrative of the Horrid and Unexampled Massacres, Butcheries, and all manners of Cruelties that Hell and Malice could invent, committed by the Popish Spanish.
— English edition of Las Casas, 1689
In the early sixteenth century France launched its first exploratory voyages, seeking the fabled Northwest Passage. In 1524 Francis I sent the Italian-born Giovanni da Verrazzano to explore the coast between Florida and Newfoundland for a route to the Pacific; Verrazzano gave the names Francesca and Nova Gallia to the land between New Spain and English Newfoundland, promoting French interests. In 1534 Francis sent Jacques Cartier on the first of three voyages to the coast of Newfoundland and the St. Lawrence River. Cartier founded New France by planting a cross on the shore of the Gaspé Peninsula; he was the first European to travel inland in North America, describing the Gulf of St. Lawrence, naming “the Country of Canadas” after Iroquoian words, and claiming what is now Canada for France. His attempt at a permanent settlement at Cap-Rouge in 1541, with four hundred settlers, was abandoned the next year, and other French attempts failed through the rest of the century. By the first decade of the seventeenth century, however, the English had planted Jamestown (1607) and the French Quebec (1608), with the Dutch following at New Amsterdam in the 1620s. Five European nations had embarked upon their respective missions for economic prosperity and political power, ushering in a new era of world history.

### Mercantilism and Capitalism
Mercantilism and Capitalism: The arrival of the Spanish set in motion events that changed environments, populations, and economies. Before European exploration, nations operated under feudalism — an organized division of labor and social class in which serfs, peasants, and merchants all worked for the nobility in exchange for use of the land and protection, with no room for social mobility or self-improvement. With the discovery of new goods and products, and of new trading routes to reach them, European society began to embrace mercantilism: a government-regulated economic system that enhances state power through the export and sale of colonial commodities like sugar, tobacco, and rice. Governments were not the only sponsors of exploration. Private investors eager for American commodities formed joint-stock companies, which assumed the financial risk under royal permission and created still more opportunity for European men to reach wealth and power. The new economy came with a membership list: Europeans could own and trade, and Africans and Indians could be owned. Mercantilism settled a question of its own: a colony belonged to the Crown that chartered it, and its people, native or settler, existed solely to enrich that Crown. The colonization of the Americas not only enriched Spain; it blended two entirely separate worlds into one, mixing organisms that had been separated for millions of years. European animals, vegetation, insects, and disease transformed American landscapes and populations — a process the historian Alfred Crosby named the Columbian Exchange.

### The Atlantic Slave Trade
The Atlantic Slave Trade: The English word slave is the name of a European people. So many Slavs were carried to the markets of the medieval Mediterranean — trafficked out of the Black Sea by Venetian and Genoese dealers — that in language after language the ethnic name hardened into the common noun: sclavus, esclavo, slave. That is the first thing to understand about the Atlantic slave trade: it was not a novel practice. It was a brutal calibration of a system that had operated across the Old World for as long as anyone could remember, reaching from Spain to the eastern edge of the Ottoman Empire and fed by prisoners of war, debtors, and criminals. Slavery was a currency for pirates, a feature of justice systems, and a product of constant warfare. Muslim corsairs sold captured European sailors and merchants into Ottoman markets; the trans-Saharan caravans had been carrying enslaved Africans north across the desert for some seven centuries before the first Portuguese caravel worked down the African coast; and by the mid-1500s roughly one person in ten on the streets of Lisbon was enslaved.
The Atlantic version of the business announced itself at Lagos, Portugal, in August 1444, when 235 African captives — the first large human cargo carried to Europe by sea — were divided on the docks while the royal chronicler Zurara recorded the cries of separated families and Prince Henry, mounted, collected the crown’s fifth. Fifty years before Columbus sailed, Portugal had pioneered not slavery but its Atlantic supply line. And just as the French and Dutch would introduce nothing new to North America’s diplomacy except a demand that deformed it, Europe introduced nothing new to African slavery except an appetite the old system was never built to feed.
It matters what that old system was. In many of the societies the traders drew from, slavery was a condition with exits: enslaved people could marry free persons, hold property, and see their children absorbed into the lineage within a generation or two; bondage was rarely hereditary, and it was never racial. The New World changed the arithmetic. A status a person could leave became a status a person inherited; a traffic that ran in every direction ran in one; and a condition that had never been about the body was rewritten, statute by statute, as the permanent inheritance of Africans and their children — machinery a later chapter watches Virginia assemble piece by piece (see Chapter 5). West African kingdoms sold captives, usually prisoners of war, to European merchants for European manufactures and American goods, and a “triangle trade” formed connecting the Americas, Europe, and Africa: American raw materials and cash crops to Europe; textiles, technology, and guns to Africa; enslaved labor to the plantations of Spain and Portugal. The three-part diagram is tidier than the shipping ever was, but it is true to the cargoes.
As native populations became devastated by slave labor, murder, and disease, the conquistadors were forced to find another source of labor, and the Spanish conquest of the Canary Islands had opened their eyes to the African trade in which Portugal was already a key player. Following his victory over the Aztecs, Hernán Cortés purchased African slaves to labor on his sugar plantations, marking Spain’s official entry into the transatlantic trade. Between 1520 and 1640 some 530,000 enslaved Africans arrived in Spanish America directly from Africa — the great majority of the roughly 590,000 who had arrived there by 1700 — out of about 1.8 million landed in the Americas as a whole by that date, most of the remainder in Portuguese Brazil. Unlike the indigenous peoples, Africans carried greater resistance to Old World diseases, specifically to the malaria and yellow fever that flourished in plantation zones. Planters seized on this to argue that African labor was naturally suited to the work. In reality, enslaved Africans died in staggering numbers — what differed was not their capacity to suffer but the traders’ capacity to replace them — and it was that capacity, more than any immunity, that fixed the demand for African labor.

### Conquest
Conquest: Mexico and Peru: After brutally subduing the Arawaks and Taínos of Hispaniola, the Spanish probed the mainland for gold and slaves. In 1513 Juan Ponce de León explored the coast of Florida and gave the peninsula its name; in the same year Vasco Núñez de Balboa crossed the Isthmus of Darién and became the first European to see the Pacific. Rumors of rich Indian kingdoms drew other Spaniards, hardened veterans of the reconquista among them, to invade the mainland, and the monarchs offered successful conquistadors noble titles, vast estates, and Indian laborers. With these inducements before him, in 1519 Hernán Cortés led an army of six hundred men to the Yucatán Peninsula. Gathering allies among native peoples who chafed under Aztec rule, he marched on Tenochtitlán and challenged its ruler, Moctezuma, who received him with great ceremony; Cortés soon took the emperor captive, and after a prolonged siege he and his men captured the city. Those allies made the conquest possible. The most important were the Tlaxcalans, an independent people the Mexica had never subdued, who fought the Spaniards in 1519, then joined them, and sent tens of thousands of warriors to the siege. The Mexica had built an empire of tributaries, deciding city by city who owed them allegiance, extracting from them payment in the form of both goods and human capital. Cortés arrived with an army, but he also found a network of native rivals, whose grievances he could weaponize into an alliance. Just as decisive was a single woman: Malintzin — Doña Marina to the Spanish, La Malinche to later generations — a Nahua woman enslaved among the Maya, one of twenty enslaved women given to the Spaniards at Potonchán in 1519. Fluent in Nahuatl and a Maya language, she became Cortés’s interpreter and adviser, the voice through which he spoke to Moctezuma, and later the mother of his son Martín, one of the first mestizos of New Spain. The Spanish had a silent and invisible ally: disease. Separated from Eurasia for thousands of years, the inhabitants of the Americas had no prior exposure to common European diseases, and after the Spaniards arrived a massive smallpox epidemic ravaged Tenochtitlán, “striking everywhere in the city,” according to a Nahua source, and killing Moctezuma’s brother and thousands more. Subsequent outbreaks of smallpox, influenza, and measles killed hundreds of thousands and sapped the survivors’ morale.
Exploiting this demographic weakness, Cortés quickly extended Spanish rule over the Aztec Empire, and his lieutenants moved against the Maya city-states of the Yucatán. In Peru, Francisco Pizarro’s first exploratory expedition of 1524 failed; it was his third, departing in 1531, that reached Cajamarca in 1532 with about 168 men and 62 horses — by which time half the Inca population had already died of European diseases. Weakened militarily and divided between rival claimants to the throne, the Inca nobility was easy prey. Pizarro killed Atahualpa, the reigning Inca, and seized his enormous wealth; although Inca resistance continued for a generation — the Neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba held out until 1572 — Spain was master of the wealthiest and most populous regions of the Western Hemisphere. The Spanish invasion changed life forever in the Americas. Disease and warfare wiped out virtually all of the Indians of Hispaniola — at least 300,000 people. In Peru, a population of nine million in 1530 plummeted to fewer than 500,000 a century later. Mesoamerica suffered the greatest losses: in one of the great demographic disasters in world history, its population of twenty million in 1500 had dwindled to three million by 1650.

### The Encomienda, the Requerimiento, and Las Casas
The Encomienda, the Requerimiento, and Las Casas: Among the first policies of New Spain was the encomienda, used to replace feudalism by seizing native lands and enslaving native peoples for mining, building, farming, and conversion in exchange for “protection.” The indigenous were systematically rounded up and presented with the Requerimiento, an official proclamation read aloud as an ultimatum to conquered Indians. It asserted the religious authority of the pope over the entire earth and the political authority of Spain over the Americas, and it demanded that the conquered accept Spanish rule and Christian preaching or risk subjugation, enslavement, and death. Often it was read in Latin with no interpreters present, or delivered from shipboard to an empty beach — revealing its prime purpose as self-justification for the invaders. Encomenderos forced tribal leaders to comply, but most quickly recognized the brutality of Spanish slavery and either fled into hiding or mounted feeble efforts to fight; indigenous slaves continually failed to adhere to working conditions, and many ran away. And according to Spanish law, baptized natives could not be enslaved. Baptism thus became a test of belonging, but the men who controlled their labor also controlled who the law protected.
Despite that legal caveat, the conquerors enslaved large groups of the newly encountered peoples and worked many of them to death. In 1502 Bartolomé de las Casas arrived in Hispaniola and soon became a land and slave owner, joining military expeditions against the native peoples and taking holy orders in 1510. After his participation in the violent invasion of Cuba in 1513, however, he came to view European interference in native affairs as illegal and immoral. His petitions began in May 1515 and continued until his death in 1566, as he cajoled, shamed, and begged the Crown to end its practices of invasion and enslavement, documenting the cruelty in A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies. In 1542, with his help, the Crown issued the Leyes Nuevas, the New Laws, which banned the encomienda — on paper. The Spanish pattern of conquest became known as the Black Legend, due in large part to his writings, and it was not long before tales of Spanish cruelty spread through Europe, prompting other nations both to seek similar fortunes and to counter Spanish brutality with more humanitarian missions to pacify and enlighten the Indians. As later chapters will show, the brutalizing of indigenous peoples in the name of God would motivate other nations to seek alternative methods of colonization — and would later serve as a justification for American imperialism and westward expansion under the name of Manifest Destiny.

### The Valladolid Debate
The Valladolid Debate: The argument Las Casas had been making for thirty-five years finally got its hearing. In April 1550 Charles V halted further conquests in the Americas until a panel of theologians and jurists could judge whether they were just. The junta sat at Valladolid, then the seat of the royal court, and on August 15, 1550, the humanist Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda opened with a three-hour oration. Sepúlveda argued from Aristotle’s doctrine of natural slavery, which held that some peoples are born to be ruled. The Indians, he wrote, were “as inferior to the Spaniards as children to adults, women to men” — homunculi, half-men, “in whom you will scarcely find even vestiges of humanity.” War against them was therefore just, and their subjection was for their own good.
Las Casas answered with a lifetime of evidence. The Indians, he argued, were fully rational peoples who built cities, kept laws, and governed themselves; he went so far as to defend even human sacrifice, the Spaniards’ favorite proof of barbarism, as devotion misdirected rather than absent. A people capable of reason could be brought to Christianity only by persuasion, never by the sword. The judges heard both men, argued among themselves, and dispersed without issuing a collective decision. What the debate revealed is what the two men shared: both assumed the Indians must become Christians. The quarrel was over the method — conversion by persuasion or by conquest — not over whether Spain had the right to bring the faith at all.
Las Casas’s own record was not clean. In his 1516 Memorial de remedios para las Indias, pleading for relief for Native laborers, he proposed that the colonists be licensed to import enslaved Africans in their place. Years later, writing of himself in the third person in his Historia de las Indias, he recorded that “the cleric, many years later, regretted the advice he gave the king on this matter — he judged himself culpable through inadvertence — when he saw proven that the enslavement of blacks was every bit as unjust as that of the Indians.” The great defender of the Indians had been, for a time, an advocate of the African trade, and the first to say so was Las Casas himself.

### New Spain and the Caste System
New Spain and the Caste System: The massive new territory was divided into two viceroyalties, Peru and New Spain — the latter including the Caribbean, Mexico, and the American Southwest and Southeast — and Philip II appointed viceroys to rule each in the exact manner of the monarch, primarily to impose order. With only twelve outposts and some 250,000 Spaniards immigrating from Spain, the viceroyalties operated with remarkable efficiency: by 1650 the Spanish had planted more than two hundred towns, and registered shipments between 1500 and 1650 carried 181 tons of gold and 16,000 tons of silver from America to Seville — the recorded flow only; contraband, and the silver that stayed in America or crossed the Pacific to Asia, went uncounted. The rapid expansion of Spanish presence and wealth proceeded in three phases — brutal treatment and eradication of native culture, enslavement, and conversion to Christianity. But arguably the most effective of all methods was the unforeseen consequence of foreign disease.
By the mid-sixteenth century New Spain had become a heterogeneous population of Spaniards, Native Americans, and Africans, and Spanish officials created a hierarchy of classes to separate themselves from the rest: a caste system of race, ranked by the amount of Spanish blood each person carried. The more Spanish blood, the higher the rank; the less, the lower. It was far more than an order of classes. Each caste carried strict rules governing wealth, career, attire, and even permitted friendships, placing an effectual ceiling on one’s ability to rise. At the top stood the peninsulares, Spaniards born in Spain — the conquistadors like Cortés and Pizarro, and the men who held the most important offices in government, church, and army. Next came the criollos, pure-blooded Spaniards born in the Americas; then the mestizos, of mixed Spanish and Indigenous descent. At the bottom were full-blooded Native Americans and Africans.

### The Columbian Exchange
The Columbian Exchange: The transfer of European and American animals, foods, and people created a seismic shift in indigenous environmental and social systems. It also brought new foods, organisms, and wealth to Europe, producing social and economic change as Europe embraced mercantilism and eventually capitalism. The exchange had devastating and lasting effects: European diseases and the hunger for labor-intensive cash crops overwhelmed native peoples, who succumbed to disease and exposure, and the shortage of native labor produced merchant partnerships with West African slavers. The exchange brought incredible wealth and natural resources to Europe, the adoption of capitalism, and opportunity for nations previously plagued by starvation and unemployment; European animals, plants, insects, and disease had a significant and detrimental impact on indigenous environments and people. The triangle trade eventually reached Asian markets, usually for Spanish silver, and created the first global trade network.
The consequences reach to the present. Following the split of the prehistoric supercontinent Pangaea, two separate worlds evolved over millions of years, each developing countless organisms unique to its environment, and human civilizations everywhere had different natural resources that challenged and facilitated their growth. Only with the arrival of the Spanish did the cultural and biological landscape begin to be transformed. The transfer of native plants, animals, diseases, and knowledge to non-native lands produced significant changes, beneficial and detrimental, on every continent. The Columbian Exchange essentially created the societal and biological landscapes we live in today. It also set the terms of membership for the centuries that followed: Africans in the New World remained on the periphery of European societies. It is the greatest geographic story in history.

### Biological Impact
Biological Impact: The vast majority of the foods and spices enjoyed today originated thousands of miles from where they are eaten, and the introduction of new crops transformed societies. The tomato was first domesticated in Mexico, the potato in the Andes, and the cacao behind chocolate in the American tropics. Italy’s tomato sauce is younger than Columbus’s voyages by two centuries — the first printed recipe, in a Neapolitan cookbook of 1692, is called “in the Spanish style” — and Florida’s oranges came west from Asia by way of Spain. The American potato, once limited to the wealthy aristocracy of Europe, was coveted and eventually stolen by lower-class farmers and spread across the continent. The result was a significant increase in caloric intake and nutrition, and a boom in population wherever potato cultivation spread: it stood as a barrier against seasonal famine, crop failure, and the diseases of malnutrition. The Irish population doubled, from about four million in 1780 to 8.2 million at the census of 1841, with no other improvement in agricultural technique or infrastructure to explain it but the potato. Native tobacco, used primarily for medicine and ritual, quickly became the first global drug pandemic. By 1614, one contemporary reckoned, London had more than 7,000 tobacco houses; tobacco became a cash crop and the primary revenue of the English joint-stock companies, and its profits encouraged further English settlement and colonization of North America. After reaching China, competitive gangs formed to control its distribution and profit. Recreational smoking spread through Europe, the United States, Asia, and the Middle East. Today more than a billion people smoke tobacco worldwide — and tobacco-related illness, frequently cited as the leading cause of death in modern society, kills more than 480,000 people a year in the United States alone.
While Europe reaped social and biological benefits from American foods and raw materials, European crops and animals had mostly negative consequences for indigenous peoples and environments. In 1493, during Columbus’s second voyage, horses were introduced to Hispaniola; in 1519 to the mainland. Horses were gradually traded to native populations by the Spanish, and many escaped and went feral; the horse transformed entire native societies by providing faster transportation, more efficient hunting, and superior warfare. The horse filled a gap the Americas had never been able to fill. The hemisphere’s domesticated animals were the dog, the turkey, the Muscovy duck, the guinea pig, and, in the Andes, the llama and alpaca — the only large animals among them, and none able to pull a plow or carry a rider. Jared Diamond’s Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) built a theory of world history on that imbalance: Eurasia’s wealth of large domesticable animals gave its peoples draft power, cavalry, and, through millennia of close contact with herds, the very diseases that would cross the Atlantic. Historians still debate how much geography explains, but the asymmetry itself is not in dispute. The smallest of the European arrivals had the greatest impact: the smallpox virus. Without knowledge or intent, Europeans brought pathogens common only in the Old World, and smallpox, malaria, and yellow fever devastated natives and Europeans alike throughout the colonization process.
Smallpox, measles, and influenza inflicted losses no army could have. Mortality varied enormously by region — coastal New England lost perhaps nine in ten to the epidemics of 1616–19, and Mesoamerica fell from twenty million to three by 1650, while other regions suffered less, and later. But staggering loss did not translate simply into lost power. Native institutions were built to absorb death: mourning-war and adoption customs converted captives into kin, rebuilding populations and preserving political strength even as epidemics raged. For decades, European negotiators faced nations that had buried most of their people and still dictated terms. What disease set in motion was not the end of native power but its transformation — a story taken up in the next chapter. Unlike the indigenous and the Europeans, meanwhile, slaves from West Africa carried resistance to the diseases of the Old World, which led to a decrease in indentured servants and an increase in the importation of the enslaved.

### Human Consequences
Human Consequences: The introduction of native crops and commodities significantly increased food availability and nutrition, raising European populations and bringing more political stability as new nation-states came to power. But just as the transfer of European crops, animals, and disease was unbalanced, so the Americas and Africa suffered catastrophic human loss. Smallpox and malaria wiped out the indigenous labor force, pushing the Spanish into alliance with West African slavers to trade captives for European manufactures; the labor-intensive sugar plantations of the Caribbean and South America demanded workers from similar climates, and as natives and Europeans died of exposure and disease, Africans filled the void with increasing frequency. Historians and anthropologists estimate that between ten and fifteen million West and Central Africans were captured, enslaved, and brought to the New World. The African diaspora enriched European economic systems while weakening the economic and political systems of Africa, and Africans in the New World stood at the center of the societies Europeans built there while being denied standing in them — at the bottom of the Spanish caste system, and inside the chattel slavery of North America that a later chapter examines.

### How the Story Got Told
How the Story Got Told: For most of American history the continent’s first peoples remained on the periphery of the more important narrative of Euro-American progress. Thanks to the writers of the nineteenth century, the Vanishing Indian became a common character in a story from which he was destined to disappear, whether by assimilation into the broader society or by eradication — marginal to a grand narrative dominated by assumptions of Manifest Destiny and governed by the powerful teleology of American nationhood. In the 1970s, as America began its moral transition away from a past marked by racism, sexism, and xenophobia, scholars began to change the way Native Americans had been portrayed. Starting with the first encounters between indigenous people and Europeans, historians presented a drastically different view: instead of savage, subhuman, and primitive, they saw noble, intelligent, and powerful. Many argued that, if not for the devastating impact of foreign disease and the inability of native nations to align themselves, European colonization would have taken significantly longer and cost significantly more. And historians now understand the complexity of Native American cultures and their successful establishment of a “Middle Ground,” from which they maintained power and autonomy for decades. Through clever negotiation, alliance, and warfare, native nations served as a constant reminder to European colonizers of how close they were to becoming conquered themselves.

### Complex Characters
Complex Characters: Christopher Columbus: On October 12, 1492, the day he came ashore, Columbus wrote the first of two things about the Taíno in his journal. He called them gentle and intelligent, a people who “would easily be made Christians.” Two days later he added that “with fifty men they can all be subjugated and made to do what is required of them.” The admiration and the threat sit two days apart, and the rest of his career played out between them.
The sailor deserves his reputation. The Norse had reached Newfoundland five centuries earlier and left; Columbus crossed the open Atlantic, found his way back, and did it four times, working out the wind system that carried ships west on the trade winds and home on the westerlies. The governor earned his reputation too. When the gold tribute failed to produce gold, people became the dividend. In February 1495 he shipped about 500 Taíno to Spain to be sold as slaves, and roughly 200 died on the crossing and went over the side. Enslaving captives taken in war counted as ordinary practice in the Mediterranean world he came from, and the Portuguese had traded enslaved Africans for half a century. Even so, Queen Isabella ordered captive Indians freed and returned in 1500, a sign that his own sovereigns drew a line he had crossed. The Crown sent Francisco de Bobadilla to investigate the colony that same year, and Columbus came home in chains. Ferdinand and Isabella released him, but they never gave him back the governorship.
The strangest episode in his record explains the rest. On June 12, 1494, after six weeks sailing along the coast of Cuba without reaching its western tip, Columbus had his crew swear before a notary that Cuba formed part of the Asian mainland and not an island. The penalty scaled to the man: an officer who later said otherwise faced a fine of 10,000 maravedís, a common sailor a hundred lashes and the loss of his tongue. The Taíno had told him on the first voyage that Cuba is an island. He sat a short sail from the point where he could have seen it for himself, and he turned around instead. He died on May 20, 1506, still insisting he had reached Asia, having never set foot on the North American mainland.
The likeliest explanation for the oath lies in what Columbus believed about himself. He believed his voyages fulfilled prophecy. His Book of Prophecies, compiled in 1501 and 1502, argued that scripture had foretold them: the gospel carried to every nation, Jerusalem recovered, the gold of the Indies paying for a last crusade. If God had ordained the enterprise, then the means served God, and the evidence had to fit the prophecy rather than the other way around. Cuba had to be Asia, the gold had to be there, and the colony had to be succeeding. When the evidence disagreed, Columbus made his crew swear the conclusion.
The counterweight belongs on the same page. The seamanship and the catastrophe came from the same skill, because the man who could read the winds could reach the islands. In February 1504, stranded on Jamaica, he used an almanac to predict a lunar eclipse and warned the local cacique that his God would darken the moon unless the Spaniards received food. Brilliant astronomy, used as a threat. The great dying on Hispaniola came from disease, forced labor, and hunger together, with disease the largest share by modern estimates, and he neither understood the microbes nor intended the epidemics. In addition, the encounter would have happened without him; in 1500 a Portuguese fleet bound for India swung west and struck Brazil, by accident in the usual telling. The encomienda, the epidemics, and the extraction did not depend on Columbus. The first decade did, and that decade ran under his command.
The admiring entry and the threatening one belong to the same man, and so does everything that followed.

### Columbus, Reconsidered
Columbus, Reconsidered: The reckoning eventually reached the admiral himself. In the twenty-first century his statues came down across the Americas, and a growing number of states and cities renamed or paired the holiday as Indigenous Peoples’ Day. That argument offers a crude dichotomy — hero or villain — and history needs more than that: nuance, complexity, and a diversification of viewpoints are crucial to understanding it. The theme runs through the chapters ahead, with Jefferson, who wrote that all men are created equal while enslaving more than six hundred people over his lifetime, and later with Lincoln. Columbus was not the construction-paper hero we learned about in elementary school, and he was not the sole author of a catastrophe that thousands of conquistadors, three centuries of imperial policy, and a hemisphere’s worth of microbes carried out. He answers for what happened under his own command on Hispaniola, and that record is damning enough without making him the author of all of it. Nor do the peoples who suffered the conquest need to be remembered as innocents of empire to be remembered as its victims: the Mexica had built an empire of their own, which is why Tlaxcala marched with Cortés. The distinction that matters is between presentism, which judges the past by the standards of the present and congratulates itself on the verdict, and historical understanding, which rebuilds the world a person acted in without excusing what he did there. Understanding is not condoning. It is the only way to learn how people who were not monsters did monstrous things, and the only lesson that might keep us from repeating them.

## Then and Now

In 2020 a virus crossed the world in weeks along the same routes that carried its goods, its travelers, and its ideas, and the costs did not fall evenly — they followed work, housing, age, and access to care. The Spanish brought with them a multitude of novel fauna, flora, and technologies to the New World, but the one thing they could not share proved to be the most consequential: their immunity to smallpox. This chapter’s own figures show what that cost, and war and forced labor decided how much higher it ran.
