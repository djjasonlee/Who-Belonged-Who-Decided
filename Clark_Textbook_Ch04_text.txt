CHAPTER 4
Founding and Conflict
The Chesapeake and New England, 1607–1676
“They said they had been the first in doing good to the English, and the English the first in doing wrong.”
— Metacom and his counselors to John Easton, deputy governor of Rhode Island, June 1675, as Easton recorded it
Across New England and the Chesapeake, the English built their societies around the pursuit of land. To an Englishman of 1600, property meant more than a farm of his own. Land ownership stood as proof of individual worth, wealth, and achievement, and it opened the doors closed to men without it: the vote, the jury box, the militia, a place in the front pews. However, the land that English settlers coveted for themselves belonged to the Indigenous peoples already living on it. Who could claim it, who could control it, and who held the authority to decide became questions that defined the future of both regions. In both regions, English survival in the first years depended upon Native food, Native knowledge, and Native alliances. As the colonies matured, that dependence took a new form: the English no longer needed help from their hosts, nor the hosts themselves, only their land. The chapter builds on the previous one, tracing how interactions among British colonies, European rivals, and Native Americans created alliances and fueled military conflict. As the demand for Native land increased, Native communities faced mounting pressure to balance the opportunities of trade and prosperity against the need to preserve their cultures and homelands. Greater proximity brought more frequent and often more hostile encounters, fueling English aggression and Indigenous resistance. Native adaptation to English commodities, the depletion of natural resources, and military conflict all carried increasingly severe consequences for Native communities — consequences that arrived here a full century before they reached the Great Lakes. In both regions, land became the ultimate qualification for belonging: who could claim it, who could own it, who could be driven from it, and by whose authority.

### England, 1600
England, 1600: The Americas of 1500–1600 belonged to Spain: native populations decimated by as much as ninety percent in places, several hundred thousand enslaved Africans working the sugar plantations of the Caribbean, Mexico, and Brazil, galleons of gold and silver flowing home. North America remained largely unexplored and unclaimed. England, meanwhile, was in social crisis. Between 1550 and 1600, England’s population rose from approximately three million to four million, and more families competed for less land. For generations English peasants had held a secure grip on their plots of land; now landlords seeking profit in the expanding woolen trade raised sheep instead, evicting farmers and the “commoners” who occupied their lands. This enclosure movement forced thousands of displaced people into the cities, resulting in a cascade of declining wages, housing, and health. The Crown answered with punishment. The Elizabethan clergyman William Harrison reported that “rogues and vagabonds are often stocked and whipped,” and that in an ordinary year three or four hundred of them went to the gallows. In 1553 London turned Bridewell, a former royal palace, into the country’s first house of correction, where officials flogged vagrants on admission and set them to hard labor. English promoters of colonization also proposed shipping the unruly poor to the New World, where the laboring classes might acquire property of their own. By Elizabeth’s reign, then, North American colonization could both remedy England’s social crisis and provide new wealth, and the crown, unable to subsidize large ventures, allowed privately funded joint-stock companies to explore the Atlantic coast. Elizabeth also authorized the invasion and colonization of Ireland, whose people the English viewed as “dirty, lazy, and pagan savages”; the English military engaged there in total war — intense violence, the indiscriminate killing of Irish people, the destruction of Irish communities — and the pattern of conquest established in Ireland would later serve as the military blueprint for removing Native Americans from coveted lands. The English had decided who counted as a savage before they met one in America.

### Jamestown, 1607
Jamestown, 1607: During the early 1500s the Spanish and French had explored the mid-Atlantic seaboard and found it too cold for sugar and too warm for the best furs; the Spanish stayed in Florida to protect their shipping routes, the French settled the Canadian interior, and England, after the failure of its get-rich-quick schemes, chose agriculture. Because England could not afford a massive colonizing effort, wealthy members of the aristocracy pooled their money in joint-stock companies, and in 1606 the Virginia Company sent three ships and 144 men — mostly gentry and adventurers — to the Chesapeake, hoping to find gold and a passage to the Indies. Aware of the notorious cruelty of the Spanish, the colonizers wrongly assumed native people would respond favorably to friendlier policies and would desire to become English. The Chesapeake is a fertile series of peninsulas and navigable rivers; the Company’s leaders chose to settle a low, swampy, mosquito-infested peninsula for its defensible location. Natives had avoided it for a reason. It sat in the middle of the Powhatan Confederation, thirty tribes strong. The colonists bickered about labor, refused to work with their hands or to farm, looked for gold to pay back their investors, had no immunity to malaria, brought no women to manage domestic life, and relied upon natives for food. In June, Captain Christopher Newport sailed two of the ships home with their crews, leaving the Discovery and 104 colonists behind. Only thirty-eight of them survived the first winter. John Smith, considered socially inferior by the gentry, assumed command and sought a relationship with the Powhatan; he led men into the interior, was attacked, and — as the story of Pocahontas has it — was spared and negotiated for food. Suffering from starvation and native attacks, Smith took charge of the dying colony by threatening those who refused to labor: “He that will not work, shall not eat.”
In 1609 nine more ships and six hundred people arrived, and the colonists suffered more. Natives killed and tortured wandering settlers. The winter of 1609–10, the “starving time,” forced colonists to eat dogs, cats, rats, and the dead; one man killed and ate his wife and was burned at the stake. Smith, credited with prolonging the colony’s survival, was resented by the gentry for his autocratic style and his common birth, and in 1609, badly burned by a malfunctioning musket, he was forced to return to England. The new governor increased violence against the Powhatan and failed to organize the settlers as Smith had; colonists preferred exploring for gold to farming and wrongly assumed the natives would feed them. Severe drought forced the Powhatan to keep only enough food to sustain themselves, and starving colonists raided native food supplies, resulting in the systematic torture and killing of wandering English. In 1610 Lord De La Warr arrived — familiar with English warfare against the Irish — and changed the goals and philosophy of the colony toward the natives, opting for total war. Chief Powhatan had viewed the English as another tribe he could manipulate for their attractive commodities — beads, glass, guns — supplying them just enough to obtain what he wanted rather than eliminating them; in 1610 settlers attacked a village, stealing food and destroying homes, and tensions escalated, but Powhatan refused to issue an extermination order, thinking he could still profit from English goods. The stalemate broke when Pocahontas was captured in 1613, forcing Powhatan to declare peace. Living in Jamestown, she learned English, married the tobacco baron John Rolfe in 1614, and was taken to England by the Virginia Company, which was eager to show off her apparent conversion to English ways to attract investors. She died there in 1617. The next spring Powhatan died, at about seventy, leaving his brother in charge — a brother who desired the annihilation of the English.

### Tobacco
Tobacco: John Rolfe experimented with Caribbean and Virginian strains of tobacco and produced a high-quality product ready for sale. Tobacco finally made Jamestown profitable and transformed the colony into a destination for the wealthy and the poor alike. Labeled a “noxious weed” by James I, it was coveted across Europe and became the first cash crop in America — by 1614 a contemporary counted more than seven thousand tobacco houses in London — and tobacco became Virginia’s substitute for gold, enriching an emerging planter class and the colonial officials who granted themselves the best land, while the crown profited from customs duties. In 1624, the Virginia colony produced more than 200,000 pounds; by 1664, the crop totaled fifteen million pounds, doubling again by the 1680s. Tobacco planters spread out across the countryside, leaving Virginia with few towns and little unity; they chased quick fortunes and scrambled frantically for land and labor. By mid-century a new influx of well-financed immigrants — the sons of merchants and English gentlemen — had used the headright system and government connections to acquire large estates, and Jamestown lured thousands of indentured servants, who exchanged free passage for seven years of service and were promised land and “full rights of Englishmen” upon finishing out the contract. John Smith, writing of New England in 1616, had already put the offer in a sentence: in America, “every man may be master and owner of his own labour and land.” The Company extended that offer to Englishmen and to no one else. By 1630 the colony was eighty percent young single men laboring in the tobacco fields. The climate proved deadly for new arrivals, who faced malaria brought unintentionally from England; mortality was so high that the trading companies warned planters their laborers needed a year of “seasoning” before full work. Jamestown served a dual purpose — a place for England to send its poor and malcontents, and an economic venture — and the result was a highly stratified population of planters, merchants, laborers, and servants spread across semi-independent plantation communities. England sent no clergy, hoping to convert native people by assimilating them.
To attract immigrants and maintain a population that high mortality and few women could not sustain, the Virginia Company allowed settlers to own land, granting fifty acres to every freeman who paid his own passage and fifty more for each servant he imported, and it created the first system of representative government in the New World: the House of Burgesses, first convened in 1619, which could make laws and levy taxes subject to the veto of the governor and the Company council in England. Politics was run exclusively by the wealthy with connections in London. By 1622 landownership, self-government, and a judicial system based on “the lawes of the realme of England” had attracted some 4,500 recruits, and the Company recruited “Maides young and uncorrupt to make wifes to the Inhabitants” — several dozen “tobacco brides” arrived in 1620 and 1621 — though men outnumbered women in the Chesapeake by four or five to one for most of the century. And in 1619 an English privateer landed twenty Africans at Jamestown: the beginning of a story that a later chapter tells in full. Virginia had already written the terms of servitude into law, but it took another forty-three years to define the legal status of the twenty.

### The Powhatan Wars
The Powhatan Wars: The successful cultivation and export of tobacco had created a lucrative transatlantic trade and supplied a solution to English social problems, and the influx of servants and planters drove demand for native land. Unlike his brother, who had wanted to incorporate the English into his empire, Opechancanough disliked the colonists and viewed them as a growing threat. By 1620 the colonists had acquired more land, often through intimidation and manipulation. Opechancanough knew he could not face English military superiority — guns, and ships that could sail the length of the Chesapeake — so he complied with English demands, lulled the settlers into complacency, illegally acquired guns from eager settlers, and built alliances with the English against smaller nations of the confederacy. On the morning of March 22, 1622, at settlements and plantations along the James from present-day Richmond to Hampton Roads, Indians from the Powhatan confederacy approached the colonists in a friendly fashion, weapons hidden, and at the prearranged time picked up whatever tools or weapons were at hand. The coordinated attack was unprecedented in its brutality; according to Edward Waterhouse, the Powhatans did not distinguish between man and woman, adult and child. That disregard for age or sex was almost certainly an innovation learned from the English — the Powhatan traditionally killed only adult male enemies while abducting or adopting women and children — and George Percy, acting president of the council in Virginia, had recorded a 1610 incident in which English soldiers executed captured Paspahegh children by throwing them into the James River and shooting at their bobbing heads. Yet Jamestown itself was left untouched, which lends weight to the view that Opechancanough’s goal was not the extermination of every European but a flexing of native superiority; the attack would have been deadlier had a Powhatan not warned several colonists hours before. It claimed approximately 347 colonists — a quarter of the settler population of 1,200 — and came perilously close to extinguishing England’s most promising outpost in North America.
Before the attack, the English had sworn never to replicate the inhumane actions of the cruel Spanish conquistadors; England would gain its foothold not through swords and muskets but through “faire and loving meanes fitting to our English natures.” Unlike the Spanish, who had used intertribal rivalries to build large alliances against the Aztec and Inca, the English were surrounded by a unified confederacy that made such alliances nearly impossible. After the attack, in typical native fashion, the Powhatans retreated into a defensive position to deny the settlers any access to outside food and water, and for two months more settlers died of starvation and disease. Then, sensing an opportunity for truce, Opechancanough sent emissaries inviting the English to a ceremonial peace that would let both sides plant, and offering to return the surviving English prisoners. One emissary was the same man many narratives credit with saving Jamestown by his warning; the other was recognized as a participant in the attack and put in chains. Sir Francis Wyatt, the newly appointed governor, pretended to accept the terms — seeing plainly that if the Powhatans planted their corn, he would know where and when to reap it for Virginia’s storehouses — and sent a colonial party to Pamunkey with casks of wine to toast the negotiations. The wine meant for the Powhatans was poisoned; the English made a show of drinking first from a hidden, untainted cask. An estimated two hundred Indians died of the poison or the musket balls the peace party fired into them, and Opechancanough was reported both poisoned and shot, “it beinge impossible for him to escape.” He did not die, but his name vanishes from the record for several years. The Virginia Company’s advice to Wyatt included an injunction to “roote out from being any longer a people” the Powhatans who had so violently rejected English culture and faith. Jamestown militias enacted a series of raids mimicking the total-war tactics used against the Irish. In 1624 some eight hundred Pamunkey warriors fought sixty English musketeers for two days and lost, and the English burned corn enough, they boasted, to feed four thousand men for a year; the war ground on until Opechancanough made peace in 1632. The uprising was also the last of a series of blows to the Virginia Company: in 1624 the Crown revoked its charter and took Virginia as England’s first royal colony, with a governor named by the king. The Company had sent some 6,000 settlers across the Atlantic; about 1,200 were alive in the colony when the king took control. For the next twenty years, as more Englishmen arrived with more weapons, the militias no longer sought peaceful relations; the 1622 attack, which the English classified as an act of terrorism, forever changed English attitudes and marked the beginning of a policy of warfare and extermination. As the English grew in strength, most nations of the confederacy switched their allegiance to them. Opechancanough struck once more in 1644, and in 1646, aged and nearly blind, he was captured and shot by a guard while in English custody, ending the Third Anglo-Powhatan War.
The uprising destroyed English hopes of assimilating Native Americans into Virginia’s social order; after the second war, all hope of an interracial society vanished. Mistrust of Native Americans dominated Anglo-American thinking after 1622, inspiring a program of almost total separation and incessant enmity, and the uprising sparked a new effort by all the English colonies to enslave native peoples and ship them to the sugar fields of the British West Indies. Colonial officials tolerated in their settlements only those Native Americans who had converted or displayed complete subservience. By 1685, only 2,500 Powhatans remained of a population of more than twenty thousand in 1607.

### Plymouth, 1620
Plymouth, 1620: In England the Anglican Church served as an instrument of political control, a merger of church and state. The Puritans wished to reform church and society — they too believed in a union of church and state, but only to unify society in religious harmony — and the Separatists among them went further, rejecting the role of men as intermediaries with God, along with tradition and symbol, in favor of personal discipline and morality. They believed in predestination: God had chosen his saints, and crime, poverty, and disease were his punishment for wickedness. In 1611 James I tightened political and religious control with the King James Bible — to defy the Church of England was to defy the king — and threatened to drive the Separatists “out of the land, or else do worse.” The Separatist congregation fled to Holland in 1607–08 to escape persecution, sneaking across the Channel because leaving without royal consent was illegal. In Holland they found freedom of worship, but the truce with Spain was ending, Holland was growing less tolerant, and their children were becoming Dutch. Rumors of native savagery and a risky voyage did not deter them; they believed God would provide. Through William Bradford they secured a patent from the Virginia Company and financing from London merchant investors, and after months in Holland waiting for the ship — during which many of the congregation lost heart and returned to England — they sailed.
The Mayflower left in September 1620 with 102 passengers and a crew of about thirty, bound for the Hudson River. For two months they lived cramped and sick in the dark middle deck, with partitioned rooms, water casks, and chamber pots — an ocean passage that was a literal rite of passage. Severe weather blew them north, and on November 11 the ship dropped anchor off present-day Cape Cod, a hundred yards from shore. They had no royal authority to colonize outside the Hudson valley, but it was too dangerous and difficult to sail on. A small party went ashore in search of suitable land and people and found neither; searching for supplies, they found the remnants of an English fort and indigenous burial sites, and they dug into them, removing stored corn and bows and arrows. Without land or resources to build shelter, the passengers stayed aboard, upsetting a crew that wanted to go home, and conflict consumed them until some of the Virginia Company men proposed leaving the Separatists and sailing back. Captain Christopher Jones and William Bradford knew survival depended on total cooperation, and over the next day forty-one men, Separatists and “strangers” alike, hammered out what came to be called the Mayflower Compact — the first written framework of self-government among the English colonies — created, ironically, by a sect that had learned the benefits of dividing church and state while stranded in Holland. It was important that all eligible males, old and young, servant and master, sign. Eligible meant male and English: the women aboard did not sign, and the Wampanoag, on whose shore the ship mistakenly landed, had no part in it. Plymouth decided who belonged before it built a house. Lacking a royal charter, they combined themselves “together into a civill body politick,” using the Separatists’ self-governing congregation as the model for their political structure.
The people they had come to live among were in the middle of a catastrophe. Champlain had encountered heavily populated nations along this coast in 1605, and English fishing crews had been driven off in 1607. In 1614 John Smith and Thomas Hunt returned to trade, and Hunt lured several indigenous people aboard his ship and sold them into slavery in the Spanish markets. Among the twenty-seven stolen from the bay was Squanto — Tisquantum — who was sold to a Catholic priest, sent to England, lived several years in London, and in 1619 talked his way onto a ship bound for home by promising to show the English where to find gold; as the ship neared the coast he leapt overboard and swam ashore, to find his people dead of disease and their lands abandoned. In 1615 a French vessel had wrecked on Cape Cod, its crew killed or enslaved, and one Frenchman reportedly told the Wampanoag that his God would take revenge and wipe them out. A great plague then ravaged New England between 1616 and 1619, killing nearly ninety percent of the native population of the coast. When the Mayflower arrived, the land had already been cleared and the people decimated; Massasoit and the Wampanoag had been reduced from two thousand to almost three hundred. The remaining natives still outnumbered the English more than two to one and could easily have eliminated them — but they feared the white man’s God even more, and the Wampanoag’s largest rival, the Narragansett, largely untouched by the plague, were poised to overtake Massasoit. The plague of 1616 was a turning point that made English colonization possible: it weakened native peoples enough for a small, unsupported group of religious refugees to establish a foothold. Where the Wampanoag saw godly punishment, the Pilgrims saw God’s blessing and his authority to colonize.
Massasoit was confused about how to engage the newcomers — whether to befriend them as allies against the Narragansett or to attack. The Wampanoag sachems performed rituals to invoke supernatural forces to drive the English away; when their witchcraft produced no immediate result, Tisquantum urged Massasoit to befriend the colonists, because his enemies would then be “constrained to bowe to him.” Samoset, who had also learned English as a captive, petitioned to make contact, and on Friday, March 16, while the settlers were at military training, he “boldly came alone” into the settlement and set their fears at ease by asking for beer. He spent the day giving them intelligence of the surrounding nations, returned the next day with five men bearing deer skins and a cat skin — and, standing at a safe distance, Massasoit asked the Pilgrim leaders to accept him for a talk, on condition that they send out a valuable hostage to remain with the Wampanoag until the meeting concluded. They chose Edward Winslow. Bradford and Massasoit agreed on a mutual alliance: in exchange for military technology, the Pilgrims would receive crucial assistance in farming and gathering the land’s resources. The relationship was celebrated after the fall harvest of 1621 in what we call Thanksgiving — three days of feasting, gambling, shooting exhibitions, and other forms of friendship. No tables with white linen, not even silverware. Almost everything we know about it comes from a few lines in a letter:
Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that so we might after a special manner rejoice together, after we had gathered the fruits of our labors; they four in one day killed as much fowl, as with a little help beside, served the Company almost a week, at which time amongst other Recreations, we exercised our Arms, many of the Indians coming amongst us, and amongst the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five Deer, which they brought to the Plantation and bestowed on our Governor, and upon the Captain and others. And although it be not always so plentiful, as it was at this time with us, yet by the goodness of God, we are so far from want, that we often wish you partakers of our plenty.
— Edward Winslow, December 1621
When news of the Virginia uprising of 1622 reached Plymouth, the colony was instructed to be wary of native hostility, and selling guns and ammunition to native peoples was made illegal. Both peoples remained skeptical about their futures, and perhaps the most significant moment between them came when Massasoit fell gravely ill. Unable to heal him by traditional means, several Wampanoag traveled the eleven miles to Plymouth for help, and Edward Winslow, the most learned of the Pilgrims, went to care for him: suspecting typhus, he scraped the infection from Massasoit’s mouth with a knife and fed him a brew of English herbs and fruit. Massasoit recovered and proclaimed the English good and honest partners. The relationship would last nearly three decades — until reckless and greedy newcomers violated several treaties, resulting in a bloody campaign to exterminate every native inhabitant of the surrounding area. By 1630 the Plymouth colony had grown to a settlement of a few hundred — about three hundred, and roughly a thousand by 1640, with the Compact as its governing frame, a tight-knit community of schools, churches, businesses, and farms, and, in Bradford, an elected governor who drafted much of its legal code and built a community focused on religious tolerance and private agriculture.

### Complex Characters
Complex Characters: Massasoit: The English remembered him by his title. Massasoit means great sachem, and the man who held it in 1621 called himself Ousamequin, or Yellow Feather. By 1632 Plymouth’s records used his own name, but the title stuck, and so did a familiar picture of him: the friendly chief at the first Thanksgiving.
The record shows a politician, though every line of it comes from English writers, chiefly Winslow and Bradford, so his reasons have to be read from what he did. His treaty of March 22, 1621, required him to hand over any of his people who harmed the English, and it pledged each side to aid the other if attacked. In 1622 he used those terms against Tisquantum, the interpreter who had brokered the alliance and then begun to work it for his own advantage, and sent messengers to Plymouth with his own knife to cut off Tisquantum’s head and hands. Governor Bradford came close to handing him over, then stalled when a boat appeared offshore, and the messengers left in anger. Tisquantum died of a fever that fall.
In March 1623, soon after Winslow nursed him back to health, Massasoit repaid the debt with a warning: the Massachusett, he said, planned to destroy the English settlement at Wessagusset and then Plymouth itself. Myles Standish marched to Wessagusset and killed the Massachusett leader Pecksuot and several of his men in a sudden attack, and one man’s head went on display at Plymouth as a warning. Ironically, the colony’s first deadly strike against Native people began with a tip from its closest Native ally.
The alliance also held, and for forty years. Massasoit kept the Pokanoket out of the Pequot War, and in the winter of 1635–36 he sheltered Roger Williams after Massachusetts Bay banished him. In addition, he sold land. In 1649, at an outcrop later called Sachem Rock, he deeded the land that became the Bridgewaters to Myles Standish and two other Duxbury men for seven coats, nine hatchets, eight hoes, twenty knives, four moose skins, and ten and a half yards of cotton. A deed for Dartmouth followed in 1652. Each sale bought goods and goodwill from the English, and each one left less land for the sons who would succeed him.
He died around 1660. Soon after, his son Wamsutta went to the Plymouth court and asked for English names for himself and his brother Metacom, and the court named them Alexander and Philip. Within fifteen years Philip would lead the war that nearly destroyed New England.
In 1921, for the three-hundredth anniversary of the landing, the Improved Order of Red Men, a fraternal order that admitted only white men, commissioned a bronze Massasoit from the Utah sculptor Cyrus Dallin and set it on Cole’s Hill, above Plymouth Rock. Copies stand today at the Utah State Capitol and at Brigham Young University. In 1970 Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag known as Wamsutta, received an invitation to speak at the 350th anniversary of the landing; the organizers read his remarks in advance and judged them too inflammatory. On Thanksgiving Day he delivered them anyway, on Cole’s Hill beside the statue. Native people have gathered there every Thanksgiving since for a National Day of Mourning.

### Massachusetts Bay, 1630
Massachusetts Bay, 1630: With the accession of Charles I the Puritans in England faced new and more intense persecution, and the Puritan leader John Winthrop — a reformer of the Church, not a Separatist from it — decided to lead his troubled sect to New England. Having heard of the Pilgrims’ success, Winthrop organized a mass migration: the Winthrop Fleet of eleven ships and nine hundred people, and within two years the Massachusetts Bay Colony, which drew the bulk of the Great Migration — some twenty thousand people to New England by 1640, nearly nine thousand of them in Massachusetts Bay alone and became the dominant settlement in the region. Winthrop wanted to prove that Puritan belief was truly the way to serve God — run out of England for those beliefs, the Puritans meant to validate them in a new land — and his sermon “A Modell of Christian Charity” set out how the settlers should treat one another so that they and the colony would survive: “We shall be as a city upon a hill; the eyes of all people are upon us.” Inspired by Calvin, many embraced predestination and lived in great anxiety over whether they were among the elect; some hoped for a conversion experience, others relied on “preparation” under their ministers, and still others believed themselves God’s chosen, the new Israelites, saved if they obeyed his laws. The colony’s economy was small but diverse — wheat and barley, fishing, timber exported to the West Indies — and unlike Jamestown it would not see enslaved Africans until much later. Where Jamestown desperately needed labor, the Puritans had all the labor they needed. They only required more land.
That requirement set the course of everything that followed. Unlike the passengers of the Mayflower, who had relied on native assistance to survive, Boston by 1640 was a self-sufficient colony that no longer saw Native Americans as useful. On the contrary: “Boston,” Winthrop wrote, “is surrounded by a hideous and desolate wilderness, filled with wild beasts and wild men.” The Puritan leadership saw native peoples as their opposites — degenerate pagans who had failed to overcome the land — and among the greatest fears of Puritan society was the seductiveness of native ways; preachers and magistrates denounced any contact with the heathen. As more immigrants arrived, land grew scarce, and the vast cultural differences produced conflict. The nations of the Northeast shared linguistic commonalities but lacked political unity; the Powhatan had belonged to a great confederacy, but no such organization existed in New England, where sachems chronically fought one another for wampum and, most of all, pride, in skirmishes that produced few casualties, since neither side sought annihilation but subservient debtors. New England nations farmed and gathered on land and sea; women and children performed most of the farm and household work while men hunted and fought, spending the long summer months in what the Puritans, who viewed work as essential to existence, saw as leisure. The Puritans dismissed native men as lazy ingrates who exploited their women; natives saw the English as grossly obsessed with land, stingy, enslaved by greed. English notions of property confused native people, who viewed all land as belonging to everyone. As the Puritans cleared land, uprooted stumps, and built fences for structures and livestock, Native Americans were prosecuted for hunting on English land while English livestock destroyed native “three sisters” fields — prompting natives to kill roaming cattle and pigs. The Puritans looked on native land use as wasteful mismanagement in need of white, Christian improvement; Winthrop preferred to purchase land rather than take it, and the Puritans paid pitiful sums, usually in items they deemed of little value, like kettles and axes, presenting deeds with boundaries that were meaningless to people who did not recognize private property.

### The Pequot War, 1637
The Pequot War, 1637: The Pequot, who commanded large territories, had partnered with the Dutch in the fur trade and conquered smaller surrounding nations, and in a proxy war the Dutch and English competed for dominance in the region and control of the trade. As the English encroached on Pequot territory, the Pequot killed several English traders, among them John Stone — wanted by the Massachusett people for kidnapping and by the Massachusetts Bay Colony for other crimes. Both parties demanded that Stone’s killers be surrendered for compensation; the Pequot refused and continued to kill English traders, and the Massachusett, the Narragansett, the Mohegan, and the English formed an alliance against them. The war lasted eleven months, from John Endecott’s raid on Block Island in August 1636 to the swamp fight at Fairfield in July 1637. The Pequot, familiar with European warfare and superior warriors through years of conflict with their neighbors, easily withstood English and native attacks — until the morning of May 26, 1637, when the English and their native allies attacked the Pequot village at Mystic while most of its men were away. Pequot casualties ranged from four hundred to six hundred — men, women, and children. The war forever changed the political and social landscape of southern New England: it demonstrated the English ability to secure native cooperation in order to wage total war against their enemies, it became the turning point in total English domination of the region and the end of Dutch power there, and it marked the beginning of English efforts to enslave native peoples. Like the Powhatan uprising, it marked a shift away from the initial goals of peaceful relations and humane treatment. The peace settled the matter on paper. The Treaty of Hartford, signed on September 21, 1638, by the English, Miantonomo for the Narragansett, and Uncas for the Mohegan, with no Pequot signature on it, divided the surviving Pequot between the two allied nations, barred them from their homeland, and decreed that they “shall no more be called Pequots but Narragansetts and Mohegans.” The treaty divided the survivors and then killed all that remained after the massacre, eradicating with pen and ink what fire and sword could not: a people’s name, their history, and their future. Its most significant outcome was a new pattern of English policy that demanded the complete eradication of native peoples from their lands — a policy of total war and the elimination of entire nations that would be repeated in Anglo-Indian encounters from the Thames in 1813 to Horseshoe Bend, Sand Creek in 1864, and Wounded Knee in 1890.

### King Philip’s War, 1675–76
King Philip’s War, 1675–76: By 1650 Plymouth was governed by the second generation. Unlike their parents, who had relied on native support to survive, the English now exploited native people for goods and land. Josiah Winslow, son of the Edward Winslow who had nursed Massasoit back to health, became governor of Plymouth Colony and lacked the respect and egalitarian view of the indigenous his father had held; the colony embraced a new policy for obtaining native land as it struggled for room, and Indian land sales in the colony multiplied from the 1650s into the 1660s, often through illegal and manipulative means. The newer generation of Native Americans, meanwhile, had slowly become more reliant upon English commodities while depleting their largest source of economic strength, the beaver — the same conversion the previous chapter traced in the Great Lakes, arriving in New England with no rival empire to cushion it. Massasoit’s eldest son, Alexander, became sachem in 1662 and was soon summoned by the English on charges of conspiring with the Narragansett; Josiah Winslow and several soldiers marched to find him and took him at gunpoint to answer questions. Released for lack of evidence, he died of a “sudden illness” before he reached home. His younger brother, Philip — Metacom — became sachem at about twenty-four, convinced the English had poisoned Alexander. Despite his youth he displayed great leadership and strength, declaring to the English that just as Charles II ruled England, so he commanded all the native peoples of Plymouth; in mockery, Massachusetts Bay officials nicknamed him “King Philip.” Sensing the English hunger for land, Metacom used their greed, selling what little land he had left for wampum and English coin, which he used to buy weapons from the Dutch. Between 1671 and 1675 he amassed arms and native support, believing he could overtake the colonies, and in 1671, as rumors of war circulated, the English summoned him to Taunton to explain himself. In the meetinghouse — colonists on one side, natives on the other — Philip stated that his preparations were not against the English but against the Narragansett; the colonists demanded he sign a treaty requiring the Wampanoag to pay annual tribute, a fine of one hundred pounds, and the surrender of their arms. Philip resolved to sell more land to the English in order to secretly obtain more weapons, and the English, for the land, were willing.
The breaking point came in an illegal trial: three of Philip’s closest warriors were accused of murdering John Sassamon, a native convert to Christianity and an English spy. English law required two witnesses to convict for murder; all three were hanged on the testimony of one native. Philip understood that survival depended on uniting the nations, and he began to plan the destruction of the English. For forty years he had signed treaties with them and kept every promise, asking only one thing in return — that they do nothing to turn his people from their religion — while Puritan influence forced members of weaker nations to convert and relocate into “Praying Towns,” designated residences where converts toiled in labor and social inferiority. Combined with rising English aggression and oppression, it was enough. The unprecedented unification of rival nations allowed Metacom to sustain a year of war. On June 20, 1675, Pokanoket warriors raided, looted, and burned several homes, and over the following days killed nine colonists. By the summer of 1675 the Massachusetts Bay Colony was on the brink of extinction; through the winter Metacom’s alliance terrorized English villages, and settlers abandoned their homes for the protection of the larger towns, until by the spring of 1676 nearly all had fled to Boston. Native warriors slaughtered hundreds of colonists, burned homes and fields, and killed livestock. What made the attacks even more devastating was their rejection of Christianity: warriors desecrated Puritan churches, mocked the Bible, and left terrorizing messages for the survivors. They specifically targeted cattle and pigs, which they detested for ruining their fields, and English fences, which symbolized English notions of property and greed. Rumors circulated of settlers routinely tortured, burned, and dismembered — stories that naturally excluded the equally brutal tactics of English soldiers.
But the superior number of English soldiers and guns proved too much to overcome, and so did the incomplete unification of the nations: had the Narragansett — the largest nation in the region, formally neutral until the English preemptive assault on their fortified village in the Great Swamp on December 19, 1675, drove them into the war — joined Metacom from the start, the English might have been driven from Massachusetts. The English intensified their raids on native villages, burning crops and food stores and forcing Metacom to retreat into the wilderness; troops tracked him to the Pocasset swamp, from which he escaped on rafts into Nipmuck territory, and the English, too, demonstrated the ability to form native alliances, which ultimately prevailed as Metacom’s forces sustained insurmountable losses. On August 12, 1676, in the Miery Swamp near Mount Hope, Philip was shot and killed by John Alderman, a Praying Indian. His body was brought to Plymouth, hanged, and then beheaded, drawn, and quartered; his head was placed on a spike at the entrance to the town, where it remained for more than twenty years as a reminder to any native who dared oppose the English.
Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay lost close to eight percent of their adult male population to the war. Native losses were far greater: about two thousand men killed or dead of wounds, more than three thousand dead of sickness or starvation, and another thousand sold into slavery and transported to the British Caribbean; about two thousand escaped to other nations to the north and west, from which they continued to raid well into the next century. Together those losses came to some eight thousand people, between roughly forty and eighty percent of the region’s Native population, depending on the prewar estimate. The war lasted only fourteen months, but it changed the face of New England. After fifty-five years of peace, the lives of natives and English had become so intertwined that when the fighting broke out many of the region’s Indians did not know what to do — some supported Philip, others joined the colonial forces, others tried to stay out — and violence spread until the entire region became a war zone; a third of the hundred or so towns in New England were burned and abandoned. Of approximately seventy thousand people in New England at the outbreak of hostilities, somewhere near five thousand were dead by the end, more than three-quarters of them Native Americans. In terms of the percentage of population killed, King Philip’s War was more than twice as bloody as the American Civil War and at least seven times more lethal than the American Revolution. It had taken fifty-six years to unfold, but one people’s quest for freedom had resulted in the conquest and enslavement of another.

### Aftermath — The Seaflower
Aftermath — The Seaflower: King Philip’s War remains the deadliest conflict in American history per capita, and, as after the Powhatan wars, Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay adopted a policy of strict intolerance. Natives who had converted remained in the Praying Towns, with their posterity, as members of the colonial lower class. The death of Metacom removed the presence of all “friendly Indians” who had once coexisted with the Pilgrims in order to survive and maintain peace, and without that buffer the New England frontier would experience decades of violence. By destroying the people who had sustained their forefathers, the colonists destroyed their forefathers’ way of life. The Pilgrims had come not to conquer but to find a world of their own; but the moment they took native charity, and the moment Massasoit befriended William Bradford, the two nations became intertwined, each depending upon and learning from the other. The newer generations of both began to envision a world without each other — and attempted to achieve cultural independence and superiority, ironically, by removing the very people who had sustained them. In 1676, more than five decades after the Mayflower dropped anchor off Cape Cod, a similarly named but far less famous ship, the Seaflower, departed from the shores of New England. Like the Mayflower she carried a human cargo — not 102 potential colonists but 180 Native American slaves, bound for the Caribbean. Josiah Winslow, son of Mayflower passengers Edward and Susanna Winslow, had provided the captain with the necessary documentation: a certificate under his seal explaining that these men, women, and children had joined in an uprising against the colony and were guilty of “many notorious and execrable murders, killings, and outrages,” and that as “heathen malefactors” they had been condemned to perpetual slavery. The Seaflower was one of several New England vessels bound for the West Indies with native slaves, but by 1676 the planters of Barbados and Jamaica had little interest in slaves who had already shown a willingness to revolt; no evidence records what became of the people aboard, though the captain of one such ship was forced to sail all the way to Africa before he could dispose of his cargo. And so, half a century after the Mayflower, a vessel from New England completed a transatlantic passage of a different sort. What, then, is the Pilgrims’ legacy?

### The Balance, Broken Early
The Balance, Broken Early: In the Great Lakes, as Chapter 3 showed, the nations that the fur trade transformed kept their leverage for two centuries, because there were always two empires to bargain between and the price of an alliance never fell to nothing. In the Chesapeake and New England there was one bidder. The Powhatan and the Wampanoag ran the same conversion — dependence on English commodities, the beaver depleted, land sold to buy guns — with no rival empire to discipline the English or to raise the price of native friendship, and their power ended a century before the Haudenosaunee’s did: the Powhatan confederacy broken by 1646, the Pequot destroyed in a morning in 1637, the nations of southern New England reduced by as much as four-fifths in fourteen months in 1676. Same diseases, same trade, same exhaustion of the endogenous base — a different market, and a century’s difference in how long the leverage held. That is what the imperial balance was worth, and it is the measure of what was lost when the balance ended everywhere in 1763.

## Then and Now

1. Since 1970 a different gathering has met on Cole’s Hill, above Plymouth Rock, every Thanksgiving Day. It began with a speech that was never given: Wamsutta Frank James, a Wampanoag, was invited to speak at Massachusetts’s three hundred fiftieth anniversary observance that year, and when the organizers read his prepared text they would not let him deliver it. He read it on the hill instead, and the United American Indians of New England have returned every year since for a National Day of Mourning. One landing, two memories, and the question of who gets to tell the story.
