### A different approach
This is a survey of American history from the worlds that existed here before 1492 through the end of the Civil War in 1865, the first half of the two-semester sequence. While it covers the standard first-semester timeline, it approaches that history from angles most textbooks avoid. Rather than simply recounting the familiar sequence of events, it examines the people, competing interests, institutions, and struggles for power that defined the American experience. The chronology remains intact, but the book organizes that timeline around complex questions, controversial interpretations, and the competing arguments that shaped the nation’s development. It is currently free and unpublished, written and revised while it is being taught. The chapters are grouped into units drawn by argument rather than by era, and each unit opens with a guiding question.
There is a business side behind the disconnect between students and textbooks. Publishers want to sell their books to school districts across the country, particularly in large markets such as Texas and California. That means producing a book that can satisfy a wide range of expectations without upsetting the people who decide what gets taught. The safest course is often to soften the edges.
Two things set this book apart from the conventional commercial survey. First, it advances a single argument that runs through the whole text: the history of America belongs to all the people who helped shape it. Native nations, the enslaved, and ordinary men and women from every background appear as historical actors in their own right, alongside the explorers, founders, political leaders, and generals who usually occupy the center of the story. Their ambitions, choices, and struggles are examined within the circumstances of their time. No one is reduced to a hero or villain; the reader is left to weigh the decisions people made and the consequences that followed.
Second, the notes do more than document sources. They examine the evidence itself. They distinguish authentic historical statements from quotations fabricated or misattributed long after the fact, identify where the record is uncertain, and show where scholars disagree. A student can watch how historical knowledge is established, where interpretations diverge, and why some conclusions rest on firmer ground than others. Historical method is not confined to a sidebar or treated as an abstract exercise. It becomes part of the reading, giving students the opportunity to practice the historian’s craft as they move through the text.
At first, I offered my own material as a supplement to the standard textbook, and students were free to cite either one. Before long, I noticed several of my students had started citing my work instead. I asked my class for a show of hands: Who preferred my chapters? To my surprise, nearly two-thirds raised their hands. I asked what, specifically, made my chapters land for them. One student said, “The textbook feels like work and I lose interest quickly. I can actually read yours and stay focused.” Another explained that my chapters included anecdotes and context that helped make the material easier to understand. Some appreciated the variety of perspectives, saying they never felt pushed toward any particular conclusion. Years later, when I resumed teaching on a college campus, I noticed the same pattern: by the middle of the semester, most of my college students were citing my work rather than the assigned textbook.
### What this book is not
It is not a document reader, a reference encyclopedia, or a substitute for the primary sources students are expected to examine for themselves. It assumes you will bring those sources into the reading and engage them alongside the text. It also makes no attempt to include every term or topic found in a comprehensive survey. The book is written to be read from beginning to end, not consulted. ### How a chapter fits a class session
You should not have to rebuild your course to use this book. Twenty chapters carry the survey from the worlds before 1492 to 1865, and a chapter is the pre-class reading for one session. They run six to nine thousand words — twenty to thirty pages — which is one sitting, not one week.
Each chapter ends with four to seven Questions for Consideration. They are not review questions, and they have no answer key, because none of them has a single answer. They ask a student to take a position and defend it: which single event made the war inevitable, whether a lawful thing can be immoral, which of three men answered an unjust law correctly. They are written to be the first ten minutes of your class rather than a quiz.
My own courses run on three steps: Approach, Analyze, and Demonstrate. The chapter is the Approach—students come to class having already encountered the material, equipped with a method rather than simply a summary. The session is where they Analyze. The assignment is where they Demonstrate.
You do not have to adopt any of that to use the book. The only expectation it places on a syllabus is that students read the chapter before they walk in. If you lecture straight through, the chapter does the narrative work and leaves your lecture free to argue with it. Either way, the reading carries the story and the questions carry the argument.
Chapters are grouped into units drawn by argument rather than by era. Each unit opens with a short essay posing the question its chapters answer, which gives you a natural place to start a week or a module.
### The notes as an assignment engine
The notes are where the uncertainty lives. They record where a claim came from, what had to be corrected, and what could not be confirmed—making them among the most useful teaching materials in the book. Three examples illustrate the point. The Oregon chapter says the cry “Fifty-four forty or fight!” has echoed through textbooks ever since, and the note explains the careful verb: scholarship going back to Edwin Miles in 1957 established that it was never an 1844 campaign slogan but surfaced during the Oregon debate of 1845–46. The Cold Harbor chapter recounts the most famous scene of the battle—soldiers pinning their names to their coats the night before the assault—and then states plainly that it may not have happened. The chapter on emancipation quotes Lincoln’s August 1862 remarks to a Black delegation and notes that the printed account describes itself as “merely given as the substance of the President’s remarks,” not a verbatim record, and that a second printing differs in wording.
Each of those moments is an assignment: find a claim this chapter treats as uncertain, state the standard that makes it uncertain, and cite the page. A chatbot might produce a plausible answer, but it cannot reproduce the reasoning in these notes without the text in front of it. Students have to open the book.
### The Then and Now question — what it is for
Then and Now, the short block after the Questions for Consideration, does one thing in two moves. First it states a contemporary parallel as a claim, with a note behind it, because a parallel offered as a question commits to nothing and a student can agree with it for free. Then it tells the student to go after the claim. The Reform chapter closes this way: “Test it. Does that pairing hold, or does it reduce four men into two positions? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a cause today where allies agree on the goal and split over the methodology, and say which methodology the record suggests actually changes a country.”
Both branches are deliberate. A student who takes the first has to argue against the book, which means reading the chapter closely enough to find where the comparison strains. A student who takes the second has to build a parallel that appears nowhere in the chapter, the lecture, or the syllabus, and then defend it with the chapter’s evidence. Neither can be produced from a prompt, because neither has a correct answer sitting somewhere to be retrieved. In my own sections the past-to-present move is what students consistently rate highest, which is why it sits in the spine of the chapter rather than in a sidebar.
The parallels to the present are not decorative additions or convenient comparisons. They are part of the argument each chapter develops. The Constitution chapter ends by showing how the arithmetic of the Great Compromise continues to shape presidential elections. The Market Revolution closes with the Panic of 1819, a financial crisis whose consequences bear a striking resemblance to those of 2008. The Peculiar Institution ends with two institutions established by the same generation of free Black Americans, one of which remains standing today. A House Divided closes with two universities whose athletic teams still carry the name Rebels, although the word never meant the same thing in both places. The War for the Union ends with Benjamin Butler inventing “contraband of war” to get around the Fugitive Slave Law, and with the legal wall every administration since has had to navigate. These connections are not departures from the history being told. They reveal how its consequences continued to shape the country long after the events themselves had passed.
### Three habits, taught rather than described
Three habits run through the book, taught by use rather than explained in a sidebar. They are not evenly distributed, and you should know which is which before you build a syllabus around any of them.
Law, morals, and power, where the material supports it. For Americans, the idea of progress has long been the shared premise; the means of achieving it has been the conflict. The premise appears in successive promises—an errand into the wilderness, a more perfect union, a new birth of freedom, the Social Gospel, the Great Society—and in successive indictments: the jeremiad, “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God,” “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?,” a cross of gold, the iron heel of capitalism, a dream deferred. Every one of them, promise or indictment, assumes the nation can become better. The indictments hold the country to its own standard, not against it. What none of them settles is what better looks like, or how to reach it.
When a chapter arrives at that conflict, the book lays out the three instruments a movement can reach for—law, moral argument, and power—and asks which of them actually moved the country. Two chapters are built on this framework and name it: The American Reformation, where it stands as a section of its own, and A House Divided, where the Anthony Burns case forces the question I put to every class: whether something lawful can be immoral. The rest of the book never requires the framework, and it is not imposed where the material does not call for it.
Explaining is not excusing. The Peculiar Institution tests the chapter’s own premise on a population nobody in the chapter is arguing about, then stops. A legacy explains; it does not command. Cultural legacies can account for a stereotype and can become convenient vehicles for justifying present conditions; they are not predestination orders. The Peculiar Institution is where the book tests that principle most directly, with a single cross-reference in The War for the Union. It also serves as the guardrail beneath all twenty Then and Now blocks. Each claims that the past reaches the present; this is the line that keeps those connections from hardening into determinism.
Reading between the sources. Every chapter carries notes—twenty to seventy-one apiece. Four chapters label their corrections in bold throughout: Manifest Destiny, A House Divided, The War for the Union, and A New Birth of Freedom. Those are the Civil War-era chapters in which the sources fought hardest. The remaining chapters do the same corrective work without the label.
### What is missing
You should know what you are not getting.
No test bank. None exists, and I am not going to pretend one is coming soon. The Questions for Consideration are built for discussion and for writing, not for a scantron, and there is no item bank keyed to them.
No slides keyed to the chapters. I teach from decks, but they are built around my class sessions, not around these chapters, and handing you mine would be handing you my lecture rather than the book’s.
The figures are nearly done, and not done. Every image is checked against the holder’s own rights record before it is chosen, and most chapters now have their images selected, measured, and credited. The images themselves will be placed when the book is laid out for print, so the chapters in this draft are text only. Where an image could not be cleared, there is no image.
Some passages still follow a published survey closely. Where they do, the note says so and cites the passage as closely paraphrased rather than hiding it. The disclosure is the policy, not a placeholder for one.
No second semester. This is Volume I. If you teach the full year, the back half of your course still needs its usual text.
It has not been peer reviewed, and it is being revised while it is taught. You are looking at a draft with its seams visible on purpose.
One more thing, which is a limitation and also the point: where a figure or a quotation could not be sourced, it is not in the book. There are claims I would like to make that are sitting in a file waiting for a citation, and some of them are good. The book is thinner than it could be because of that, and I would rather it be thinner.
### What I want back
If you use any part of the book, I would like to know how it worked in your classroom—and where it did not. Not if you personally enjoyed it, although it’s nice to hear. More importantly, did a chapter land? Did the questions produce an argument or silence? Did the Then and Now connections illuminate the present, or did they feel like a stretch? If you find an error, tell me. I will correct it and credit the catch in the notes. If a chapter does not work for your course, I would rather know than have you quietly work around it. Honest criticism will improve the book as well as my writing.
