UNIT VIII
The One Boundary
Everything Between Texas and the Pacific, and the Line the Nation Could Not Draw Through It
~
In February 1848 the United States conquered and purchased everything between Texas and the Pacific. Thirteen years later it went to war with itself. These two chapters are the taking and the failure to settle what had been taken, and they are one story rather than two: the land won in Mexico handed the nation the one question the founders had deferred, and every attempt to answer it made the division worse.
“Manifest Destiny” follows the expectation that produced the war. The young republic had grown in leaps: the Mississippi in 1783, Louisiana in 1803, Spanish Florida in 1819. Each acquisition opened land, and land for white Americans meant the farm, the vote, the jury box, the militia, and the public school, the rights the Democratic Revolution had just extended to white men regardless of property. By the 1840s, Americans had known little but a frontier that kept moving. They expected it to keep moving. An idea with a much older pedigree gave that expectation its moral justification: Manifest Destiny put the Puritans’ oldest argument, they are wasting the land, into a continent’s worth of ambition. The war began in 1846 and ended in 1848 with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. It delivered the territory expansionists had sought. It also delivered the question they had not settled: what would become of slavery in the land won from Mexico? Ralph Waldo Emerson had warned in 1846 that the victory itself could become the poison.
“A House Divided” follows thirteen years of answers. Every one aimed to take slavery out of national politics, and every one carried it further in. The Compromise of 1850 settled California’s admission and put a federal fugitive slave law into Northern courthouses. Popular sovereignty promised to let settlers decide for themselves. Instead, rival governments and Sharps rifles arrived on the Kansas prairie. The Dred Scott decision tried to remove the question from politics by law. Instead, the Court held that Congress had never possessed the power to prohibit slavery in the territories. Slavery reached the Northern town, the Northern courtroom, and finally the floor of the Senate, where a South Carolina congressman beat a Massachusetts senator nearly to death with a cane.
Law, morals, power: across the decade, each method failed in turn. Law failed first: every statute and every ruling pushed the question into more places than it removed it from. Morals did not fail so much as multiply: Stowe’s novel galvanized Northern opposition to slavery, the South answered with sermons of its own, and the churches split before the nation did. That left power.
The nation that could draw a line with Britain at the 49th parallel and a line with Mexico at the Rio Grande could not draw one through its own territory. It took a war to draw that one, and the next unit is the war.
