CHAPTER 7
Empire and War
The Atlantic Economy, the Imperial State, and the Struggle for North America, 1660–1763
“These savages may, indeed, be a formidable enemy to your raw American militia; but upon the king’s regular and disciplined troops, sir, it is impossible they should make any impression.”
— General Edward Braddock to Benjamin Franklin, 1755, as Franklin recorded it[[1]]
Between the Restoration and 1763, Britain’s relationship with its North American colonies ran first on commerce and then on war, and each redrew the boundaries of imperial authority: which colonial subjects the Crown would protect and tax, where they could settle, and how far its claims extended into the homelands of Native nations. Markets, labor, technology, private enterprise, the environment, and Crown policy shaped the colonial economies differently across British North America. Each region developed its own economic patterns, society, and politics. European nations acquired new lands to produce lucrative commodities for domestic use and for trade, and Britain attempted to regulate the flow of colonial goods through the series of laws known as the Navigation Acts, the working machinery of mercantilism. Meanwhile, the rivalry between Britain and France intensified. As the British colonial population grew, settlers pushed inland, threatening the French trade network and the autonomy of Native nations. The contest among Britain, France, and Native nations for trade and power culminated in the Seven Years’ War, known in America as the French and Indian War. Britain defeated France and its Native allies. Victory brought new conflicts. Colonists resisted the Crown’s effort to keep settlement east of the Appalachians, while Native nations sought to preserve trade with Europeans and keep settlers off their lands.[[2]] The chapter asks two questions of the same event: how the war changed colonial society and its relationship with England, and how it changed Native American societies and their relationships with both empires. Victory left the Crown with the authority to decide where its subjects could settle and which Native nations would stand beyond its reach. Neither the colonists nor the Native nations who had fought alongside the British accepted the boundaries the Crown sought to impose.

## Part I · The Empire as Market


### The Wheel of the Atlantic
The Wheel of the Atlantic: As the English colonial economies expanded, more land and labor were required to satisfy the European appetite for New World commodities. North America and the Caribbean produced raw materials and a few finished goods — rum, lumber, iron — which were exported to Europe for refining and manufacture. Textiles, iron tools, and guns were sent to Africa, where they armed the coastal states that conquered and sold captives; European traders carried the enslaved across the Atlantic, primarily to South America and the Caribbean. The plantation ran on people who owned nothing, not even their own hours. Planters had tried other hands first and lost them: European laborers walked off fields that used them up, and captive Native workers slipped into country they knew better than their captors did, or died of the diseases the ships brought in. England had entered that trade in earnest after the Restoration — the crown chartered the Company of Royal Adventurers in 1660 and its successor, the Royal African Company, in 1672, and when the Company’s monopoly was broken in 1698 the trade was thrown open to any English merchant willing to pay the duty. By the early eighteenth century England had become the leading carrier of enslaved Africans on the Atlantic. The mechanics of that trade, and what it built in the colonies, are Chapter 5’s subject; what matters here is that the wheel of Atlantic commerce, with slavery at its hub, is what the English state now organized itself to control.[[3]]

### Mercantilism
Mercantilism: Most colonists benefited from new opportunities in North America, primarily in terms of economic growth. But as colonial societies matured, an elite social class emerged. Neither as powerful nor as wealthy as the aristocracy of England, this upper class nonetheless began to shape politics and society. The eighteenth century may have seen the gap between rich and poor widen more sharply than at any other time in the colonies’ history. Expanding commerce made New England and Middle Colony merchants wealthy and politically influential, often through family ties and business partnerships with London’s great trading houses. By 1750, slave plantations dominated the Chesapeake and the Lower South, and the planter elite built enormous fortunes from their tobacco and rice.[[4]] As a new class of elite, colonial merchants and planters emerged, the English Crown amplified its efforts to control the means of production, transportation, taxation, and sales of all colonial products to ensure maximum profitability. In addition, as Spain, France, and the Netherlands each pursued their own paths to wealth and power, a new economic system emerged called mercantilism. In a mercantilist economy, the government sought to secure national wealth from its colonies by extracting raw materials, which could be sold to competing nations for gold or used to produce manufactured goods that could also be sold. According to mercantilist philosophy, wealth was a zero sum game. Or in other words, there was a finite amount of wealth in the world that was divided like a pie, where one country’s gain meant another nation’s loss. In this way, nations used their economies as weapons of war in four ways: create financial reserves in real money (hard coin), usually gold and silver; regulate the nation’s economy by producing manufactured goods that other nations would pay for in trade; finance the production of colonial raw materials and agriculture to end dependency upon other nations for goods; and, in order to conduct international commerce, secure sea travel through a strong naval presence. Lastly, according to mercantilist philosophy, no great nation could exist and be self-sufficient without colonial resources.
Consequently, with its colonies more than 3,000 miles away, England had a difficult time supervising trade and commerce in the New World. In addition, the great distance between the Mother Country and North America allowed enormous freedom and autonomy for nearly all of the 13 colonies. New York traded with the Algonquins, Barbados engaged with New England for grains and other raw materials, Pennsylvania regularly traded with the Delaware tribe and French, while many planters in the Carolinas traded frequently with the Cherokee, Choctaw, and even the Spanish. While foreign trade and interstate business deals produced significant wealth and prosperity for the colonies, England seldom received the bulk of its colonial goods, which ultimately inspired new restrictive, trade legislation from the Crown that criminalized trade between the colonies and European nations, and Native Americans as well.

### The Navigation Acts
The Navigation Acts: Beginning in 1651 and tightening after the Restoration, Parliament’s Navigation Acts built a legal wall around the empire’s commerce. The Navigation Acts required that only English ships, with English crews, could trade with the English colonies. Commodities could only enter through and leave from English customs or ports, and British authorities enumerated a set of protected goods that could only be sold to British merchants, including sugar, tobacco, cotton, indigo, furs, and iron. The success of the Dutch trading companies taught the English about the importance of quality ships and sailors, and the increased revenue was used to build better ships and employ more sailors. Other stipulations in the Navigation Acts were designed to make American colonies more dependent on manufactured products from Great Britain. This guaranteed that British manufactured products would always have a customer base in the colonies.[[5]] The machinery of enforcement, however, lagged a generation behind the law, and in the lag the colonies learned habits that would prove very hard to unlearn.

### Cousinocracy
Cousinocracy: In addition to regulating production, transport, taxation, and sales, the English authorities forbade the creation of colonial banks, which forced the colonial elite to take credit and loans from England. Across British America, a handful of prominent men ran colonial government. Virginia’s gentry married among themselves so often that the colony earned the label “cousinocracy”: the same families sat on the vestries of the Anglican Church, ran the county courts, which levied taxes and passed local ordinances as well as judging cases, and filled the House of Burgesses. By the 1750s a man of modest birth could no longer rise into that circle, and by 1770 almost every wealthy Virginian owed his fortune to inheritance. Birth, not merit, now decided who would be included among the Virginia elite, and the men at the top had the vestry, the court, and the Burgesses to keep it that way. Thomas Jefferson’s father, Peter, sat in the House of Burgesses, and his grandfather had held county office as justice of the peace, sheriff, and militia captain; three generations of Washingtons before George, his father, grandfather, and great-grandfather, served as justices of the peace and prospered as planters.[[6]] In these ways each region of British North America developed its own aristocracy of business and political elites — the men who would later face down the Crown when Parliament sought more control.

### Three Regions of One Empire
Three Regions of One Empire: For New England, this meant a boom for lumber and land (Native American land), shipbuilding, and food exports for the Caribbean, who used all available farm land for sugar cane. As the only colonies without an appointed governor or direct royal rule, Massachusetts and its charter-colony offshoots operated almost independently of king and Parliament, enacting their own laws, electing their own leaders, and enforcing their own religious code — a code severe enough to send four Quakers to the gallows at Boston between 1659 and 1661.[[7]] The Middle and Southern colonies, each one being a Royal Charter (under direct rule of the King, who usually appointed a royal governor) or Proprietary Charter (ruled by a wealthy aristocrat who made laws and appointed others independent of the king), exploded in tobacco, rice, and indigo production, creating a boom in English revenue, exports and most importantly, a demand for labor. But as colonial economies became more prosperous, the lack of local banks and credit forced colonists to procure more financial support from England. New England became more of a business and merchant colony, the Middle colonies produced the bulk of consumer agriculture, shipped to the other colonies, the West Indies, and England, and the Southern colonies maintained their role as the cash crop producer, enriching the mother country with sugar, tobacco, and rice with the expansion of slave labor.

### Native American Economies
Native American Economies: Native peoples felt the influence of the Atlantic economy long before, and far beyond, any direct contact with Europeans. Guns mattered most, followed by iron goods a village could not produce for itself—knives, hoe blades, and kettles—along with alcohol and glass beads used in ceremony. In exchange, traders wanted what came from the animals: beaver pelts from the northern woods and deerskins from the southern piedmont. The exchange favored the traders, since Native hunters did the work of hunting, skinning, and curing before a pelt ever changed hands.
Native nations competed for influence with the English, French, and Dutch, while maneuvering to keep their rivals from gaining access to the gun trade. Europeans soon discovered that trade offered no position of neutrality. A trader who supplied the Hurons had effectively taken sides against the Iroquois, and Native partners expected military support as part of the bargain. European demand for beaver outpaced the animal’s ability to reproduce, driving hunters into one another’s territories to maintain the trade.
Disease killed on a scale no raid could match. The losses fed mourning wars, expeditions that captured people to replace those who had died. As eighteenth-century warfare intensified, Europeans and Native peoples alike profited from the trade in captives, selling captured enemies into West Indian slavery.[[8]]

### Allies and Enemies
Allies and Enemies: One of the most persistent misunderstandings about Native American societies is that Native peoples thought of themselves as a single people, united by a common identity and a shared struggle against European expansion. They did not. Native nations pursued their own interests, settled old rivalries, and competed for power, sometimes by making European allies against Indigenous enemies. The Carolina trade reveals how readily those alliances formed—and how European traders learned to profit from divisions they had not created. The pattern repeated across generations, and the alliances rarely lasted. In the 1670s, Carolina traders armed the Westo, who raided neighboring peoples for captives to sell. By 1680, the Savannah had joined the colony against them, destroying the Westo in turn; Charleston’s merchants then sold Westo survivors into Caribbean slavery. During the Tuscarora War of 1711–13, South Carolina sent a small force of colonists north alongside as many as a thousand Native allies, including Yamasee and Apalachee warriors. At Neoheroka in March 1713, some 950 Tuscarora died or were sold into slavery. Those who survived moved north and joined the Iroquois Confederacy as its sixth nation.
The alliances shifted again two years later. In April 1715, the Yamasee joined Creeks, Catawbas, and other Native peoples in an attack on Carolina’s southern frontier, killing about seven percent of the colony’s settlers. Carolina survived because the Cherokee chose to fight alongside the colonists against the Creeks. That decision opened a war between the Cherokee and Creeks that would last forty years. Native nations did not confront European expansion as a single people with a common cause. They pursued their own interests, made their own calculations, and sometimes fought Europeans’ wars against one another—until the balance of power changed and yesterday’s allies became tomorrow’s enemies.[[9]]

### The Glorious Revolution in America
The Glorious Revolution in America: In 1685, King Charles II died leaving the throne to his younger brother James II. A Catholic, James II had firmly established his belief in authoritarian rule as proprietor of the New York colony, curtailing the liberties of the conquered Dutch, treating all protests as insubordination, and dismantling their colonial governments. As the new King of England, James II dispatched military men to govern the colonies, where they exercised more control over the colonists’ day to day lives. James II viewed the colonies as cash cows, meant to fund a more authoritarian crown. With more colonial revenue, James II sought to limit the power of Parliament, seeking more control over taxes and expenditures. James sought to reel in the colonial governments of New England by dismantling the Massachusetts colonial legislature, which had lost its charter in 1684, and combining all eight Middle and New England colonies into one “dominion” — Massachusetts, Plymouth, New Hampshire, Maine, Rhode Island, Connecticut, and eventually New York and New Jersey — under Governor Sir Edmund Andros, who ruled without an assembly. The dominion dispensed with colonial governments, signaling a reversal of colonial freedoms and autonomy. James II’s reduction of colonial power coincided with a substantial increase in taxes and tariffs imposed upon colonial merchants and producers, which generated wide-spread protest and resistance. In addition, James intensified the enforcement of the Navigation Acts, and violations were pressed in courts that sat without juries. The increased taxes and regulations imposed by James II stifled colonial economic and political power.[[10]] In England, James II alarmed members of Parliament and the Protestant aristocracy with his support for the Catholic elite and his unilateral decisions. In response, James II’s opponents plotted a coup to overthrow the tyrant, by bringing William of Orange — stadtholder of the Dutch Republic, James’s nephew and son-in-law — to ascend the Crown. In 1688, with the assistance of the English military, William replaced James II in a nearly bloodless uprising and vowed to work in cooperation with Parliament as a Constitutional Monarchy.
By 1689, word of James II’s departure reached the colonies, resulting in a restoration of colonial governments. They staged their own revolutions in miniature: Boston jailed Andros and his officers; in New York the militia captain Jacob Leisler seized the government in William’s name, and was hanged for treason when the new regime’s governor arrived; in Maryland a Protestant association overthrew the proprietor’s officers. Leisler had seized power in the king’s name, yet the king’s governor condemned him as a traitor. Loyalty to the Crown did not guarantee acceptance by the men who controlled its colonies. From Maryland to Massachusetts, new colonial legislatures expressed their allegiance to the new monarch, resulting in the formation of a more intertwined relationship between the colonists and the Crown. Colonial assemblies became more stable, as business and political elites gained more political and economic freedoms. Although royal governors remained in power, they remained subject to colonial legislatures, having to balance the interests of the Crown with their respective colonies.[[11]]

### The Fiscal-Military State
The Fiscal-Military State: Upon becoming the new English monarch, William of Orange intensified war efforts against France, developing a new militarized government. During the 1690s, England expanded its military expenditures to maintain control of Ireland, preserve its American colonies, and protect the Netherlands from French invasion. The massive military buildup produced the largest naval force in the world, but at the expense of higher levels of taxation. Parliament supported additional taxes by establishing a national debt and a national bank — the Bank of England, founded in 1694 — and by exercising more power over the king. However, despite England’s radical military expansion, nearly all military resources were dispersed outside of North America, which continued to suffer repeated defeats by the French and their Native American allies. Nonetheless, by 1715, England became the most powerful military and economic force in the world, supplanting the Dutch and Amsterdam as the financial center of Europe. However, in order to sustain its global dominance, England became ever more dependent upon its North American colonies, resulting in more economic freedom and colonial wealth. Colonial exporters were furthermore enabled to trade with foreign companies and colonies, producing even more prosperity and autonomy from England.

### Becoming American — and More British
Becoming American — and More British: The Triangle Trade had significant economic and social consequences for North America, the Caribbean, Europe, and Africa. From 1650 to 1700, transatlantic voyages from Europe rose from approximately 150 to 1,500, with the Atlantic Ocean transforming from a barrier to a bridge. Most colonists clustered along the coasts, looking eastward to the mother country for news and to maintain cultural ties. Westward expansion was largely viewed as dangerous and complicated, where uncertainty and violence with Native Americans discouraged most colonials from venturing west. As a result, colonial and English relationships strengthened from economic and cultural exchanges. The influx of print media served to provide the colonists with much desired news from England, which solidified the connection between the two continents: the colonies’ first continuously published newspaper appeared at Boston in 1704, and by 1739 thirteen colonial papers were in print, filled largely with London news that focused a diverse population on a single culture.[[12]] Furthermore, increased transatlantic commercial voyages helped bolster colonial economies and trade, which bolstered colonial income levels, resulting in higher consumption of the latest European fashions and commodities. A colonist in New York commented that one could find nearly every European commodity or luxury in Philadelphia, Boston, and New York. The economic growth of British North America simultaneously strengthened the cultural and political bonds between both groups, and inspired mass immigration of other European peoples to the colonies. The colonies grew more rapidly than any other economy in the 18th century. The rise in colonial population mirrored an increased standard of living as well. Free colonists enjoyed higher incomes and living conditions than their English counterparts, with the planter class of the Chesapeake experiencing the highest standard of living. At the other end of the colonial spectrum was New England, which without the benefit of slave labor saw higher levels of poverty. Yet even the most modest of farmers in New England fared better than residents of England. In addition, the free colonists in North America had higher disposable incomes and were free of the high taxes that English citizens were forced to pay. The surplus of land and resources provided more living space and better diets, resulting in better nutrition and physical health. Thus, by 1750, the average colonist was taller, heavier, and lived longer than their English counterparts.[[13]] Prosperity, paradoxically, made the colonies more British: the elite replicated the dress and culture of the mother country to shed the stereotype of rugged provincials, and the thirst for consumer goods produced, by 1770, a rising colonial debt to British merchants — for with no colonial banks permitted, companies and individuals procured loans from England against promises of sale.
However, as New England declined in agricultural production, it experienced higher levels of unemployment and poverty, especially during the winter and within the more populous seaports. Expenditures on the poor, namely “alms houses,” and welfare programs doubled from 1730 to 1760, and doubled again by 1775.[[14]] By 1700, the amount of New England poor startled British officials, especially when contrasted against the rise of New England merchant and business elites. Colonial officials cited three reasons for the drastic rise in economic inequality. First, English wars with France demanded colonials to serve in various capacities, resulting in physical handicaps and death, which left their families destitute. Second, following the French and Indian War, thousands of poor immigrants flocked to colonial seaports, causing lower wages and additional unemployment. And lastly, the merging of British and colonial economies resulted in more financial downturns, where economic instability in England in turn affected colonial industries. In essence, the rise in colonial population and urbanization caused colonists to compete for employment and land, with many choosing to migrate westward into hostile territories and at the expense of land speculators, which offered land to rent at high prices. Compounded debts were often passed down to future generations.

### Immigration
Immigration: Despite England’s dominance in Atlantic shipping, information, and goods, the overall number of immigrants declined in the early 1700s from previous levels as more jobs became available and industry expanded in England. In order to facilitate additional growth abroad and at home, England encouraged its other territories, namely Scotland and Ireland, to immigrate to North America. Parliament passed several laws that provided citizenship and land opportunities for foreign immigrants to North America, capped by the Plantation Act of 1740. Parliament had written the test of belonging into statute: seven years, a Protestant oath or a Jewish one, and no door at all for Catholics. Even with those limits, British America soon became known across Europe for its religious freedom and ethnic toleration. Paradoxically, as more European immigrants arrived in North America, colonial societies became more diverse and uniquely “American,” experiencing higher levels of social and economic stratification; wealth and poverty, Protestant and Catholic, White and Black. The combination of English sponsored immigration and recruitment efforts of the individual colonies to secure more labor produced new and distinct cultures in British North America.[[15]] In other words, it became the interest of England to strengthen its colonies with people not her own. More than any other 18th century empire, the British relied on foreign immigration to fulfill its labor demands: Scots, Germans, Irish, and most of all, Africans. As the slave trade grew, the number of Africans began to overwhelm southern colonial elites, prompting fears of revolts and uprisings. To counterbalance the influx of African slaves, southern colonies actively recruited and subsidized European immigrants, which created even higher levels of social stratification among slave societies in the South. By mid-century the Middle Colonies and the Chesapeake were the destinations of choice for Scots, Irish, and Germans, who gathered in ethnically similar communities along the western frontier — and that expansion, against a fixed supply of land, meant collision with Native Americans who had already lost too much of it. By 1754 the economic and cultural bonding of England and North America had produced, ironically, a plural society competing over ethnic, religious, and political power — and a frontier about to catch fire.

## Part II · The Empire as Army


### New France, 1754
New France, 1754: In comparison to their English rivals, the French had a difficult time populating their North American colony. The harsh climate and Native American presence made colonizing difficult and dangerous. Focusing primarily on its more lucrative Caribbean and South American ventures, the crown largely ignored the development of French North America. Despite their small population, the French actually occupied more territory than England. Stretching from the St. Lawrence River, to the Great Lakes Region, and down the Mississippi River Valley to New Orleans, the French controlled significantly more land than their British counterparts. Furthermore, their inferior numbers forced the French to foster better relationships among the Native Americans, where they became much more adept than the English at assimilating into native societies, forming alliances, and creating economically profitable trading networks. The French also realized that marriage into native kinships provided the best chances for trading success and personal safety. Understanding the fundamental actions of exchange and reciprocity, the French became favorable allies to many Native American societies, as they also lacked the population to pose a significant threat. Furthermore, participation in the fur trade with France provided the indigenous access to French guns, goods, and alcohol. With the exception of the Iroquois Confederacy, the French had established trading and political alliances with Native Americans to serve as both trade partners and a military buffer between the English and themselves. The smaller and weaker tribes, forced from their lands by Iroquois aggression, sought a more peaceful and cooperative relationship with the French. In turn, the French, who required intertribal peace to maximize trade profits, served as mediators and as a paternal-like figure, to negotiate peace. Mistakenly, the French interpreted native use of the term “father” and “children” to represent European notions of authority, giving the French a false understanding of their relationship. Furthermore, unlike European patriarchal societies, where one general commanded the entire army, Indian chiefs could only sway their clans to do France’s bidding, needing a consensus of both male and female leaders. However, despite the positive relationships with the indigenous and the creation of a “Middle Ground,” the French realized that their inferior numbers created a greater dependence upon Native American assistance than an Indian need for French goods. By the 1750s, the French had made fortifications from Canada, into the Ohio River Valley, and down the Mississippi River to the port city of New Orleans.[[16]]

### British North America, 1750
British North America, 1750: While the French claimed more land in North America than England, the British outnumbered their European rival by a ratio of 20 to 1 — more than a million colonists against some sixty thousand.[[17]] Throughout the 18th century, the rise of European immigration into Virginia and Pennsylvania pushed the limits of their available land. The backcountry stream was overwhelmingly Scots-Irish — Ulster Presbyterians by way of Pennsylvania — who, accustomed to rugged frontier life, opted to secure their living in dangerous proximity to Native Americans along the western edges of Pennsylvania, Virginia, and the Carolinas; Highland Scots came later and settled apart, most famously in the Cape Fear valley of North Carolina.[[18]] And unlike the French, the English colonists no longer needed native assistance or trade; they wanted native land. Penned between the Atlantic and the Appalachians, the exploding settlements required new territory, and wealthy colonists formed companies to survey, purchase, and resell western lands at a profit — speculation. The largest and most famous, the Ohio Company, was organized in the late 1740s by Colonel Thomas Lee with Lawrence and Augustine Washington, and Virginia’s royal governor, Robert Dinwiddie — himself an investor — authorized exploration and expansion into the Ohio country to secure lands for future sale.[[19]]

### The Native Ground
The Native Ground: After nearly 130 years of European colonization, most Native American tribes in the North East and east of the Appalachian Mountains had either been overrun or wiped out from war and disease. Those who remained fled west, with many assimilating into larger, stronger tribes. In addition, over a century of trade with Europeans had completely changed indigenous life, as they became dependent upon manufactured goods, weapons, and trade. By 1750, the geopolitical landscape for Native Americans featured new intertribal rivalries that competed for access to English and French trade. The most powerful tribes included the Huron, Shawnee, Cherokee, and the Iroquois Confederacy — the Six Nations whose founding and constitution Chapter 1 described. As the dominant powers in the region, these societies remained critical to both English and French prosperity in the frontier, both as trade and military partners. Among the first native people to assist the British were the Mingo and Wyandot of the Ohio Valley. The Huron and Ottawa chose to align themselves with the French, whose smaller numbers made them less of a threat, and were more favorable than the deceitful and greedy English. Both the English and French saw an alliance with the Iroquois as a crucial necessity for winning control of North America.[[20]] Iroquois alliances with Europeans began in 1677, when the English petitioned assistance against Metacom and other Algonquian tribes in King Philip’s War. Violence against the Algonquins and their allies, the French, started a new cycle of revenge killings called the “Mourning Wars,” which allowed the Iroquois to significantly bolster their populations, the source of their spiritual and physical power. However, in 1701, as the English and French increased in population and strength, the Iroquois Confederacy executed the Grand Settlement — simultaneous peace with the French at Montreal and the English at Albany — officially becoming a neutral power. For nearly five decades, the Iroquois strategically took advantage of the French and English competition for regional power, where they negotiated from a position of strength for favorable trade terms — the purest case of Native power built on position. Benjamin Franklin, watching it work, invoked the Six Nations in 1751 as proof that separate governments could form a durable union — “and yet that a like union should be impractical for ten or a dozen English colonies.”[[21]]

### Jumonville Glen
Jumonville Glen: The Ohio country broke the balance. France sought the lands south of the Great Lakes for access and defense; English speculators sought them for sale; the nations who lived there were courted by both. In 1752 the Ohio Company obtained from Iroquois representatives — whether through misunderstood terms or a deliberate Iroquois play of one empire against the other is unclear — access that English traders read as license, and speculators and trappers spilled over the Appalachians into French-claimed country, beginning a fort at the forks of the Ohio, the confluence of the Allegheny and the Monongahela, present-day Pittsburgh. The French overran the site and built Fort Duquesne. Dinwiddie sent a twenty-one-year-old major of the Virginia Regiment, George Washington — a surveyor of the Ohio country since sixteen, an inexperienced soldier — first, in December 1753, to deliver a demand that the French evacuate (the officers at Fort Le Bœuf, warm with wine, told him freely that they meant to hold the whole valley), and then, in 1754, to establish a British post. With him went a Seneca leader the English called the Half King — Tanaghrisson, whom the Iroquois had set over the Ohio nations as their spokesman — and a party of Mingo warriors.[[22]]
On May 27, 1754, native scouts reported a French force camped in a glen nearby, and Washington, primed by reports of French aggression, marched at night. What happened at dawn on May 28 was contested ever after. By Washington’s report the French went for their weapons and he ordered the volley; after a fifteen-minute skirmish the French survivors surrendered, among them the officer Joseph Coulon de Jumonville, who insisted through an interpreter that he was on a diplomatic mission carrying a letter — a mirror of Washington’s own errand the winter before. While Jumonville spoke, Tanaghrisson — who carried an old grievance against the French — said to him, “Thou art not yet dead, my father,” split his skull with a tomahawk, and washed his hands in the Frenchman’s brains, and his warriors killed and scalped the enemy wounded, sending the scalps out as proof that war had begun. The French version, from a soldier who escaped, had the Virginians firing on the wounded until the Mingos intervened. Dinwiddie blamed the Half King and his warriors — “we were as auxiliaries to them” — and the French press portrayed Washington as the murderer of a diplomat. The charge had force because the immunity of an envoy was among the oldest rules Europeans claimed to fight by, and both crowns acknowledged it — which is exactly why the French insisted Jumonville was an ambassador and the British insisted he was a scout. Whoever owned the blame, a young Virginia officer had set the world on fire: the war this skirmish began would engulf North America, Europe, Africa, and India.[[23]]

### Fort Necessity
Fort Necessity: Expecting retaliation, Washington fortified — against the Half King’s advice — a primitive stockade in an open meadow he called Fort Necessity, so close to the tree line that an attacker could fire from cover. Disgusted, Tanaghrisson and the Mingos abandoned him. On July 3, 1754, some six hundred French and a hundred native allies, led by Captain Louis Coulon de Villiers — Jumonville’s elder brother — attacked in a torrential rain that muddied the fort and ruined the Virginians’ powder; Washington’s men, believing death at hand, opened the rum casks. His regiment shattered, Washington signed a capitulation whose French text — unmentioned by his translator — included a confession to the “assassination” of Jumonville, a document that would surface in the British press. He marched back to Virginia without his guns or his colors. Tanaghrisson, his authority spent, lost favor with his own people and died before the year was out.[[24]]

### Albany
Albany: In 1754, as the French mobilized to secure the Ohio country and as relations with the Iroquois Nation deteriorated, British officials in London commanded its colonial governments to meet. Although the treaty with the Iroquois was the main purpose of the Congress, the delegates also met to discuss intercolonial cooperation on other matters. With the French and Indian War looming, the need for cooperation was urgent, especially for colonies likely to come under attack or invasion. Imperial officials saw the advantages of bringing the colonies under closer authority and supervision, while colonists saw the need to organize and defend common interests. One figure of emerging prominence among this group of intellectuals was the Pennsylvanian Benjamin Franklin. Earlier, Franklin had written to friends and colleagues proposing a plan of voluntary union for the colonies. That spring his newspaper, the Pennsylvania Gazette, published the political cartoon “Join or Die,” which illustrated the importance of union by comparing the colonies to pieces of a snake’s body.[[25]] The Albany Plan of Union won the delegates and lost everyone else: the colonial assemblies, sensing a check on their own authority and territorial claims, rejected it or ignored it, and the British government, having already dispatched General Edward Braddock to manage the war and the Indians, believed direction from London would suffice.

### Braddock
Braddock: Despite his participation in the Jumonville incident, Washington’s experience in the Ohio was just beginning. Later that year, news of Washington’s defeat at Fort Necessity broke like a thunderclap when it reached imperial officials in London. The British government intervened by sending Maj. Gen. Edward Braddock with two regiments of regular troops to accomplish what the colonists could not. Braddock, who had almost no experience on the battlefield and had never been to the colonies, reluctantly decided to allow Washington to accompany him on the campaign. The British forces intended to take possession of the French Fort Duquesne and eliminate the existence of the French altogether. As a distinguished military commander, Braddock dismissed both the colonial militia and Native Americans as incapable soldiers. Before leaving for the French fort, Braddock spoke with Benjamin Franklin, who warned the general of the guerrilla-style warfare strategy used by Native Americans — such warriors, Braddock answered, might trouble “your raw American militia,” but upon the king’s regulars “it is impossible they should make any impression.” In June of 1755, ignoring Franklin’s counsel, Braddock proceeded towards Fort Duquesne in a slow procession with cumbersome wagons and cannons, building a road as he went to supply the fort he expected to capture; Washington, wracked by dysentery, made much of the journey lying in a wagon. Ten miles from the fort, on July 9, about two hundred French regulars and Canadians with some six hundred Potawatomi, Ottawa, Huron, and Shawnee allies met the column at the Monongahela. The British van collapsed backward into the advancing baggage train; regulars fired volleys at war cries in the trees; the Virginians fought from behind rocks and were cursed by Braddock for cowardice. After hours of fighting Braddock was shot from his horse, mortally wounded — he asked Washington, whose coat was shot through and who had two horses killed under him, to organize the retreat, and died on the road four days later. Of some 1,400 men who crossed the river, nearly 1,000 were killed or wounded; captives were burned at Fort Duquesne. The disaster taught the colonials that their militia could outfight regulars in American woods, and it made Washington, “the hero of the Monongahela,” a continental name. Among the Iroquois he bore a revived name: Conotocaurius, Town Taker.[[26]]

### Pitt and Victory
Pitt and Victory: Despite having fewer men and no colonies to supply militia forces, the French dominated the first years of the conflict, largely due to their indigenous allies and strategic fighting styles. But in 1757, following two years of disastrous outcomes against the French in North America and raids reaching Philadelphia’s hinterland, King George II appointed the legislator William Pitt as Secretary of State and placed him over military operations. He believed North America was critical for England’s global domination. Pitt turned recruitment and supplies over to local authorities in America and promised to reimburse them for their efforts. He committed more troops, replacing old war heroes with vigorous young officers, and dedicated more royal funds to fight the French. Pitt, the “Great Commoner,” was known and feared throughout the world. In addition, Native Americans were not accustomed to traditional European fighting styles, choosing to refrain from open field battles. As the war progressed, Native American allies of the French quickly recognized the inevitable English victory, and began to abandon French fortifications. Only then did the Iroquois lean, late and carefully, toward the English, helping build Fort Stanwix on Oneida land in 1758 and screening the frontier. Pouring resources into the colonies, the British took advantage of their superior navy to cut off French supplies. Louisbourg fell in 1758, Quebec in 1759, and Montreal in 1760. England vastly outspent the French to secure victory, and the war officially ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1763.[[27]]

### 1763 — The Price of Everything
1763 — The Price of Everything: By the treaty France ceded its North American empire: Canada and every claim east of the Mississippi went to Britain, Louisiana west of the river and New Orleans to Spain, and Spanish Florida to Britain in exchange for Havana. England now possessed the continent from Canada to Florida and from the seaboard to the Mississippi. The victory was total, and it came dear. Unlike France, which kept its richest sugar islands and had financed its war by borrowing, England faced a fiscal crisis, and chose to recover the cost from the colonies the war had been fought to secure. George III — who had come to the throne in 1760 and viewed the colonials’ independent spirit with contempt — assigned Parliament the task of recovering the losses, which meant ending decades of lax enforcement. Under the mercantile system as it had actually operated, the burden of imperial taxation had fallen overwhelmingly on English taxpayers, who paid many times per head what the colonists paid; the era of salutary neglect had taught the colonies to mistake that circumstance for a right.[[28]] The colonies of 1763 were separate political and economic entities with decades of self-governance behind them: every colony had an elected legislature that voted its own taxes, colonial merchants had built fortunes on trade the Navigation Acts technically forbade, and when England began to enforce — on manufacturing, sales, shipping, imports — the merchants answered as they always had, with smuggling, and the assemblies answered with opposition. The colonies had tasted victory, believed they had proven themselves on the battlefield, and expected the standing of Englishmen. The war had also left them something more dangerous than confidence: military experience, veteran officers, and the discovery that direction from London could march an army into a river valley and lose it.

### Consequences — Native America
Consequences — Native America: The British victory in North America proved to be disastrous for Native Americans. With the exception of the Iroquois Confederacy, nearly every Native American tribe that fought in the French and Indian War had become significantly weakened; but the deeper loss was positional. Most tribes had grown accustomed to the favorable actions of the French, who respected indigenous cultures with gift giving and reciprocity, and by marrying into native clans and assimilating into their societies. The English, however, had treated Native Americans much differently. For over a century, native peoples had their lands taken either by crooked deals or by military force. And without the French to counter English aggression, Native Americans were left without any leverage, negotiating power, or military aid. As the new dominant power, British officials sanctioned the disruption of native economic and political systems, by aggressively occupying native lands, which threatened their very existence. Even the Iroquois Confederacy, who sided with the British against the French, witnessed their authority and previous agreements disappear. Consequently, thousands of colonists spilled over the Appalachian Mountains into the Ohio River Valley, because the Treaty of Paris excluded their lands from any English protection.

### Pontiac’s War and the Proclamation
Pontiac’s War and the Proclamation: Weakened from their war with England, most tribes were powerless to stop the rapid influx of English into their lands. And unlike the French, who followed native customs, the new Commander-in-Chief of North America, General Jeffery Amherst, announced an end to the exchange of gifts, stating that Native people must learn to live “without our charity,” further aggravating indigenous populations. In 1761, a Delaware prophet named Neolin was inspired by a religious vision to ask native people to give up their dependency upon European goods, like alcohol and tools, and return to their traditional ways. Neolin’s teachings impacted around twenty tribes of Native Americans, including the Seneca, Huron, and Shawnee people. One of Neolin’s most devoted followers was an Ottawa leader named Pontiac. While Neolin did not support Pontiac’s tactics of aggression and warfare, Pontiac was able to join together numerous tribes in resistance to the British partly because of Neolin’s unifying vision — though of the Six Nations only the Senecas joined, and the rest of the Confederacy did not. Pontiac’s War began in 1763 when Pontiac and several hundred followers attempted a surprise attack on Fort Detroit. While Pontiac’s siege on the fort was ultimately not successful, word of Pontiac’s actions spread and the war expanded far beyond Detroit, leading to at least eight British forts being captured. These clashes resulted in the death and capture of over 2,000 settlers in the Ohio Valley, while thousands more were forced to flee from their homes. At besieged Fort Pitt the garrison’s officers gave visiting Delaware emissaries blankets and a handkerchief from the smallpox hospital — an act Amherst endorsed in his correspondence, wishing the disease could be sent among the disaffected tribes; whether the blankets caused the epidemic that followed among the Ohio nations is uncertain, but the documented intent marks the episode as an attempt at biological warfare.[[29]] After three years of war, a resolution was reached between the British and the native peoples of the Ohio River Valley and Great Lakes Region, sealed with Pontiac himself at Fort Ontario in 1766.
The continuing violence and bloodshed along the North American frontier forced Parliament to respond with the Proclamation of 1763. This law essentially closed off all lands west of the Appalachian Mountains to English settlement. The Proclamation made enemies at both ends of colonial society. Frontier families who had fought for the Ohio country found the line closed against them, and speculators who had bought western land to resell, George Washington among them, watched their investments stall at the mountains. Rather than spend additional money and allocate more military resources, England chose to legislate all migration into Native American lands. In response, colonial settlers continued to venture west in violation of the law, while other colonists sought authority from local governments. And like their immigrant ancestors, most colonists held negative views of the indigenous, seeing them as unfit occupiers and obstacles to superior Englishmen. Despite the proclamation, conflict with Native Americans continued to plague the frontier and frustrate Parliament’s efforts to control the colonies. Nowhere did the collision cut deeper than in Pennsylvania. The Quaker elite lost the colony they had governed for decades, and with it their policy of accommodation. When the governor declared war on the Delawares, called out the militia, and offered bounties for Indian scalps, the pacifist Quakers in the assembly surrendered their seats rather than support the war. In doing so, they also surrendered their hold on Pennsylvania’s politics. In December 1763, while Pontiac’s War still raged, fifty armed men from Paxton destroyed the village of Conestoga, slaughtering men, women, and children who lived under the governor’s protection, and then asked the legislature to expel every Indian from Pennsylvania — the Indians’ “claim to freedom and independency,” they insisted, threatened Pennsylvania’s stability. By the 1760s Pennsylvanians had abandoned Penn’s “Holy Experiment,” and with it his promise of “true friendship and amity” with the colony’s native peoples.[[30]] Five years later the diplomacy of the new order showed its shape at Fort Stanwix. In 1768, Sir William Johnson, who was placed in charge of Indian Affairs, signed the Treaty of Fort Stanwix, meeting with over 3,000 Native Americans in an elaborate indigenous-style feast and negotiation settlement, where the British and the powerful Iroquois established a new line of demarcation. The new boundary provided native peoples with all lands north of the Ohio River, while providing the colonies of Pennsylvania, Virginia, and New York with additional room to expand — the lands ceded south of the river being the hunting grounds of Shawnees and Cherokees who were not at the table. The Proclamation of 1763 was issued not to protect native peoples, but to stop colonial land speculators from obtaining access to the resources west of the Appalachian Mountains; the treaty showed how quickly a line drawn to keep colonists out could be redrawn to let them in.[[31]]

## The Balance, Closed

For a century the empire had been a market — a wheel of ships and credit that enriched the colonies precisely because the state that owned it was too distant, too busy, and too much at war to govern them. In those years everything on the continent that looked like power was positional: the Iroquois neutral between two empires, extracting terms from both; the colonies autonomous between the letter of the Navigation Acts and the neglect of their enforcement; the Ohio nations courted because two kings wanted the same valley. Then the empire arrived as an army, and won — and victory, the one outcome no one had planned around, called in every position at once. France gone, the Iroquois no longer had the power to parley. France gone, the Crown had no reason to leave its colonies alone, and a war debt that forbade it. France gone, the Ohio country was no longer a prize to be bargained for but a possession to be occupied. Native nations discovered it first, in Amherst’s canceled gifts and the squatters on the Monongahela; the colonists would discover it within the decade, in the stamps and duties of a Parliament done with neglect. The two discoveries were the same discovery. For the French, the fight for the Ohio River Valley was over. For Native Americans, it had only just begun. The chapters that follow are the story of that fight.

## Then and Now

1. In 1763 the Crown drew a line along the crest of the Appalachians and told the colonists to stay on their side of it. London meant the line to keep the peace with the nations beyond it, and to keep the peace cheaply. Settlers and speculators, George Washington among them, saw a government three thousand miles away deciding what their war had won. Two and a half centuries later the same argument runs through southeastern Utah. In 2015 five tribal nations (the Hopi, the Navajo, the Ute Mountain Ute, the Zuni, and the Ute) asked the federal government to protect Bears Ears, land they hold as ancestral. A presidential proclamation drew the monument at 1.35 million acres in 2016. A second cut it by 85 percent in 2017, a third restored it in 2021, and a fourth, in July 2026, cut it to about 121,000 acres. The tribes are back in court. The line has been redrawn four times in ten years, and each time the argument underneath is the one the Proclamation of 1763 started: who gets to draw a line across western land?[[32]]
2. Test it. In 1763 the people on the far side of the line, the Native nations, never asked for it; at Bears Ears they did. Does that difference break the comparison, or is it the point? State and local officials opposed the 2016 monument as a decision made in Washington. Which side of the 1763 argument does their case resemble, and which side does the tribes’ case resemble? Or bring your own: find a line drawn on a map by a government far from the ground, and say who was asked before it was drawn.
