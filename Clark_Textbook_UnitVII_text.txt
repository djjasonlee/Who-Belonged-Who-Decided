UNIT VII
Written Into Law
The Reformers, the Enslaved, and the Law Between Them
~
At the turn of the nineteenth century, many Americans who opposed slavery, even some Virginia planters among them, believed the institution to be in its final stages. The evidence seemed to agree: tobacco had begun to fail, the profit in slave labor had fallen with it, and the Northwest Ordinance had closed the territory above the Ohio. Then the cotton gin arrived in 1793, Congress closed the Atlantic trade in 1808, and the enslaved population grew anyway: 700,000 at the first census in 1790, approaching 1.2 million by 1810, near four million by 1860, nearly all of them born on this continent. Two chapters cover those sixty years. One follows the Americans who set out to perfect the country and discovered what stood in their way. The other examines what the South built on that growth.
“The American Reformation” follows the revival out of the camp meeting and into the world. The Second Great Awakening taught Americans that society could be perfected one soul at a time. Its converts carried that promise into the saloon, the schoolroom, the asylum, and finally the slave pen, where it ran out. The wrongs they confronted ran beyond personal sins. They were written into law: the master’s claim to his slaves, the husband’s authority over his wife. Neither could be undone by individual repentance. And the closer reformers came to challenging those legal arrangements, the more they divided the nation they had set out to perfect.
“The Peculiar Institution” explains the economics behind slavery and the agrarian system, then turns to the people who lived inside it. The cotton gin did not simply revive a declining economic model. It expanded slavery westward, across Alabama and Mississippi toward Texas. After 1808, enslaved people could no longer be legally imported from Africa, so slaveholders increasingly bought and sold them domestically. The interstate trade broke apart countless families; by one estimate, it alone ended one out of every five enslaved marriages in the Upper South. Three of every four Southern white families owned no enslaved people. But the most consequential story unfolded within the system, despite policies designed to prevent it: a common language among people whose enslavers had engineered Babel, kinship networks built to survive the auction block, a faith that carried Moses into the fields, and a repertoire of resistance that ranged from the broken tool to insurrection.
The reformers and the enslaved approached the same question from opposite ends. The reformers challenged a title: who could hold one, and over whom. The people that title covered had stopped waiting for the argument to finish. They were building a language, a kinship, and a church inside the system that denied them all three. Both chapters also find fault lines within these communities, not just between them: house and field, lighter and darker, favored and driven in the quarters; competing churches and benevolent societies among the free; David Walker and Frederick Douglass divided over whether to appeal to the conscience of the country or strike at it.
The division that mattered most arrived first in the pews. In 1844, the Methodist Episcopal Church, the largest denomination in the country, split over whether a bishop could own enslaved people. In 1845, Baptists in the slave states withdrew to form the Southern Baptist Convention.[[1]] The churches divided over slavery before the nation did. If the sin was written into law and property, and the institutions built to perfect the country broke on it first, what was left to settle it?
## Notes
1. The 1844 General Conference of the Methodist Episcopal Church voted that Bishop James O. Andrew of Georgia, a slaveholder, should desist from his office; Southern Methodists organized the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, at Louisville in May 1845. Southern Baptists formed the Southern Baptist Convention at Augusta, Georgia, in May 1845.
