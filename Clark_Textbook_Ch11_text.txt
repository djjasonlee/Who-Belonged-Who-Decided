CHAPTER 11
The New Republic
The Federalist Decade and the First Transfer of Power, 1789–1801
“The alternate domination of one faction over another, sharpened by the spirit of revenge, natural to party dissension, which in different ages and countries has perpetrated the most horrid enormities, is itself a frightful despotism.”
— George Washington, Farewell Address, September 19, 1796
Chapter 10 ended on a rising sun and a promise of rights, and on a warning worth carrying forward: that the factions of the ratification fight would not dissolve in victory but harden into something the founders had no name for and no defense against. This chapter is where that happens. The Constitution the last chapter built was a machine of extraordinary ingenuity — but a machine is only a set of instructions, and it was silent on the men who would run it, silent on how a cabinet should work, silent above all on what to do when the people who had written it together discovered they wanted the country to become two different things. In a single decade, from Washington’s first oath in 1789[[1]] to the strange, tense winter of 1800–1801, the new republic answered the one question the Constitutional Convention had left open — not can the states be bound into a nation, which the last chapter settled, but can a nation this large, this free, and this divided govern itself without tearing apart. The answer came in two parts. The first was the discovery that the deepest fault line in America ran not between the states and the federal government, as everyone in 1788 had assumed, but straight through Washington’s own cabinet — between two men who agreed on almost nothing about what the country should become. The second came in 1800, when two men’s political rivalry put the republic to its ultimate test: whether power could pass from one party to another without bloodshed. Along the way, the Alien and Sedition Acts raised two fundamental questions: who could claim a place in the republic, and who had the right to speak against those who governed it. The election of 1800 would determine who authorized the answer.
## Part I · Executive Example
### Everything a Precedent
Beginning in 1754 as a colonial captain in the British Army, Washington would spend 38 of the next 42 years away from his beloved wife and Mount Vernon, to preserve the fragile experiment of America. Washington was unanimously elected President of the United States, with John Adams becoming Vice President. During his Inaugural Address, which was given behind closed doors to the members of Congress, Washington expressed his belief that the nation would only survive on its ability to self-govern. As a believer in a strong federal government, Washington favored any expansion of federal power. However, the new president strongly objected to the emergence of political parties, which he claimed inflamed division and internal conflict. As Commander in Chief and the nation’s Chief Diplomat, powers granted by the Constitution, Washington favored neutrality, preferring that America steered clear of choosing sides in any international conflict. As the first executive, Washington was forced to adapt on the fly to the needs of the nation, resulting in his decision to establish multiple precedents. And as the most revered and respected man in the country, Washington was a calming presence, providing much needed unity and stability for a nation that was experiencing unprecedented challenges, both foreign and domestic.
### The First Cabinet
As the first president, Washington was forced to establish several precedents, including the creation of several new departments within the executive branch, and appoint men to lead them. He authorized four departments—War, Treasury, State, and Attorney General. He appointed Henry Knox, Alexander Hamilton, Thomas Jefferson, and Edmund Randolph.[[2]] Following the creation of the presidential Cabinet, several other key issues needed to be addressed, including the existence of state and federal debt, but exactly how the federal government would resolve this issue became a serious point of contention. The Constitutional Convention revealed multiple, fundamental differences in political philosophy between advocates of a strong, central power, and state sovereignty. However, the question regarding the role of the federal government in the national economy proved to be the most divisive, fueling the establishment of two separate ideological groups.
### The Third Branch
Unlike the issues of state representation and the formation of an executive branch, the establishment of a federal judiciary went largely unopposed. However, as part of the three branches, the judiciary technically held as much power as Congress and the president. In 1789, Congress passed the Judiciary Act, which created a Supreme Court to serve as an appellate court, or a legal last resort where five associate justices and one Chief Justice could resolve disputes that the states could not. President Washington appointed John Jay as the first Chief Justice, who quickly became tired from serving, as the Supreme Court, also called the “riding circuit,”[[3]] spent most his time traveling from state to state on back roads and in bad weather, to resolve mostly local, petty arguments. After just five years, Jay resigned his post to become governor of New York. Throughout the 1790s, the Supreme Court had handled very few national cases and remained fairly weak in its power.
### The Forms of a Republic
Even the smallest questions had no answers, and Washington had to invent those too. How should a president be addressed? The Senate, in a fit of borrowed monarchism, proposed “His Highness the President of the United States of America, and Protector of their Liberties”; the House, and Washington’s own instinct, prevailed with the plain republican “Mr. President,” and the plainness itself became a precedent about what kind of office this would be. How often should the Cabinet meet, how should a president deal with the Senate on treaties, when should he use the veto, how should he leave office — each was decided once, by Washington, and then simply done that way afterward, because he had done it. The office of the American presidency is in large part the accumulated habits of its first occupant,[[4]] and Washington knew it, and shaped them with the care of a man who understood that the smallest gesture of the first president would bind the fiftieth.
## Part II · Hamilton’s Republic
### Faction Becomes Party
Perhaps the greatest limitation of the new Constitution was its inability to foresee the emergence of political parties. Unlike factions, which represent local, temporary disagreements among small groups of people, parties are designed to encompass large regions and cross gender, class, and ethnicity.[[5]] Furthermore, parties involve campaigns and mass propaganda. Whereas factions usually die with defeat, parties are designed to survive. In the eyes of the Whigs, parties corrupted the virtuous, democratic ideals of the Enlightenment by concentrating power in the hands of the political elite. However, the ratification of the Constitution was largely because of James Madison’s argument that a large republic, with a diverse population, would generate competing political interests, which would serve a natural system of checks and balances.
In theory, the existence of multiple factions would ensure that no majority could rule. Notwithstanding, the economic, diplomatic and political events of George Washington’s presidency would quickly divide America’s Congress and citizens into two ideological camps. Years after Congress agreed to the federal assumption of state debt, facilitated by an agreement to relocate the national capital to the Potomac, Jefferson admitted that it was the worst political decision of his life.[[6]] Hamilton’s political victory resulted in a domino effect of Federalist wins, including the raising of a foreign tariff, a tax on whiskey, and the charter of a national bank. Jefferson, as Secretary of State, felt compelled to challenge Hamilton and his Federalist agenda by organizing an opposition in the Congress and through a grassroots campaign to sway popular opinion in his favor. With the help of James Madison, the Jeffersonian Republicans, as they came to be known, recruited congressional leaders to their cause and utilized the power of the press to generate public support. In addition, local organizations openly criticized and challenged Federalist policies at every turn. Jefferson even undermined Hamilton’s stronghold of New York by recruiting several influential political figures. By 1796, America’s political landscape had been transformed into two rival, contentious parties; each striving to undermine the other.
### Two Men, Two Americas
The ratification fight handed the next decade its script: two competing readings of the same Constitution, and each reading gave a party its reason to exist in the 1790s. While the Constitution succeeded in providing a more powerful federal government, it remained vague in many regards. Among its limitations, was that it made no provision for an executive staff or Cabinet to assist the president. After receiving a unanimous vote in the Electoral College, George Washington became the first executive of the United States of America, and established the first Cabinet.
By utilizing the federal government to resolve the nation’s financial problems, Secretary of the Treasury, Alexander Hamilton, could realize his vision for America. Hamilton, born to a single mother in the West Indies, immigrated to New York, where he became an apprentice in a mercantile firm. Rising from obscurity, Hamilton gained valuable experience in the financial industry and at King’s College, where he studied prominent economists like Adam Smith. Hamilton, who constantly felt the need to prove himself among his peers, most of whom came from privilege, approached every venture with tenacity. Hamilton married into a wealthy New York family that deepened his ties to rich and powerful leaders in New York. Hamilton envisioned a strong federal government and a strong presidency, which would secure a wealthy, urban, mercantile, manufacturing nation fueled by financial securities and investments. Hamilton believed that the future of American stability lay in its capacity to engage in global markets, and desired to align the interests of wealthy capitalists with that of the government. In the debt crisis, Hamilton saw an opportunity to both strengthen the United States’ economy and the power of the federal government over the states.
Thomas Jefferson, who quickly emerged as Hamilton’s political rival, could not have been more opposite in his upbringing, career, and beliefs about the role of government. Jefferson, a product of agrarian Virginia, believed the future of American strength lay within the virtue of individual freedom, agriculture, and state sovereignty. Furthermore, Jefferson believed banks and securities to be the ultimate source of corruption and the nemesis of virtue. Before the ink could dry on the Constitution, Hamilton and Jefferson’s views of governmental power would quickly divide Congress, the states, and the nation.
### The Debt and the Defector
One of the pressing subjects of the debt crisis involved the redemption of government securities. And it was upon this issue that the Federalists lost one of their most significant contributors, James Madison. To help fund the war effort, the Continental Congress issued war bonds (IOU’s), which could be redeemed after the war for a higher price. However, following the American victory, a combination of mixed state currencies and large debts caused the value of the war bond to plummet to nearly a quarter to a tenth of its original value.[[7]] In desperation, many veterans sold their depreciated securities to speculative merchants and businessmen. In other words, imagine Apple stock was used to pay the soldiers of the Revolutionary War, but the stock crashed. Soldiers then sold their stocks for pennies on the dollar to stock traders who believed the price would eventually rise to its original level. Tens of thousands of citizens, including many of the Founding Fathers, owned war bonds, and demanded to be compensated. Hamilton proposed that the federal government redeem the war bonds at full value with funds generated by a National Bank.
James Madison, who also owned securities, argued that many veterans of the war, who sacrificed so much, had sold their bonds to vulture-like speculators, who would, under Hamilton’s plan, enrich themselves at the expense of war veterans. As Hamilton’s teammate during the ratification process, Madison helped argue for a strong federal government in the Federalist Papers.[[8]] Why now was Madison withdrawing his support and switching sides? Unlike Hamilton, who came from modest means, and earned his status in the urban, mercantile industry of New York, Madison was born into a life of privilege in agrarian Virginia. Like Thomas Jefferson, Madison accumulated wealth from slave labor. But in contrast to Jefferson, who believed in state sovereignty, Madison argued that a large republic would provide the most democracy to its citizens. Yet, despite Madison’s belief in a central power, Hamilton’s economic plans caused him to question how much power the government should hold over the nation’s finances.
### Strict and Loose
In his “Report on the Public Credit,” Hamilton recommended that the federal government assume the debt of each state, raise a new tariff on foreign imports, impose an excise tax on whiskey, and create a National Bank.[[9]] Anti-Federalists quickly objected to Hamilton’s proposal on the basis that any assumption of state debt would naturally create an imbalance in power. Furthermore, Hamilton’s opponents argued that the Constitution provided no such federal power to create a National Bank. Although Hamilton and Thomas Jefferson were crucial instruments in the formation of the country, they had never before worked together. As members of the Cabinet, both men now regularly met and debated their visions for America, spawning a new political rivalry. Among the suggestions of Hamilton’s report, it was the establishment of a National Bank that sparked the greatest controversy.
While Hamilton intended to strengthen the size and force of the federal government, Jefferson strongly opposed any increase in its capacity to control the states. Jefferson argued for strict construction, or interpretation of the Constitution, claiming that the government only had the powers that were enumerated (specifically listed or numbered). Hamilton, a loose constructionist, argued for implied powers:[[10]] a government told to do a job must also have been given the tools to do it, whether or not the Framers wrote the tools down. Hamilton's doctrine of implied powers was based on the Elastic ("Necessary and Proper") Clause of the Constitution, which states:
[The Congress shall have Power . . .] To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States, or in any Department or Officer thereof.
— U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 8, Clause 18
### The Dinner on the Potomac
Hamilton, a shrewd negotiator, needed to convince Jefferson and Madison to agree to at least part of his economic plan, believing that eventually he could gain the support of Congress. During a dinner arranged by Jefferson, Hamilton and Madison agreed to the federal assumption of all state debts in exchange for the relocation of the nation’s capital from New York to a federal district on the Potomac, carved from Maryland and Virginia.[[11]] All that remained for Hamilton was congressional permission to establish a National Bank. Hamilton argued that a federally supported bank could issue new bonds and draw investments, which would generate profits in the form of interest loans. Investors who purchased these public securities could make enormous profits when the time came for the United States to pay off these new debts. Finally, in 1791, Hamilton used his greatest advantage to secure his bank, George Washington. As a believer in Hamilton, and to the dismay of Jefferson, Washington signed Hamilton’s bank charter. Jefferson later resigned from the cabinet in frustration, but not before organizing a national opposition to the Federalist agenda. Hamilton eventually stepped down as treasury secretary in 1795, but the decade was clearly Hamilton’s. Hamilton’s economic plans bore early fruit. More than two hundred new corporations were chartered, state legislatures chartered some thirty new banks, and Europeans began investing in America. Nevertheless, Jefferson remained a serious opponent of the Hamiltonian vision. Hamilton’s joy over his successful political maneuvering would be short lived however, as his tax plan generated a violent, organized rebellion, eerily familiar to the former revolutionaries.
### The War of Words
The rivalry did not stay in the cabinet room; it spilled into print, and in doing so it built the machinery that would make parties permanent. Hamilton had his newspaper, the Gazette of the United States, to trumpet the Federalist line; Jefferson and Madison answered by installing the poet Philip Freneau in a State Department clerkship so he could edit a rival paper, the National Gazette,[[12]] to savage Hamilton and the administration from within the government itself. The two sheets went at each other with a ferocity that would make a modern partisan blush, and they were only the flagships of a growing fleet — by the mid-1790s, Democratic-Republican societies were organizing in cities and towns, drafting resolutions, coordinating opposition, mobilizing ordinary citizens into political life in a way the deferential politics of the colonial era had never imagined. Washington, who read the attacks on his government as attacks on the republic itself, was wounded and appalled. But what he was watching was not the republic’s collapse. It was its adolescence — the noisy, undignified, essential business of a free people learning to argue in public. The party press was the party system finding its voice, and the voice was loud.
## Part III · The Tests of Force and the World
### The Whiskey Rebellion
In order to generate revenue needed to pay off the federal debt, Hamilton proposed a per- gallon tax on whiskey. Favoring industry over agriculture, Hamilton chose to tax farmers, who sold their surplus of grain to distilleries for extra income. Unlike grain, which had a temporary shelf life, whiskey could be preserved and sold. However, the new tax eliminated these earnings. Farmers in western Pennsylvania, in a near replication of the Stamp Act, attacked tax collectors in a violent rebellion (sound familiar)? Washington, seeing an opportunity to demonstrate the power of the new federal government, federalized some 13,000 militiamen[[13]] and rode out at their head, with Hamilton at his side, to squash the rebellion. When the army arrived, the rioters returned to their homes, which ended in a bloodless confrontation. The Whiskey Rebellion of 1794 proved far more important for what it signaled than for anything that happened in the field. The new government had shown it would march soldiers to make its laws stick. The frontier got the message too: federal law came first, and local custom would have to give way. For plenty of Americans, though, 13,000 militiamen chasing off a crowd of angry distillers looked like overkill. Eight years after Shays’ Rebellion, the whiskey rebels tested the same question: how far could Americans push dissent before the government pushed back? Both times the answer came fast, and it came carrying muskets.
### The Revolution Across the Water
In the same year as George Washington’s inaugural year as president, France became engulfed in its own revolution. To the delight of Thomas Jefferson and supporters of global democracy, France successfully replaced its despot King Louis XVI, with a Constitutional Monarchy,[[14]] which emboldened the Republican Party and its argument against the growing power of the American federal government. However, any momentum gained from the French Revolution quickly evaporated as Maximilien Robespierre and his Revolutionary Tribunal imposed the infamous Reign of Terror. To the horror of Washington and the Federalists, thousands were ceremoniously executed. Even Jefferson, who initially conceded that revolutions required the shedding of some blood, could not condone the violence. Furthermore, France again engaged in another military conflict with England, which threatened to draw American support. Indeed France did petition the aid of the United States, and even went so far as to elicit the assistance of American merchant vessels to sequester British ships.[[15]] The war between France and England exposed an additional rift within the first Cabinet. While Hamilton, Adams, and other pro- Britain members of Congress encouraged Washington to side with their former sovereign, as Great Britain remained a critical component of America’s economy, Madison and Jefferson passionately encouraged the president to support a similar experiment in democracy happening overseas. Furthermore, many Americans felt indebted to France, who provided crucial aid to the colonies during the war for independence. Washington decided that the welfare of the nation overshadowed any foreign interests, and any alliance with a foreign power came with too much risk. Therefore, Washington declared the nation too young to engage in foreign affairs, and declared the Proclamation of Neutrality, a position of Isolationism. This infuriated Jefferson, who was a former ambassador to France and supported their revolution. John Adams, ambassador to England, supported the policy, which marked the beginning of the end of their once close relationship.
### Two Treaties
Although President Washington declared neutrality in the war between France and England, the economy of the United States depended upon British imports and investments in the newly established national bank. In addition, British forces remained stationed in the Northwest Territory, claiming that America had failed to comply with the Treaty of Paris, refusing to compensate Loyalists’ for seized lands. And to make matters worse, British authorities in North America allegedly incited Native American violence against American settlements on the frontier. Furthermore, Britain commanded the seas with a superior navy, and began sequestering American merchant ships, often removing cargo and impressing (forced service) American sailors into the Royal Navy.
In response, Washington sent Supreme Court Chief Justice, John Jay, to England to negotiate a settlement. Jay succeeded in creating a temporary agreement, where Britain agreed to cease antagonizing Native American violence in exchange for its status as a preferred trading partner. However, many Jeffersonian Republicans found the treaty unfavorable, as Britain did not agree to stop seizing sailors and U.S. goods. The Senate ratified the Jay Treaty in 1795 by exactly the two-thirds required, twenty votes to ten,[[16]] which created intense protest in America. Jay commented on the negative reaction saying he could “find his way by night from Philadelphia to New York by the fires of my burning effigies.” While Jay’s Treaty decreased the possibility of a war between the United States and Great Britain, the unresolved issues continued to strain the relationship between the two countries, leading to the War of 1812.
While the presence of British forces in the Northwestern Territory created conflict on the frontier, the western and southern borders of the United States led to tension between Spain and the United States. The United States now reached to the Mississippi, but Spain held both banks of the river’s lower course, and Spanish officials, uneasy about American traders and settlers swarming a vital borderland, closed it to American boats. In addition, the John Jay Treaty with England caused the Spanish Empire to fear an Anglo-American alliance that could potentially invade their lands in North America. As a result, Washington sent South Carolinian, Thomas Pinckney to Spain to negotiate another treaty. After relieving Spanish concerns over the Jay Treaty, The Treaty of San Lorenzo, as it came to be known,[[17]] enabled and encouraged American settlers to continue westward expansion, made frontier areas more attractive and lucrative, and encouraged trade through the port of New Orleans. That made the treaty a hit, nowhere more than in the West and the South. Pinckney’s Federalist ties paid a dividend as well: the treaty carried the party out of its New England base and into regions where it had never found much footing. Diplomatically, unlike the unfavorable concessions made in Jay’s Treaty, Pinckney’s efforts placed the United States in a stronger position in relation to European powers.
### The Sum of Eight Years
In summary, from 1789 to 1796, the “inventors of America” experienced several economic, political, and diplomatic catastrophes, each with the potential to destroy the new republic. Furthermore, the patriotic nationalism and civil cohesion created by the war with Britain quickly eroded, as diverging views and the formation of organized political opposition engulfed the nation. By 1796, the first cabinet had disassembled, with Thomas Jefferson and Alexander Hamilton retiring to private life, where they orchestrated their own designs for power. The Federalists and their counterparts emerged in competing factions, setting the stage for the first true experiment in the transformation of power, from the unquestioned and infallible Washington, to the winner of the next presidential election.
### The Farewell
In 1796, after serving two terms as the nation’s figurehead, Washington finally ended his service. At the time, the Constitution had yet to set term limits for the executive, which surprised and disappointed many that Washington would relinquish the presidency. In his renowned farewell[[18]] address, Washington implored the nation to remain true to the principles that made independence possible. Washington admonished Congress to maintain its foreign policy of Isolationism and to avoid the divisive and destructive nature of political parties. Following his shocking decision to step down as president, the nation quickly degenerated into political division and conflict, where the Federalists and Jeffersonian-Republicans immediately began to conspire and compete for power. The Election of 1796 revealed just how deep the political divide truly was, where John Adams defeated Thomas Jefferson by just three electoral votes, 71 to 68.[[19]] And under the rules of the Electoral College, the runner-up would become the Vice President, which highlighted the extent of the existing internal division. Consequently, the Adams administration was beset by crisis after crisis.
### Complex Characters
George Washington treated slavery as a problem to settle later: by the legislature, by degrees so slow no one would notice them, by the sale of western land that never sold, and finally by a will that took effect only after his death. Every act of opposition he postponed. Every act of enforcement he carried out at once. And at the end of his life he did what none of the other Virginia presidents would do.
In 1799, 317 enslaved people lived at Mount Vernon. Washington owned 123 of them. Another 153 belonged to the Custis estate as dower slaves, and neither George nor Martha could legally free them; the Washingtons rented the remaining 41 from neighbors. Washington inherited his first ten enslaved people at age eleven and bought steadily before the Revolution. In 1786 he wrote that he never meant “to possess another slave by purchase.” Two years later he accepted 33 enslaved people from the estate of Bartholomew Dandridge to settle a debt.[[20]]
The private record shows genuine opposition. In that same 1786 letter, to John Francis Mercer, Washington called it “among my first wishes to see some plan adopted, by the legislature by which slavery in this Country may be abolished by slow, sure, & imperceptable degrees.”[[21]] In 1794, in a note to his secretary Tobias Lear marked “Private,” he admitted holding enslaved people “very repugnantly to my own feelings” and hoped to sell his western lands to free them. The land never sold. Virginia law had permitted private manumission since 1782, and Washington could have freed the 123 people he owned at any point in his last seventeen years. He chose not to.
As president, he enforced slavery directly. On February 12, 1793, he signed the Fugitive Slave Act, which allowed a slaveholder to seize a person accused of escaping, in any state, and reclaim that person before a judge or magistrate without a jury. When Philadelphia became the temporary capital, Washington brought eight enslaved household workers with him. Pennsylvania’s Gradual Abolition Act freed enslaved people whom outsiders kept in the state for six months, and a 1788 amendment had already outlawed moving them out and back to beat the clock. Washington rotated them anyway, on the advice of Attorney General Edmund Randolph. His instructions to Lear on April 12, 1791, asked that the moves be “accomplished under pretext that may deceive both them and the Public.”[[22]]
Ona Judge, Martha Washington’s maid and a dower slave, refused to wait. On May 21, 1796, rather than become a wedding gift to Martha’s granddaughter, she walked out of the President’s House and boarded a ship for Portsmouth, New Hampshire. Washington pursued her for three years, first through the secretary of the treasury and the Portsmouth customs collector, and in 1799 through Martha’s nephew, whom he sent north to seize her and any children she had. Judge never returned.[[23]] She died in Greenland, New Hampshire, on February 25, 1848, still legally a fugitive. On Washington’s sixty-fifth birthday, February 22, 1797, his cook Hercules also escaped, and the family never recovered him.
The will, written in his own hand on July 9, 1799, ordered that the people he owned “shall receive their freedom” after Martha’s death. Freeing them sooner, he wrote, would create “insuperable difficulties on account of their intermixture by Marriages with the dower Negroes.” Only his wartime valet, William Lee, went free at once, with an annuity of thirty dollars a year. The will also ordered his heirs to feed and clothe the elderly and the sick for the rest of their lives, required that orphaned children learn to read and write and a useful trade before their freedom at twenty-five, forbade the sale of any of them out of Virginia, and charged his executors to see the clause “religiously fulfilled.” Martha freed the rest on January 1, 1801. The dower slaves stayed in bondage, and when Martha died in 1802 the Custis heirs divided them, separating at least twenty couples and families.[[24]]
The counterweight belongs on the same page. Of the four Virginia slaveholders who held the presidency, only Washington freed the people he owned; Jefferson freed five in his will, and Madison and Monroe freed none. He refused to sell people Mount Vernon could not employ, because sales broke up families. He also worked within real limits. Virginia’s 1782 law made an owner who freed the old or the very young responsible for their support, Mount Vernon ran chronically short of cash, and many of his people had married into the dower families he could not free. He signed the Slave Trade Act of 1794, which barred Americans from carrying enslaved Africans to foreign ports. During the Revolution he reversed his ban on Black enlistment, and thousands of Black men served in the Continental Army.[[25]] Washington never spoke publicly against slavery. In private he hoped to see it end, and in his will he ended it for the people he owned. The people who waited for that will, the people it left behind, and the people who freed themselves all belong in the same account.
## Part IV · The Second President
### Democracy’s First Test
George Washington’s retirement into private life set the stage for the first presidential election in an era of political parties. From 1789 until 1796, the Federalists, fueled by Alexander Hamilton’s economic and political agendas, scored multiple victories by strengthening the power of the federal government with the creation of a national bank, foreign tariffs, military power (Whiskey Rebellion), and international treaties. After surrendering his position as Secretary of State, Thomas Jefferson, with the assistance of James Madison, organized a congressional and national opposition to Federalist power and had positioned himself as the next heir to the executive office. Under Article II of the Constitution, the executive was to be chosen by electorates, chosen by state legislators, in a process known as the Electoral College. According to the Electoral College, each state was given a number of electors equal to the total of its congressmen. Furthermore, in the event that no candidate received a majority of votes, or that two candidates tied with a majority of votes, the House of Representatives was to decide the election, with each state, regardless of size, having a single vote. During the Constitutional Convention, James Madison successfully negotiated the process to satisfy the demand for a democratic election, while protecting against the collective ignorance of the American citizenry. However, unlike today, where presidential candidates select a “running mate,” as their potential Vice President, the Constitution mandated that whoever accumulated the second most electoral votes would become the Vice President.
Could the United States experience a bloodless transfer of power? The Federalist caucus in Congress settled on Adams and Thomas Pinckney, a South Carolina veteran of the Revolution and Washington’s envoy to Spain, as its candidates. Republicans countered with Thomas Jefferson and New York’s Aaron Burr, who had fought in the Continental Army and then, early in Washington’s presidency, held a Senate seat. Supporters of the four candidates campaigned vigorously, revealing the depth and organization of political machines. The Federalist press labeled Jefferson a Francophile (French loyalist), questioned his courage during the War of Independence, and charged that he was an atheist. In contrast, Jefferson’s campaign portrayed Adams as a monarchist and an Anglophile (British loyalist), who was secretly bent on establishing a family dynasty by having his son succeed him as President. When the electors finished, Adams had 71 votes to Jefferson’s 68, a three-vote margin. His support sat almost wholly in the North and Jefferson’s almost wholly in the South, an early sign of a country dividing by section. As runner-up, Jefferson became vice president. For the next four years, the president and vice president would answer to rival parties.
Despite President Washington’s admonishing of political parties, the election of 1796 revealed just how deep political factions and ideological differences had become. Two organized parties now competed for power, and with them came what historians label the First Party System. Colonial legislatures had their cliques, built around family, ethnicity, religion, and region, but nothing anyone would recognize as a party. Neither the state constitutions nor the federal one left any place for parties. Most Americans of the day regarded parties as factions in disguise, organized self-interest dressed up as public service. But a shared understanding of the public interest collapsed because of deep, ideological conflicts over Hamilton’s economic plan. Merchants and creditors lined up behind the Federalists, joined by Chesapeake planters who sold their wheat overseas. On the Republican side gathered the tobacco and rice planters of the South, western farmers nervous about debt, and the German and Scots-Irish settlers of the southern backcountry. Party identity had become crystallized by 1796. With the presidency at stake, both parties called caucuses in Congress and conventions back home. They courted ordinary voters with spectacle, too: Federalists raised glasses to Washington at birthday banquets every February, and Republicans took to the streets every Fourth of July to celebrate the Declaration. Federalists came out of the election with Congress in hand and John Adams in the President’s House. As president, Adams held to Hamilton’s tilt toward Britain and denounced France for seizing American merchant ships.
From issues ranging from the size of government and the election of the president, to solving the national debt and which European nation to support, both parties developed increasingly argumentative responses to each other. In a broader context, the Federalists supported the growth of business and industry, while the Jeffersonian Republicans envisioned an agrarian society that placed virtue upon self-reliance and small government. Jefferson warned against the evils of banks and the credit system, which empowered the wealthy to control both society and the government. Alexander Hamilton, the architect of the nation’s economic plan, strongly supported the establishment of new taxes (Whiskey Tax), the assumption of all state debt by the federal government, and a national bank, which could be used to earn international credit to secure loans. Ultimately it was Hamilton’s plan, which had Washington’s support, that won over Congress. But Hamilton’s victory came at a cost; a backroom agreement with Thomas Jefferson that would move the nation’s capital to Washington D.C., away from the merchant city New York. Nonetheless, Jefferson and his supporters continued to oppose Federalist policy and actively campaigned against Washington’s elected successor, John Adams. Although Adams and Jefferson worked as an effective team during the Second Continental Congress, the two men fell out during Washington’s two terms, specifically over their disagreement regarding support for France, the formation of a standing army, and domestic taxes.
### Adams Alone
In the Election of 1796, John Adams defeated Thomas Jefferson 71-68. Unlike the popular, stoic, and temperate gentleman, George Washington, Adams was argumentative, obnoxious, and disliked, even by many within his own Federalist Party. However, whereas Washington was self-admittedly inexperienced and unqualified for executing the office of the president, Adams was a refined politician and lawyer, who was well versed in the classical arts of history and political science. And like his predecessor, Adams possessed a keen intellect and was selflessly devoted to liberty and his country. In many ways, Adams was both capable and limited in his abilities as Chief Executive. Adams became president amidst several foreign and domestic challenges, including to a contentious, feverous political division. The pressures of serving during this partisan climate, combined with several political miscues, resulted in Adams serving only one tumultuous term.
### The XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War
The aftermath of John Jay’s Treaty placed the United States in a contentious position[[26]] between England and France. Negotiating from a position of strength, England became the clear victor of the treaty. In France, the end of the infamous Reign of Terror failed to achieve international peace. Feeling slighted by Jay’s Treaty, which provided England with the status of “preferred trading partner,” the French Navy also began to accost American trading vessels in the Caribbean and impressed American sailors into French service. In response, Adams sent a diplomatic envoy to Paris to negotiate an end to the hostilities. While in Paris, the American diplomats waited weeks before they would be seen by the French Secretary of Foreign Affairs. However, instead of meeting with the chief diplomat, he sent three of his representatives to meet with the Americans, where they demanded a bribe of $250,000 for the French foreign minister, Talleyrand, and a loan of $10 million to France before any talks could begin. In essence, the corrupt French government attempted to bribe, then extort the United States into a peaceful settlement. After a vehement rejection of the proposal, the American envoy was sent back. Upon arrival in New York, the American diplomats gave President Adams a detailed account of what took place in Paris. Adams (or someone in his administration) then leaked the official report to the Federalist press, where it referred to the three infamous French diplomats as X, Y, and Z. The sensational story praised Adams for refusing to accept such a deal. For the first time in his political career, Adams was universally cheered. Aside from scoring a massive political victory, the XYZ Affair mobilized Congress to allocate $2 million dollars to bolster the army and navy.
Furthermore, Adams’ actions brought national fame and support for the Federalist Party, while simultaneously demonizing the French sympathizers of the Jeffersonian Republicans. The Federalists had never been so popular, and the Republicans so despised. Unfortunately for Adams, he failed to capitalize on the momentum gained from the XYZ Affair by overplaying his hand. Rather than “take a knee,” Adams chose to press the narrative of Republican disloyalty by passing a series of controversial laws that ultimately erased any political gains Adams had made.
As President Adams battled partisan opposition at home, the conflict with France could no longer be ignored. The United States could afford a war with neither France nor Britain, and the two had been fighting each other, on and off, for most of a century. Both powers had designs on the young republic, still working out what kind of foreign policy it wanted and where it stood in the world. John Jay’s Treaty with England signaled to France that America had little interest in returning any favors, which essentially gave a green light for all French vessels to sequester and detain any American merchant ships. In addition, the heavily publicized “XYZ affair” rallied public in defiance against France’s attempt to extort American financial support, which empowered President Adams to seek Congress’ approval for the immediate construction of several navy frigates. As a result, American navy vessels engaged in multiple naval battles with the French. Known as the “Quasi War,” because Congress never officially declared war with France, between 1798 and 1800, American naval forces proceeded to battle against the French Navy. When Adams delivered his annual message to Congress in December 1798, the Quasi War took up a good part of it. Each side had signaled interest in a settlement, but, Adams told Congress, “hitherto… nothing is discoverable in the conduct of France which ought to change or relax our measures of defense. On the contrary, to extend and invigorate them is our true policy.” He even summoned George Washington out of retirement and gave him command of the army for the duration of the two-year Quasi War. Another reason for Congress’s decision to finance more naval vessels was the rise in attacks on American merchant ships by the Barbary states of North Africa,[[27]] which since 1785 had seized American ships and held their crews for ransom.
### The Alien and Sedition Acts
Federalist media coverage of the XYZ Affair[[28]] awarded President Adams and the Federalists with a significant political win. The pro-English faction achieved enormous momentum as defenders of American liberty and power. However, instead of capitalizing on the popular achievement, Adams chose to double down on his victory by targeting those perceived as French sympathizers through new legislation. A key component to Hamilton’s economic plan was to incentivize foreign immigration to the nation, which would theoretically provide the burgeoning manufacturing industry with a steady supply of cheap labor.
The decade’s temper reached the floor of the House itself. In February 1798 Vermont’s Matthew Lyon and Connecticut’s Roger Griswold fought there, Griswold with a cane and Lyon with a pair of fire tongs, after an exchange of insults the sources report differently. The House moved to expel both men and failed.[[29]]
However, the Naturalization Act of 1798 expanded the requirements to attain citizenship by increasing the number of years living in the country from 5 to 14. Furthermore, all immigrants who applied for citizenship needed to demonstrate their loyalty to the country. By limiting immigrants’ political access, Adams hoped to suppress any foreign support for his political rivals. In addition to regulating immigrant political power, Adams set his sights on domestic opponents through the Sedition Act, which curtailed all written, spoken, and published criticism of his administration by punishment of fines and/or imprisonment. The new law disciplined any language that was perceived as libelous to the Federalist cause, or which supported Jeffersonian Republican ideals. This new legislation opened the door for those sympathetic to the Anti-Federalist to vocalize their opposition. As both Republican and Federalist critics of the law were arrested and brought before Federalist courts, members of the Republican press portrayed Adams as a tyrannical despot who had violated the fundamental liberties of a free press and free speech. In 1798, Jefferson and Madison oversaw the passing of the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, which illustrated the oppressive actions of the government. Both state legislatures argued that the Alien and Sedition Acts violated the 10th Amendment, which justified the “nullification” of federal law.[[30]] These resolutions would later serve as the primary argument for states’ rights. The rhetoric of the Republicans quickly transformed the Adams administration from champions of progress into an authoritarian mob.
The arrest of some twenty-five individuals quickly rallied public support for the Republican cause. By punishing both immigrant and citizen opposition, Adams and the Federalists squandered all political victories. And by the election of 1800, Thomas Jefferson had positioned himself as the defender of Republican virtue. The aftermath of the Alien and Sedition Acts exposed the division within the Federalist Party and paved the way for Thomas Jefferson to ascend to the presidency. After 12 years of Federalist control, the nation would begin a new era of Republican control.
## Part V · The Revolution of 1800
### The Federalists Fall
Had President Adams simply enjoyed the early successes of his administration; he and the Federalist Party may well have extended their 12-year-long control of the executive branch.[[31]] However, by overreaching in an attempt to silence his critics, both immigrant and domestic, Adams reinvigorated Thomas Jefferson and the Republicans. Referring to Adams’ administration as the “Reign of Witches,” Jefferson highlighted the need for a new president who was committed to preserving individual liberties. By 1799, the political miscues of Adams and the end of French hostilities and paranoia, proved too much for Adams to overcome.
### The Midnight Appointments
The Midnight Appointments: By 1800, two competing political parties had formed, with the Federalists supporting a strong government and the Democratic-Republicans envisioned an agrarian society with independent states. President Adams recognized that he no longer had the support of Congress and the election of 1800 was almost certain to result in a loss for himself and his Federalist Party. Then, Chief Justice Oliver Ellsworth resigned, giving Adams an opportunity to appoint a Chief Justice who was sympathetic to his beliefs, John Marshall. Although Marshall had no previous experience serving on a court and was Thomas Jefferson’s first cousin, he was a familiar face and had established a strong reputation during the Constitutional Convention. With just over a month left in office, John Adams’s appointed members of the Supreme Court met for the first time in Washington D.C. and debated several issues until a new president took office.
Word of his defeat arrived in the same days as news from New York: his son Charles had died there seventeen days before. Neither loss caught Adams by surprise; he had watched both coming for a long time. He bore the grief with dignity in public and in private, but no one could comfort him. As the returns trickled in, Adams kept working, and the courts claimed most of his attention. Facing the loss of the presidency and Congress, the Federalists made one more push to lock in their hold on the federal courts, and the Judiciary Act of 1801 delivered it. The act cut the Supreme Court from six seats to five, carved the country into sixteen districts organized in six circuits, and added sixteen circuit judges; another act, signed later in February, opened the way for forty-two new justices of the peace[[32]] to serve the capital, a figure that alarmed Jefferson the moment he saw it.
By late February Adams had staffed the new courts with Federalists, and on his final night as president he went on signing commissions, the bulk of them for posts in the capital. Before dawn the next day, Adams climbed into a coach and set out for home in Quincy, Massachusetts. He skipped Jefferson’s inauguration altogether. Nicknamed the Midnight Judges, these last minute appointees became the topic of serious debate. Republicans had always regarded the 1801 Judiciary Act as a Trojan horse for federal power in the states, and they repealed it within a year. John Marshall, though, outlasted them all, presiding over the Supreme Court until 1835, a quarter century after Jefferson left office. The capital may have held no one else with the intellect and the political feel to beat Jefferson at his own game, and Jefferson resented Adams for putting him on the Court. His contempt for Marshall fueled his assault on the “Midnight Judges,” and the commissions Adams signed in his last hours in office became, for Jefferson, a personal insult.
### The Experiment’s Real Exam
In 1789 the question was whether the Constitution’s machine could be made to run at all; by 1801 it was running, and it had survived a rebellion, a foreign war scare, a near-civil crisis over free speech, and the retirement of the one man everyone trusted. But the true test — the one Washington’s Farewell had worried over, the one no republic of that size had ever passed — was the last one. On March 4, 1801, Thomas Jefferson took the oath of office, and power passed from the Federalists to the Republicans, from one party to its bitter rival, peacefully, by the counting of votes, without an army in the streets or a drop of blood.[[33]] Jefferson himself understood exactly what had been proven, and said so in his inaugural: “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists.” The quarrel the founders swore they did not want had come anyway, hardened into permanence, driven a president to jail his critics — and then, at the end, the system had held, and the losers had walked away to plan their next campaign instead of their next rebellion. That was the true revolution of 1800. In essence, the republic did the one thing very few nations had ever done before — it handed power to the other side without a shot. The founders had built a machine that could run without them, and could change hands without breaking. The experiment had passed its real exam. Its reward was to discover that the two-party quarrel it had just survived was not a disease to be cured but the permanent weather of American politics — the same weather, as we will see, still blowing hard through the Age of Jackson and every age after.
## Questions for Consideration
1. The chapter argues that the deepest division in the 1790s ran not between the states and the nation but “straight through Washington’s own cabinet.” Build the case from the evidence — the debt, the bank, strict versus loose construction — and explain why two men who had both fought for the Constitution came to want such different countries.
2. Washington loathed political parties and warned against them in his Farewell Address — yet parties formed on his watch, in his own cabinet. Was the party system a failure of the founders’ design, or an unavoidable feature of a free and diverse republic? Use Madison’s own ratification argument about factions in your answer.
3. The Alien and Sedition Acts and the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions were each, in their way, attacks on the constitutional order — one by the party in power, one by the party out of it. Evaluate both. Was nullification a legitimate defense of liberty against a genuinely oppressive law, or a dangerous doctrine that happened to be aimed at a good target? Consider where the doctrine goes next.
4. “The republic did the one thing very few nations had ever done before — it handed power to the other side without a shot.” Explain the claim. Why is the peaceful transfer of power between hostile parties treated here as a harder test — and a greater achievement — than the writing of the Constitution itself?
## Then and Now
1. This chapter’s newspaper war is the oldest American media story, and it has not changed as much as it looks. John Fenno’s Gazette of the United States carried Hamilton’s line. Jefferson and Madison answered by putting the poet Philip Freneau on the State Department payroll as a translating clerk, with a salary on the side,[[34]] so that he could edit the National Gazette and attack the administration from inside the government that was paying him. It ran from October 1791 to October 1793 and went after Washington’s government, Hamilton’s program, and eventually Washington himself. Neither sheet was trying to be fair. Objectivity wasn’t invented yet — the press was born weaponized. The two went at each other with a ferocity that would make a modern partisan blush, and the neutral press Americans now remember losing was the exception that arrived later.
2. Test it. Does a subsidized party sheet in 1792 really belong in the same sentence as a feed sorted by a company’s algorithm — or does the comparison hide the thing that changed, which is who chooses what you see? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a claim moving through your own sources right now, trace it back to whoever benefits from your believing it, and say how long the tracing took you.
