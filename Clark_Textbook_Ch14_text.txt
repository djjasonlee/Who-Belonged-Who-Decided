CHAPTER 14
The Age of Jackson
The Democratic Revolution and the Second Great Awakening, 1815–1840
“As long as our government is administered for the good of the people, and is regulated by their will; as long as it secures to us the rights of persons and of property, liberty of conscience and of the press, it will be worth defending.”
— Andrew Jackson
Between 1815 and 1840, the same revolution ran through the ballot box and the camp meeting. Ordinary white men captured American politics — tore down the property tests, built the mass party, elected one of their own and wrecked the White House furniture celebrating it — while ordinary Americans captured American religion, pulling salvation out of the hands of an educated clergy the way they had pulled politics out of the hands of an educated elite. The two captures were one movement, and they drew their circle in the same ink: wider than it had ever been for white men, and closed — now explicitly, by race and by sex — against everyone else. The white male majority now held the pen that drew that circle, and Indian Removal showed what it could do with it. Democracy in these years became the emblem of American freedom, and the age wears the name of the man who embodied every promise and every cruelty in it.
## Part I · The Democratic Revolution
### Context, 1824
By 1824, the prevailing government ideology of republicanism (rule by men of talents, virtue, and wealth) was quickly giving way to popular sovereignty, or rule by the people. Three forces unleashed by the Revolution gathered speed after the War of 1812: the market reached farther, Americans moved west in a flood, and political democracy came alive. Each process powerfully affected the development of American society. They also helped to reshape the idea of freedom, which slowly became more closely associated with economic opportunity, social mobility, and participation in the political system. But not all Americans embraced the expansion of democracy, nor were women and African Americans allowed access to such privileges. Many of the framers of the Constitution warned against the expansion of suffrage to men without property and took measures to prevent the inclusion of the less educated. However, as the United States expanded geographically, so did the number of eligible voters by eliminating the property requirement. By the 1820s, politics in the United States had evolved from a business of educated and wealthy elites, into a competitive sport for all white, adult males. The words, democracy and party politics, now consumed the common man, who actively participated in the political process. Whereas the political sphere had been reserved for men of status, this Democratic Revolution provided the opportunity for all white, adult males to campaign, vote, and even run for office. The expansion of political parties and their respective campaign efforts, built through newspapers, broadsides, and public debates, redefined the meaning of a democratic republic. The expansion of democracy facilitated the growth and activism of political parties, who sought to gain power and spread their ideologies. Yet as “freedom” stretched to cover more poor and rural white men, slavery kept defining the word by its opposite. In 1824, the young United States had grown from 13 to 24 states, yet slavery was also expanding. The same steamboats and canals that hauled the harvests of millions of farm families also hauled the cotton of a spreading plantation South, grown by enslaved hands. Slavery also fenced democracy in by color: the ballot, public office, and the public square stayed in white hands alone. Half a century after independence, liberty and slavery still grew up together, side by side, the central contradiction of American life.
### The Fall of the Property Test
Following the war for independence, class emerged as a new line of social demarcation. The state and federal legislatures, to this point, remained primarily from America’s upper class. However, the rhetoric of the Revolutionary War inspired many to challenge the notion that politics remain in the hands of the elite. The most significant obstacle facing the lower class and full access to voting rights was the requirement to own property. Many veterans of the Continental Army lacked property, and thus could not vote. For many Americans, the property requisite remained a form of tyranny, prompting eligible voters to endorse men of more modest means. However, attitudes about the definition of freedom were changing. The market revolution and territorial expansion helped create a third historical process in the evolution of American freedom; political democracy. The Revolution had opened the attack on property requirements for voting, and in the early 1800s that attack carried the field. Not one state that joined the Union after the original thirteen tied the ballot to owning property. As industry and commercial farming multiplied the wage earners in the cities and the older countryside, men without property insisted they had as much right as anyone to act as citizens, and their pressure did a great deal to open American politics. Maryland begrudgingly became the first state to remove property ownership as a requirement to vote. And by 1860, only one state still required that voters owned property.[[1]] Soon farmers and laborers in the South and West became full participants in their state and local politics and transformed the landscape of American politics forever.
### Tocqueville
By 1840, better than nine in ten adult white men held the vote. American politics was passionate, highly partisan, and sometimes violent, engaging massive numbers of citizens from all backgrounds. In a country so diverse in class, geography, religion, and ethnicity, political parties often provided a sense of unity and common identity. Alexis de Tocqueville, a French writer who toured the country in the early 1830s, went home and wrote “Democracy in America,” still the classic portrait of a society in political upheaval. Officially, Tocqueville came to study American prisons. He soon saw that no one could understand America without understanding democracy, a system he, as a privileged aristocrat, disliked. His central insight held that American democracy had become much more than the vote or a set of political institutions. Scholars call it a “habit of the heart,” a way of living that prized individual drive, assumed equality, and crowded public life with voluntary societies bent on improving the world.[[2]] Tocqueville saw that Americans had folded democracy into their very idea of freedom, and he recognized how deep a political shift that represented. The idea that voting rights could include masses of ordinary citizens was indeed a radical concept in Western Civilization. Aristotle had warned that democracy ends the same way every time: first anarchy, then tyranny. In America, though, democracy strengthened the sense of equality among those inside the political nation and widened the gap between them and everyone left outside it. Elections and their pageantry, the parades, bonfires, mass meetings, and party conventions, helped define who counted as the American “people.” More and more, the ballot became the badge of American citizenship.
### The Circle Closes
By 1830, just as the economic barrier to democracy was crumbling, a new obstacle began to rise in its place. While many landless farmers, frontiersman, and even newly arrived European immigrants were being given the right to vote, new requirements to participate in American politics emerged in the form of race and gender. The “principle of universal suffrage,” declared the “United States Magazine and Democratic Review” in 1851, meant that only white males of age could be allowed to vote. For the millions of American women who actively participated in the American Revolution and in the War of 1812, universal suffrage still remained out of reach. Even after being charged with the sacred responsibility of raising and educating their children, women were believed to be physiologically incapable of understanding the complexities of politics, and thus dependent upon the wisdom of men to decide political matters. But unlike women in America, who were never allowed to participate in politics, many of the original thirteen colonies did allow free African Americans to vote. During the Revolutionary Era, only South Carolina, Virginia, and Georgia banned African Americans from voting. And as late as 1800, not a single northern state placed any voting restrictions on African American males. And where Europe sorted people by rank, white Americans of every class dressed alike, rode the same coaches and rail cars, and checked into the same hotels. Yet at the same time, men and women of African descent were increasingly becoming a separate group. In 1821, New York rewrote its constitution to remove property tests for white voters while imposing a $250 freehold requirement on Black voters alone. And by 1860, only five New England states, home to only 4% of the free African population, still allowed for African Americans to participate in elections.[[3]] Racial inequality ran deep, yet many white Americans of the revolutionary generation had still counted free Black people as “citizens of color,” members of the wider public. In the early nineteenth century, however, race and gender came to define the political nation more and more. In essence, race and gender had replaced class as the boundaries between those Americans who were permitted to enjoy political freedom and those who were not. In addition, just as skin color became a method to divide American politics, it simultaneously provided a way for many of the diverse groups of European origin to quickly assimilate into the nation. In a country that had come to equate the ballot with freedom, the contrast stung: in some states a white immigrant man could vote almost the day he landed, while nearly every free African American, and every woman, from families rooted in the country for generations, stood outside politics altogether.
### Party Politics
Although factions and political parties had already been established, the business of politics had largely been reserved for the colonial elite. The extension of suffrage to all adult, white males created unprecedented political organization and participation. The new political arena was extremely contentious and competitive because it attracted ambitious men from all walks of life. Despite George Washington’s view and warnings concerning political parties, partisanship in the United States evolved and took newer, more divisive forms.[[4]] Whereas the Federalists and Antifederalists openly campaigned for and against ratification of the Constitution, these debates remained largely the business of the political elite. However, by 1830, parties had evolved into political machines, guided by lawyers and professional journalists, who utilized the power of newspapers, broadsides, handbills, and public forums to engage in vigorous debates over government policy. One issue in particular that produced significant contention was the expansion of slavery into the Louisiana Territory.
## Part II · The Hero
### Waxhaws
Much like Alexander Hamilton and Benjamin Franklin, Andrew Jackson rose to national prominence as a “self-made man” without having been born into privilege. As a descendant of Scotch-Irish immigrants, Jackson was born in 1767, in a small town called Waxhaws along the Carolina frontier. Jackson’s father died before he was born, forcing his widowed mother to move him and his two brothers to live with relatives. As a boy, Jackson experienced several violent encounters with the Cherokee and Creek tribes, and later when the British invaded the Carolinas during the Revolutionary War. His older brother Hugh died at Stono Ferry in 1779, and Andrew, only thirteen, joined the local militia as a patriot courier. In 1781, Andrew and his older brother Robert were captured by the British and were confined to a primitive jail cell. While in captivity, a British officer gashed his left hand and slashed his face with a sword because the young boy refused to polish the Redcoat’s boots. The Jackson brothers were later released after their mother successfully negotiated with a British officer, but while in captivity, the brothers contracted smallpox, which Robert would die from just days after his release. Shortly after Robert’s death, Andrew’s mother contracted Cholera and died, leaving him an orphan at 14 years old.[[5]] Jackson’s early experiences with Native Americans and the British caused the young man to retain intense feelings of hatred and vindictiveness, which would manifest themselves later in his life.
### The Rise
Raised by his uncles, Jackson began studying law in his late teens. He won admission to the bar in 1787 and, at twenty-one, became public prosecutor for North Carolina’s western district. In 1788 he moved to the frontier town of Nashville, where legal fees slowly turned into land and land into wealth. He helped write Tennessee’s constitution in 1796, and voters sent him to Congress as the new state’s first representative. He was elected to the U.S. Senate the following year but resigned after serving only eight months. In 1798, Jackson was appointed a circuit judge on the Tennessee Supreme Court, serving in that position until 1804. In 1802, Jackson was appointed a major general of the Tennessee militia, leading only a handful of skirmishes against the local native tribes.[[6]]
The War of 1812 gave Jackson his shot at revenge against the British. He spent five months leading U.S. troops against the British-allied Creeks, whose assault on Fort Mims, in present-day Alabama, had left hundreds of settlers dead. The campaign ended at Horseshoe Bend in March 1814, where some 800 Creek warriors fell, and the Treaty of Fort Jackson that followed stripped the Creeks of more than 20 million acres in present-day Georgia and Alabama.[[7]] Victory earned him a major general’s commission in the regular army. However, Jackson’s greatest military victory came during the Battle of New Orleans. Even though Britain and the United States had formally agreed to end the war, word had not yet reached Jackson, who vigorously recruited men from all backgrounds to defend New Orleans. His force soon grew into a 4,500 man patchwork army of regular soldiers, frontier militiamen, free African Americans, New Orleans aristocrats and Choctaw tribesmen. Given the nickname, “Old Hickory,” for his stubborn determination and willingness to fight along the frontlines, Jackson ultimately turned back an invading British force of 8,000 soldiers, saving the coveted port city and striking a definitive blow to the British.[[8]] As a result, Jackson’s bravery and efforts transformed the famed Indian fighter, also known as “Sharp Knife,” into a national celebrity. Also known as the Hero of New Orleans, Jackson became the greatest military hero since George Washington. Jackson had promised land to the free men of color who fought beside him. After the victory, at the request of New Orleans’ white residents, he ordered the Black troops out of the city they had saved. Their service earned a removal instead of a reward.[[9]] Jackson’s humble beginnings and his legendary courage quickly earned him the title, “Champion of the common man.” Old Hickory would later run for President of the United States, becoming the first president born outside Virginia and Massachusetts.
## Part III · The Elections
### 1824 — The Corrupt Bargain
Ever since 1800, Thomas Jefferson’s Republicans held a locked grip on the presidency. In fact, after the Federalists committed political suicide by organizing the Hartford Convention in the midst of the War of 1812, the Republicans remained virtually the only functioning political party with anybody to elect. However, by 1824, the Republican Party had divided into the National Republicans and Democratic Republicans, ending the Era of Good Feelings. The Jefferson Revolution and the Virginia Dynasty controlled the White House for 24 years. During that time, the successor to the presidency was chosen in a small caucus of the Republican Party, where the president would nominate his Secretary of State, followed by his confirmation. Jefferson nominated Madison, Madison nominated Monroe, and now it was Monroe’s turn to nominate his Secretary of State, John Quincy Adams.
But Monroe had his reservations about Adams. Although Adams belonged to the Republican Party, he resembled too much a Federalist. Moreover, other Democratic-Republicans with Federalist views, Henry Clay and William Crawford, decided to run as well. As Speaker of the House, Henry Clay expected no candidate to reach the electoral majority the Twelfth Amendment demanded. Thus, the decision would fall to the House of Representatives, where Clay believed he would emerge as the elected president. What Clay failed to anticipate was the addition of another candidate, the famed Indian fighter and Hero of New Orleans, Andrew Jackson. After the election, it was Jackson who emerged as the winner of the popular vote, however he failed to garner a majority of electoral votes. And according to the 12th Amendment, only the top three vote getters would go on to House of Representatives. Clay finished last, and was disqualified. It was now time for Clay to support one of the remaining three candidates. William Crawford had suffered a stroke before the election. That left Andrew Jackson, Clay’s most dangerous rival, and John Quincy Adams.[[10]]
Although Adams was an educated, New Englander, from privilege, Adams and Clay shared similar visions of a highly developed and successful transportation system. Clay often referred to this plan of his as the “American System.” Clay conjectured that neither candidate would win enough electoral votes, but before the House cast its vote, Clay invited John Quincy Adams to a dinner, where he inquired about his position on the use of federal power to achieve his plans to improve the country’s infrastructure, known as the “American System.” Clay then threw his support behind Adams, who defeated Jackson in the final vote. Shortly after his victory, Adams named Clay as his Secretary of State, which created intense controversy. Suspecting a backroom deal had taken place between Adams and Clay, Jackson’s supporters quickly accused them of negotiating a “corrupt bargain.”
The corrupt bargain tarnished the reputations of both Adams and Clay. Adams tried to prove his innocence by including Jackson’s supporters in his administration, only to have them sabotage it. Adams got no support for implementing the National Republican economic agenda. Jackson, furious at the perceived pact, gathered his forces, including assistance from Martin Van Buren, to prepare for the election of 1828.
### 1828 — The First Modern Campaign
By 1828, the number of states had doubled and the nation’s population tripled, with the expansion of voting rights to adult, white, men. By removing wealth and property qualifications to vote, significant numbers of men, from all walks of life, actively and vigorously engaged in the political process for the first time. After Andrew Jackson’s humiliating defeat in the House of Representatives on February 9, 1825, Jackson, Van Buren, and Calhoun launched the largest political campaign in American history.[[11]] The Jacksonian Democrats presented the Hero of New Orleans in large crowds, chanted his name, “All Hail Old Hickory.” Martin Van Buren operated a revolutionary campaign for nearly four years. Before this election all presidential campaigns consisted of a few gatherings of the nation’s elite, caucuses, private meetings, and “friends” who produced anonymous editorials in newspapers and other advertisements. Candidates rarely appeared in public, however during the final months of the campaign trail, Van Buren, John C. Calhoun, and Jackson made frequent appearances. Another transformative aspect to the election was the use of negative information and propaganda, to slander, libel, and spread disinformation. Called Mudslinging, political opponents would spread negative information via newspaper, handbills, or word of mouth. And just like sports, there are “unwritten rules” in politics, and personal attacks must never include any wives and/or children.
However, the intensity and bad blood that developed over four years since the Election of 1824, spilled out onto newspapers, broadsides, parades, protests, poems, and songs. Whereas Jackson was portrayed by his followers as a rough-hewn, frontiersman, and champion of the “common man, “his opponents desperately tried to insult his personal life by going below the belt. They labeled his mother a prostitute, his wife an adulteress, and made little black coffins out of handbills with the names of all those who Jackson had brawled with, dueled with, or executed as an army Commander. Unfortunately, the unprecedented amount of vitriol and degradation used to smear Adams, and Jackson found its way into Rachel Jackson’s Nashville Republican & State Gazette. The attacks on Rachel caused her such emotional pain, that she passed away just 3 weeks after the election.[[12]] The election of 1828 marked a watershed moment in American politics; a shift from republican politics to democratic politics. Democracy was the answer to distrust. Following Jackson’s victory, two parties emerged; Jackson’s Democrat Party and Henry Clay’s Whig Party.
Jackson’s 1828 presidential campaign revolutionized the election process by expanding suffrage, utilizing the press, town hall meetings, and well timed personal attacks. Jackson won an overwhelming victory, especially among new voters. He was supported by an alliance of anti-tax, anti-bank, and anti-corporate farmers and workers. Ironically, Jackson himself was a slaveholder and a wealthy man. But Jackson appeared as the enemy of great concentrations of power and the friend of territorial expansion.[[13]] The vigorous campaign against Jackson included several personal attacks, and even the slandering of his wife, Rachel, who unknowingly remained married. The election of 1828 marked a watershed moment in American politics. In 1828, the right to vote, campaign, and even run for office energized the nation as a whole because politics was no longer restricted to the upper classes. In 1789, President Washington admonished the nation to remain neutral abroad, and stay united at home, no partisan nonsense. However, Van Buren, like Madison, argued that the very existence of factions guarantees the rule of the majority and the protection of minority’s rights.
### The People’s House, Literally
In contrast to the private swearing-in ceremonies of previous presidents, thousands flooded the capital to celebrate the swearing in of the champion of the “common man.” Jackson’s inaugural reception resembled a collection of farmers, laborers, and artisans, who shared Jackson’s humble, rugged background. After the oath and the inaugural address, Jackson headed back to the White House to greet a line of politicians, dignitaries, and plain citizens. The line soon swelled past 20,000, and the mansion’s dignity vanished under the crush. Guests stood on the chairs in muddy boots and prowled the rooms in search of the president, shattering china and glass and grinding food into the carpets. To lure them outside, the staff hauled tubs of juice and whiskey onto the lawn.[[14]] In addition to becoming the first president from the West, Jackson set other precedents as the Chief Executive, by instituting an internal administrative revolution. Roughly one federal officeholder in ten — nearly 1,000 officers, from the cabinet down to the postmasters — were replaced by Jackson’s personal friends, including Martin Van Buren, Samuel Ingham, and John H. Eaton.[[15]] Jackson insisted on conducting the appointment process by the spoils system, rewarding those loyal to his campaign efforts with government positions. The Andrew Jackson presidency ushered in a new, broader, and more democratic era in American politics. Using his popularity as a mandate, Jackson transformed the national government by increasing executive power, destroying the mercantilist and nationalist American System, and supporting Indian removal. Other examples of Jackson’s unconventional customizing of the executive branch include the appointing of close friends to lofty government positions, and the removal and rotation of government office holders from previous administrations.
### The Kitchen Cabinet
From his first days in office in 1829, Jackson’s official Cabinet broke into warring camps, driven above all by the feud between Calhoun, the vice president, and Van Buren, the secretary of state. The feuding all but paralyzed the Cabinet, and Jackson nearly stopped convening it. He relied instead on an informal circle of trusted friends and advisers, whom opposition editors mocked as the “Kitchen Cabinet.” Francis Preston Blair was a valued member. The Kitchen Cabinet, a term first used as a derogatory description of Jackson’s circle, played an important role in the Jackson administration until 1831. When a reshuffled official Cabinet finally began to function, the Kitchen Cabinet faded away.[[16]]
## Part IV · King Andrew
### The Tariff of Abominations
After the War of 1812, a series of tariffs, taxes on imported goods, were enacted. The duties existed to protect American manufacturers from cheap British imports. America’s young factories could not yet compete with British prices. The first protective tariff came in 1816, and Congress pushed its rates higher in 1824. The 1828 tariff started life as a scheme to embarrass the Adams administration. Still smarting over the “Corrupt Bargain,” Jackson’s allies in Congress, with Vice President Calhoun in their corner, drafted a bill loaded with heavy duties on imports that North and South alike relied on, betting it would go down to defeat.[[17]] By failing to pass the tariff, President Adams, it was assumed, would be blamed for the failure, thus damaging him politically. However, the strategy backfired when the tariff bill actually became law on May 19, 1828. Every section of the country found something in the bill to despise, and the South despised it most. Later that year, in the Election of 1828, Jackson soundly defeated Adams in a landslide victory 178 to 83 electoral votes. As a Southern planter and slave owner, Jackson was expected, as president, to pursue policies more in line with the interests of his fellow southerners. Jackson’s choice of South Carolinian, John C. Calhoun, as Vice President, created a feeling of relief and excitement among Southern congressional leaders.
### Nullification
Southerners counted on Jackson to repeal the “Tariff of Abominations,” or at least lower it, and to stand up for them better than John Quincy Adams ever had. Protective tariffs widened the sectional divide: higher prices for manufactured goods fattened Northern factories and squeezed Southern slaveholders, who had to buy those goods. Southerners also feared that foreign governments would retaliate with duties of their own on the South’s raw materials. And when the tariff cut British sales to the United States, British merchants had less money to spend on American goods, cotton above all. Less British money meant less cotton sold, and the Southern economy slid deeper into trouble. By 1830, issues over tariffs and slavery drastically increased the amount of sectionalism within the United States. Jackson, who received northern support during his election for endorsing of the Tariff of 1828, now as president, needed to decide whether or not to support a congressional reenactment of the tariff. As the only state with an African American majority population, South Carolina vehemently opposed extending any tariffs on British imports, which would disrupt its cotton exports. For the South, their outcries went far beyond a simple tariff. The recent congressional debate over Missouri, British Parliament’s declaration that slavery in the West Indies be abolished by 1833, Southern planters, with vivid memories of Northern legislators arguing to forbid the westward expansion of slavery, and a slave uprising in 1822 forced Southern planters to visualize the finality of their economic and political existence.[[18]]
Despite the South’s open condemnation, Congress reenacted the Tariff of 1828. Later that year, South Carolina took the lead in protesting against the tariff. In a pamphlet, The South Carolina Exposition, (written by Calhoun) the tariff was denounced as unjust and unconstitutional as the Alien and Sedition Acts. Going beyond the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions, South Carolina explicitly proposed that the states should nullify the tariff; declare it null and void. Under Calhoun’s plan, a nullified law could come back to life only with a two-thirds vote of Congress and ratification by three-fourths of the states; even then, the nullifying state could choose to accept it or to secede.[[19]] Few beyond South Carolina found Calhoun’s argument persuasive. Southern interests were aghast at Jackson’s approval of the tariff. John Calhoun wrote two major protests advocating state nullification of the tariff. Calhoun delegated defense of nullification in the Senate to Robert Hayne, who was out-argued by Daniel Webster.
In April of 1830, Calhoun’s allies staged a dinner to celebrate Thomas Jefferson’s birthday and to gain support for the nullification movement. Van Buren, Eaton, and the president were also in attendance. Jackson raised his glass, looked at Calhoun, and toasted, “Our Federal Union: it must be preserved.” Calhoun rose to answer, and witnesses said the glass trembled in his hand: “The Union — next to our liberty, the most dear.”[[20]] Calhoun, humiliated, would resign as Vice President two years later. Following the infamous toast, Jackson worked to promote agricultural interests, and also fought any federal involvement regarding manufacturing, commerce, and infrastructure. In May, 1830, Henry Clay helped to pass legislation to fund the Maysville Road project, a highway located solely within Clay’s home state of Kentucky. Jackson once again used his veto power to kill the bill, leading his political opponents to illustrate Jackson’s continued abuse of his executive power.
For two years the doctrine waited while Jackson governed. In 1832 Congress passed a new tariff that lowered rates but kept the principle of protection, which to Charleston remained the whole offense. That November, South Carolina’s convention declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null and void within the state and threatened secession if the federal government tried to collect. Calhoun resigned the vice presidency in December to defend nullification from the Senate. Jackson answered with a proclamation that treated nullification as treason by another name, asked Congress for a Force Bill authorizing the army to collect the revenue, and privately offered to hang the first nullifier he could reach. Henry Clay built the exit: a compromise tariff that stepped the rates down over a decade, passed alongside the Force Bill in March 1833. South Carolina repealed its nullification of the tariff and, as a parting shot, nullified the Force Bill. Both sides claimed victory. The Union held, this time.[[21]]
### Misfire
The man who offered to hang the first nullifier came by his temper honestly. His parents had sailed from Carrickfergus, in Ulster, in 1765, part of the Scots-Irish stream that carried a borderer’s code of honor into the Carolina backcountry. The backcountry gave Jackson the temper; his climb into the planter gentry gave him the pistols, because the formal duel belonged to gentlemen — only men of standing were thought to have honor worth defending. And the code was not spread evenly across the country. Its heyday ran from about 1750 to 1850, and it was taken most seriously in the South, where a breach of honor was thought to cost more; in New England it was treated as a cultural throwback. Jackson’s own reputation as a duelist is mostly legend: he stood as a principal in only two true duels. In the first, in 1788, he wrote the challenge on the blank leaf of a law book and delivered it himself; both men fired into the air rather than at each other, shook hands, and left unhurt. In the second, in 1806, Charles Dickinson — a noted marksman who had insulted Rachel — put a bullet in Jackson’s chest; Jackson kept his feet, killed him, and needed months to recover. In January 1835, days after Calhoun reportedly told the Senate that Jackson was “a Caesar who ought to have a Brutus,” a house painter named Richard Lawrence, convinced that he was the rightful King of England and that “King Andrew” alone stood in his way, stepped from behind a pillar at the entrance to the Capitol Rotunda and fired at the president’s heart. It misfired. Lawrence drew a second pistol. Jackson, sixty-seven, charged him with his cane held high, and the second pistol misfired too. Lawrence’s insanity was plain at his trial, and he lived out his life in asylums. Whether a regional code of honor can outlive the conditions that made it is a question this book takes up twice more: once against a controlled experiment, and once on a Civil War battlefield, where the claim has been made and contested.[[22]]
### Indian Removal
As a boy growing up on the frontier of western Carolina, Andrew Jackson experienced multiple violent encounters with Native Americans. Living in close proximity to the Cherokee and Creek nations, Jackson’s primary interests involved protecting his community and land speculation. To Jackson, the presence of Native Americans hindered westward expansion and made acquisition of land extremely dangerous. Jackson’s view of the indigenous peoples took shape in bloody conflict. Furthermore, Jackson lamented the federal government’s inadequate Indian policies, which in his mind, empowered Native American resistance and failed to achieve any assimilation into white society. As the major general of the Tennessee militia, Jackson led several military operations, including the victory over the Creek “Red Sticks,” and Seminoles in Spanish occupied Florida. Jackson quickly earned a reputation as an Indian fighter and champion of westward expansion. Jackson’s experience with Native Americans, in both peaceful negotiations and war, reinforced his conception of the indigenous as socially inferior and subordinate to Anglo civilization. Furthermore, it is argued by many historians that Jackson’s commitment to Indian removal was rooted in his vision for America’s expansion, and not from any racist sentiment.
By 1828, American settlements in Georgia began to encroach upon Cherokee lands, which provoked Cherokee leaders to seek relief from the federal government. In an effort to prohibit further occupation of their lands, the Cherokee Nation drafted a constitution and declared themselves independent of Georgia law. According to the Constitution, Native American societies were sovereign lands within the United States, and thus exempt from state authority. As early as October 1823, the Cherokee council told federal commissioners seeking more land that it was “the fixed and unalterable determination of this nation, never again to cede one foot more of land.”[[23]] The Cherokees had done all that Georgia had demanded, such as adopting many aspects of American culture, including agriculture, urban living, clothing, written language, a written constitution, education, and even the ownership of slaves. They expected their sovereign treaty rights to be respected. In December 1829, while addressing the nation, Jackson remarked on the present and future state of American Indians in the United States. His message rehearsed the same assumptions and prejudices about Native peoples that American policymakers had carried since Jefferson’s presidency. White settlement, Jackson argued, kept shrinking Native hunting grounds, and with them the tribes, who would slowly disappear. His solution, dressed up as a favor to the tribes, called for moving them west of the Mississippi.
The Indian Removal Act was presented to Congress, which if passed, would authorize the removal and relocation of several Native American tribes. Debates in both the Senate and the House were quite contentious. Those opposed to Jackson's plans had many reasons for concern. Some opponents felt genuine sympathy for the tribes, and many argued passionately that Native nations held a sovereignty no state could violate. Others wanted to protect the old treaty process, and more than a few simply could not stand Jackson. Generally, those opposed to the bill constituted the emerging anti-Jackson party. The bill passed the Senate, 28 to 19, in late April and squeaked through the House, 102 to 97, in late May. On May 28, 1830, Jackson put his name to the Indian Removal Act, arming the president with new power to speed the removal of eastern Native communities to lands beyond the Mississippi. In a desperate attempt to retain their ancestral home, the Cherokee Nation filed for an injunction in the Supreme Court. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831), Chief Justice John Marshall threw the case out: the Cherokee, he ruled, formed one of the “domestic dependent nations,” not a foreign state, and so had no standing to sue. A year later the issue came back through a side door. Georgia had jailed Samuel Worcester, a missionary living among the Cherokee without the state’s permission, and in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) Marshall held that no state could override a national treaty and that Georgia law carried no force inside the Cherokee Nation. Georgia defied the ruling, and Washington lifted no finger to enforce it. Jackson, attentive to Georgia’s stubborn claims of state authority in an era when the states carried enormous weight, had no intention of enforcing the decision, and he reportedly said, “The court made its decision, now let them enforce it.”[[24]] This Indian Removal Act was Jackson's creature, and a manifestation of his long-held belief in America’s destiny to expand westward.
### The Trail of Tears
While the notorious forced-removal of the Cherokee Nation is popularly associated with the term, Trail of Tears, nearly 20 other indigenous tribes suffered similar fates. But before Indian Removal became an official policy passed by Congress, presidents of the United States, namely Washington and Jefferson, urged Native Americans to become “civilized,” that is, to forgo their traditional ways of life and adopt White, Christian customs. As president, Thomas Jefferson urged native peoples to seek assimilation, that in time the two races would “mix bloodlines” and become equal participants in the dream of America. Many large tribes believed in the prospect of coexisting with Whites by learning English, converting to Christianity, and giving up tribal wars. In the South, the Five Civilized Tribes, like the Cherokee, Choctaw, and Creek, pursued these measures in hopes that they could retain their sacred ancestral lands. But the American Revolution forced many tribes to choose sides between the British and the Americans, with those who opposed the United States facing significant retribution following the British defeat. The Creeks, Cherokees, and Seminoles were forced to make large land concessions for supporting the British. The War of 1812 too caused many tribes who opposed the U.S. to face similar persecution.
As the United States expanded through war and the purchase of the Louisiana Territory, the government saw a solution to remove the remaining indigenous tribes living in the east by offering new lands and subsidized provisions to all who relocate willingly to the Oklahoma Territory west of the Mississippi River. While some tribes took the U.S. up on this offer, the majority of native peoples held deep connections to their ancestral lands and were quite upset by the proposal. Nonetheless, with famed “Indian Fighter” Andrew Jackson as president, Indian Removal now had a strong advocate in the White House. In May 1838 the removal of the Cherokee began. Federal troops and state militia moved into Cherokee country and forced more than 16,000 Cherokee out of their homes across Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina. Soldiers penned them in makeshift roundup camps, then in three emigration depots, and from there the army sent them west. The Cherokee “Trail of Tears” exacted a catastrophic price. More than a thousand died on the march, the old, the very young, and the sick most of all; hundreds more broke away from the columns; and an unknown number, several thousand by some estimates, died later from what removal had done to them.
The Indian Removal Act gave the Cherokee and Creek Nations two years to prepare and relocate to the Oklahoma Territory. Yet the vast majority of Creeks and Cherokees refused to concede, given the support of the Supreme Court and the belief that the next President of the United States would have compassion upon them. While most chose to remain, several leaders of the Cherokee Nation signed over their lands without permission of the majority, which was against their laws. Major Ridge and Elias Boudinot, the most wealthy and prominent of the Cherokee, packed up and moved, leaving 16,000 Cherokee to face the might of the U.S. Army. On May 26th, 1838, three days after the deadline set by the Treaty of New Echota, 7,000 soldiers and state militiamen and members of the Georgia militia began rounding up the Cherokee, forcing them into cattle pens without any of their belongings.[[25]] And rather than immediately begin the trek westward, some Cherokee leaders objected to traveling during the “sick season,” opting to remain in their lands until October. Thus, thousands of Cherokee were kept in concentration camps, animal stockades, kept under watch by U.S. troops. For months, the indigenous suffered hunger, exposure, and disease from living in such primitive conditions. In October, the first group of Cherokee refugees were forcefully marched west, during one of the worst winters ever recorded. The numerous groups were often trapped by deep snow, ice, and frozen rivers, being forced to spend days suffering in bitter cold and little food. Disease, exposure, and brutal treatment from the soldier escorts caused dozens of Cherokee deaths each day. At every stop, the Cherokee were forced to bury their dead. The trek to Oklahoma took several months and nearly 2,000 miles. Nearly 1 out of 4 Cherokee died on the Trail of Tears, killing most of the elderly and small children.[[26]] Once arrived in Oklahoma, the Cherokee and Creek were forced to build from scratch new homes, schools, and farms, all while facing brutal violence from the Native Americans who already lived there. The Cherokee and Creek Nations did not receive a formal apology from the U.S. Government until 2009.[[27]]
### The Bank War
Perhaps the last significant achievement of Federalist ideas, carried out by Republican hands, was the chartering of the Second Bank of the United States. In 1816, Congress issued another 20 year-long charter for the national bank. The B.U.S. provided investment opportunities for both domestic and foreign investors. With plenty of specie (gold, silver) on hand, the B.U.S. could provide funding for projects that Congress could not afford. Furthermore, the national bank could demand payments in specie from smaller banks, which prevented them from overextending loans or printing too much paper money. No bank in America had more power than the B.U.S., which greatly irritated and concerned the Democrat Republicans. Because the national bank was a private institution, it could not be held accountable by the people. Instead, an elite circle of wealthy investors operated the day to day business, free of any government regulation. In addition, the bank often foreclosed on many western farms, draining the West of money and resources. Essentially, many Democrats believed the bank to be a weapon for corruption and that it operated for profit, not public service. The power of the B.U.S. outraged Democratic-Republicans. Even though the Second B.U.S. loaned money to smaller banks and oversaw their operations to make sure they didn't engage in unreasonably speculative activities, many still feared the concentration of so much money and influence in a privately operated institution. Furthermore, its first president, William Jones, helped trigger the Panic of 1819 by offering credit to purchase western lands at inflated prices. When the price of cotton collapsed in 1819, the bank was forced to call in its debts, causing a rapid depreciation in the dollar. Out of that crisis, a Philadelphia financier named Nicholas Biddle rose to run the Bank of the United States. In 1832, Henry Clay and Biddle proposed a bill to renew the national bank’s charter, which set to expire in 1836. As Jackson’s chief political rival, Clay chose to push for renewal four years early and make it an issue for the upcoming presidential election. Clay intended on guiding the bill for renewal through Congress, where it would end up on the president’s desk. Clay believed if Jackson signed the bill, he would alienate the majority of his supporters. On the other hand, if Jackson vetoed the bill, he would potentially lose the support of northern, wealthy, influential circles who helped him win the election of 1828. While Jackson did not hate all banks, he disliked the centralized, monopolistic B.U.S. for two reasons. First, Jackson believed specie, that is, gold and silver, was the most stable currency, not paper banknotes. Second, a bad speculation in the 1790s had burned him badly, and he never trusted banks again. Jackson vowed to obliterate the B.U.S., “to put his foot upon the head of the monster and crush him to the dust.”[[28]] As expected, the bill to renew the bank’s charter was speedily passed by Congress only to see Jackson kill the proposal with his veto power. To justify the veto, Jackson argued the bank to be unconstitutional, despite the ruling of the Supreme Court in the case of McCulloch v. Maryland. In his veto message of July 10, 1832, Jackson refused to accept the Court’s ruling as the last word: “The Congress, the Executive, and the Court must each for itself be guided by its own opinion of the Constitution. Each public officer who takes an oath to support the Constitution swears that he will support it as he understands it, and not as it is understood by others.”[[29]] Jackson’s overruling of an official Supreme Court decision only worsened his credibility and reinforced his image as an autocrat.
Jackson turned the veto into a campaign weapon. His veto message painted the bank as a tool of the rich and a servant of special privilege. He urged farmers and working men to stand with him against it, arguing that easy money and paper notes hurt the poor and set off crashes like the Panic of 1819. Although Jackson successfully utilized the Bank War to secure his reelection, the B.U.S. still had four more years to operate until its charter expired. Although Jackson defeated the proposed renewal of the bank’s charter, Jackson’s ultimate goal was to terminate the B.U.S. all together by instructing the Secretary of the Treasury, William Duane, to remove all of the federal deposits and relocate the funds into smaller local and state banks called, “Pet Banks.” Duane refused to comply with Jackson’s controversial decision, resulting in his removal from the Cabinet. Jackson then appointed his friend and attorney general, Roger Taney, as Secretary of the Treasury, where he began to facilitate the transfer of federal funds.[[30]] In response to Jackson, Biddle began to call in all the debts owed to the B.U.S., hoping to generate another financial crisis, proving the need to renew the bank’s charter. Biddle’s gamble backfired as Jackson used the actions of the B.U.S. to enhance his position, thus “killing the bank” in 1836. Following the end of the B.U.S., Jackson issued an executive order, Specie Circular, which was meant to stop land speculation caused by states printing paper money without proper specie (gold or silver) backing it. From then on, public land had to be bought with gold or silver. Speculation froze, and sales of federal land dropped off a cliff. As a result, the value of the dollar deflated, causing a credit crunch and the panic of 1837.[[31]]
### The Jackson Era
Jackson’s landslide in 1828 announced a new mass democracy, along with the party machines that fed on its patronage. Jackson’s simple, popular ideas and rugged, frontier persona reinforced the growing belief that anyone could hold public office. The “Tariff of Abominations” and the nullification standoff with South Carolina laid bare a widening sectional rift and a deepening Southern fear for slavery’s future. Jackson exercised unprecedented power of the presidency against his opponents, particularly Calhoun and Clay. He cast the Bank of the United States as a monster of concentrated money and destroyed it after a fierce political struggle. The victory hardened his followers’ distrust of elite financial power, yet it left the country with no working national banking system. Jackson’s presidency also focused on issues of westward expansion. The Native nations of the Southeast had taken up farming and schooling on a broad scale, hoping to show that they could live within American society. But pressure from white settlers and from the state governments proved overwhelming, and Jackson supported the forced removal of all southeastern Indians to Oklahoma along the “Trail of Tears.” In Texas, American settlers rose against Mexico and won their independence. Jackson extended recognition to the new republic but, wary of the fight over slavery, turned down its bid to join the Union. His opponents soon organized as the Whig party, only to lose in 1836 to Martin Van Buren, the successor Jackson had chosen. However, Jackson’s controversial economic policies ultimately ruined the unlucky Van Buren, as the country plunged into a serious depression following the Panic of 1837. The Whigs turned the hard times and the campaign style of the new democracy to their own use and elected a hero of their own in 1840. Their win marked the rise of a new two-party system.[[32]]
### Complex Characters: Andrew Jackson
Andrew Jackson divided the world into his own people and everyone else, and he gave his own people a loyalty few public men have matched. The habit started early. His father died weeks before his birth, and the Revolution took his two brothers and his mother before he turned fifteen. The orphan grew into a man who held on hard to the people he claimed.
He married Rachel Donelson in 1791 and defended her name for the rest of his life, including in an 1806 duel that left a bullet in his chest. When she died on December 22, 1828, weeks after his election, he blamed the campaign slanders against her, and her niece Emily Donelson ran the White House in her place. He could reconcile as fiercely as he fought. In a Nashville street brawl in 1813, Jesse Benton shot Jackson in the shoulder while Jesse’s brother, Thomas Hart Benton, fired at him and missed.[[33]] A decade later Thomas Hart Benton sat in the Senate as Jackson’s most important ally there.
The circle widened to the ordinary white men who voted for him. The states had already dropped most property tests for voting, but Jackson made those voters the center of a national movement. When he vetoed the recharter of the Second Bank in 1832, his message spoke for “the humble members of society—the farmers, mechanics, and laborers—who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves.”[[34]] Voters returned him to office by 219 electoral votes to 49. His loyalty ran to the Union as well, and when South Carolina nullified the tariff in 1832, he warned that disunion by armed force meant treason. In January 1835 his administration paid off the national debt, the only time in American history the debt has reached zero.[[35]]
The circle could stretch in an emergency. In September 1814, with a British army bearing down on New Orleans, Jackson called the city’s free men of color to arms and promised them the same bounty in money and land as white volunteers. On December 18 he told the Black battalions, “I expected much from you,” and then, “you surpass my hopes.”[[36]] When the emergency passed, the circle closed again. White residents asked him to send the Black troops out of the city, and he did.
For the people he owned, the circle never opened. Jackson bought and sold enslaved people throughout his adult life, and he held about 150 by the end of it. In 1804 he advertised for a man who had escaped, offering $50 for his return and “ten dollars extra, for every hundred lashes any person will give him, to the amount of three hundred.”[[37]] The Native nations stood outside it too. The Treaty of Fort Jackson in 1814 took more than twenty million acres from the Creek, including land belonging to Creeks who had fought on the American side. As president he signed the Indian Removal Act of 1830, and when the Supreme Court ruled for the Cherokee in Worcester v. Georgia, he wrote a friend that the decision “has fell still born.”[[38]] The army carried out the Cherokee removal under his successor in 1838 and 1839, and about four thousand of some sixteen thousand people died.
The hardest case sits between the two. After the Battle of Tallushatchee in November 1813, Jackson’s soldiers found a Creek infant beside his dead mother. Jackson sent the boy home to the Hermitage and wrote Rachel that “he as to his relations is so much like myself I feel an unusual sympathy for him.” He raised Lyncoya alongside his own son and hoped to send him to West Point. The boy apprenticed instead to a saddler in Nashville and died of tuberculosis in 1828, in his mid-teens. The affection appears real. It brought one Creek child inside the circle and left his nation outside it.[[39]]
The same hand drew the circle wider and drew it shut. The people inside it knew Jackson as the most loyal man in America, and the people outside it knew him as Sharp Knife.
## Part V · The Democratization of Heaven
### The Second Great Awakening
At the turn of the 19th century, religion in America had diminished as a primary social institution. Roughly only 10% of Americans belonged to a formal church.[[40]] This is not to say that most people were not spiritual, but that they only chose not to affiliate with a specific religion. Several factors contributed to the decline of organized religion. First, the teachings and philosophy of the Enlightenment remained relatively fresh in the minds of many. Its emphasis on rationalism and the popularization of intellectualism, science, and reason, provided a fresh perspective on how individuals could improve their lives without the restraints of a clergy or religious figurehead. The Enlightenment led to the spread of Deism, made famous by Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Jefferson, which proclaimed that God was the “Great Clockmaker.” God created the world, or wound up the clock, and simply let it go, refusing to interfere in mankind’s existence. Two other significant belief systems emerged during this time, called Universalism and Unitarianism. These religions rejected the idea of predestination and the divinity of Jesus Christ, who was believed to be just a preacher. These religions deemphasized the necessity to rely on God and placed more emphasis on the individual. Another reason for a decline in church attendance was that during post-Revolution society, a significant effort was made by the Framers of the Constitution to separate church and state. Favoring a more secular nation, no official religion was recognized and people were given the freedom to worship, or not worship, in any way they chose. Lastly, as the United States expanded its territory westward, more and more people ventured out into the frontier, creating a highly transient society. Rarely did people stay confined to the towns and villages of their youth, opting instead to live largely outside of any organized society or religious community.
### The Revival
However, much like what Americans experienced during the mid-1700s, many became concerned over the spiritual state of their communities. People feared that America was descending into a faithless society full of unbelievers. In addition, the spread of democracy and its ideals of self-reliance and self-determination, started to be applied to American spirituality and religion, taking the form of a new movement called Evangelism. The Methodists and Baptists, two denominations that had grown out of the First Great Awakening, now outpaced the Presbyterians.[[41]] These sects started to send preachers and missionaries into the rural communities of America to essentially recruit new members. While they didn’t share common doctrines, they did possess three similar ideas. One, that Deism and rationalism must be rejected as false. Two, that the belief in predestination was also wrong. Rather, they stressed the idea that all people could earn God’s grace through obedience and good works, giving individuals more control and autonomy over their lives. This was particularly popular because it meshed perfectly with the spread of American concepts of personal liberty and the belief that anyone could partake in the blessings of freedom to pursue a better livelihood. Lastly, these religions demanded piety and obedience that should be proudly and openly practiced.
These new religions utilized large, outdoor gatherings called revivals, where preachers acted almost like theatrical performers, inviting large masses of people to participate in the receiving of the Holy Spirit. These revivals were characterized by passionate displays of emotion, like fainting, rolling around on the ground, and speaking in tongues. Because of its egalitarian nature, the Second Great Awakening attracted many of society’s second-class citizens, namely women and African Americans, both slave and free. The Market Revolution provided young women with new opportunities to leave the limiting constraints of farm life in order to pursue financial independence and more social freedoms. Thus, as women experienced new opportunities outside of the home, they naturally gravitated to these new religions, which offered them new positions of responsibility and the ability to participate in organized activities. The egalitarian nature of the Second Great Awakening was also particularly attractive to African Americans, who relished its emphasis on spiritual freedom and salvation. For many African Americans still trapped in slavery, the idea of spiritual freedom fueled beliefs in their eventual release from slavery. The story of Moses leading the Children of Israel out of Egypt was particularly impactful. A renewed spiritual fervor swept across rural America. The Second Great Awakening’s democratic and egalitarian appeal gathered thousands to hear its message and created a shortage of messengers. Converts needed preachers and meetinghouses, and so did the seekers who had joined no church at all but came to listen and judge for themselves. In a nation with no official religion, that space stood open to anyone, and preachers competed to fill it. Each read the same Bible and drew a different lesson from it, and from those rival readings came new denominations, offshoots, and sects. One region of America that became exceptionally popular for revivals was upstate New York, which earned the name as the “Burned over district,” because of the frequency of preaching and its scorched earth effect. One young seeker near Palmyra, Joseph Smith, later recalled “priest contending against priest, and convert against convert,” and asked, “Who of all these parties are right; or, are they all wrong together?”[[42]]
### Finney and the Perfect Society
Charles Grandison Finney emerged as the Second Great Awakening’s most popular preacher. Once a lawyer by trade, Finney experienced his own conversion one evening while reading the Bible to prepare for a case. Finney, much like George Whitefield of the First Great Awakening, captivated audiences with his bold personality and theatrical flair.[[43]] Finney became popular for his preaching of “free will,” which emphasized that everyone was their own moral agent, capable of receiving salvation on their own. That message rang loudest among the descendants of New England Calvinists, who believed that God had already chosen those who would be saved. The doctrine of predestination often bred a deep sense of fatalism and despair. Finney offered optimism and agency. More importantly, as Finney and other preachers proclaimed the power of the individual, they also emphasized the possibility of perfection. Perfectionism, or the continual striving to be free from sin, would not only provide a personal safeguard against eternal damnation, but could collectively usher in the millennium, or the start of 1,000 years of heaven on earth. In essence, by perfecting each other, people could speed up the second coming of Jesus Christ. The rapid popularization of the Second Great Awakening caused millions of Americans to renew their commitment to spiritual salvation and drastically increased the amount of church membership. By 1840, church membership had multiplied severalfold within a generation, and tens of thousands of men and women had taken up preaching.[[44]]
The “Age of Democracy” expanded belonging by class while narrowing it by race and gender. As property qualifications disappeared, nearly every white man gained full standing in the republic. The ballot became the defining mark of political citizenship, but its boundaries stayed sharply drawn. Women remained excluded. Free Black men who had once voted lost that right in several states. The Cherokee faced a different exclusion: the United States denied their sovereignty despite treaty guarantees and a Supreme Court ruling, then sent soldiers to drive them from their homelands. The age widened the electorate, but it also defined who could claim the rights of citizenship, whose sovereignty the nation would recognize, and whose claims it would override.
## Questions for Consideration
What were the main features of the Democratic Revolution? Explain how democratic and republican values and competing conceptions of national identity shaped political debates and produced the second party system.
The chapter argues that as the class barrier to political participation fell, barriers of race and gender rose in its place. Trace that exchange through the evidence — the property-test collapse, New York’s 1821 constitution, the five New England states — and explain why the two movements happened together rather than by coincidence.
“Champion of the common man” and “King Andrew the First” describe the same presidency. Build the best case for each title from the record — the Bank War, nullification, the spoils system, Indian removal — then defend the one you find more accurate against the strongest version of the other.
Some historians argue Jackson’s removal policy was rooted in his vision of national expansion rather than in racial animus. Using the Cherokee record in this chapter — the “civilization” campaign, Worcester v. Georgia, New Echota, the Trail of Tears — evaluate whether that distinction matters, and to whom.
How did the Second Great Awakening and the Democratic Revolution echo one another? Consider doctrine (free will against predestination; the franchise against property tests), method (the revival against the rally), and audience — and explain why the people excluded from one flooded into the other.
## Then and Now
The survivors rebuilt in Oklahoma, on land that already had people on it, and then they waited. The federal apology came on December 19, 2009 — a hundred and seventy-one years after the Trail. It had been introduced again and again, passed the Senate in 2008 and again in 2009, reached the President attached to a defense appropriations bill, and was signed without a ceremony or an announcement. The resolution apologizes on behalf of the people of the United States to all Native Peoples for official depredations, and the same text states that nothing in it authorizes or supports any legal claim.[[45]]
Test it. Does an apology delivered that late, in that vehicle, with that disclaimer attached, do anything at all — or is the manner of its delivery the whole story? Or set it aside and bring your own: find an apology an institution made for something it did, and say what giving it cost the institution.

40. On Franklin’s Deism, see Chapter 6. The one-in-ten figure counts membership. It descends from Roger Finke and Rodney Stark, whose 1988 article “American Religion in 1776: A Statistical Portrait” put only 10 to 12 percent of the 1776 population in a church, reached by multiplying 3,228 congregations by an assumed 75 members each against the white population; their 1992 book The Churching of America shifted from membership to adherence and arrived at 17 percent. The low count draws serious dissent. Patricia U. Bonomi and Peter R. Eisenstadt, counting “member, adherent, parishioner, and auditor,” found in the William and Mary Quarterly (1982) that “56 to 80 percent of the [white] population were churched,” southern colonies at the low end and northern at the high; James Hutson, then of the Library of Congress, recalculated in 2003 at about 71 percent and showed that the 75-member average drew partly on fledgling Methodist and Baptist rolls and an 1826 Presbyterian figure; George Marsden, reviewing Finke and Stark, held that they “take the lowest possible estimate and then become dogmatic about it.” The range reflects a definitional dispute rather than a margin of error, the same one Chapter 6 turns on, where full communion and attendance stand apart. No party to the dispute denies the direction of travel.
