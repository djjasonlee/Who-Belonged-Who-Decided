CHAPTER 15
The American Reformation
Religion, Reform, and Division, 1820–1848
“Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will.”
— Frederick Douglass
The early 19th century was a period of considerable change in the United States. Economic, political, and social transformations radically transformed how Americans thought about themselves, their communities, and their nation. Democracy and opportunity reached into every corner of the country, and Americans looked to the future with something close to certainty. Yet the same years bred conflict, because the rewards of industry and democracy fell unevenly, divided by gender, race, and class. Settlers on the frontier lived farther from the cities than ever, even as new means of travel and communication shrank the distance between them. Nearly every white man could now vote, but crowded cities and a surge of European immigrants sharpened the tensions between classes. Americans greeted these changes with both excitement and suspicion, and many wondered whether the young nation’s moral fabric could survive them. As a result, more and more Americans turned to two powerful tools to help them make sense of their rapidly changing world: spiritual revivalism and social reform.
New avenues of social mobility blended with an emerging sense of religious optimism and desire to improve the human condition. The rise in religious consciousness spurred a collective desire to strive for a utopian society, rid of sin, vice, and suffering. Women’s rights, prison reform, temperance, and abolition became popular movements between 1800 and 1840 as a result of a new societal focus on spirituality and salvation. Not every crusade won, but the reform impulse, and the revival that powered it, gave antebellum America its character.
## Part I · The Engines of Reform
### Context, 1820 — Three Engines
Reform did not arrive from nowhere; reform movements seldom do. They are set in motion by a crisis, and in the decades after 1815 three of them arrived at once. The first was the Market Revolution, the subject of an earlier chapter, which carried most Americans out of a world where three of every four families grew enough to feed themselves and little more, and into a world of wages, cash, and the first business class — Americans who could spend money on anything they wanted, because for the first time they had money to spend.[[1]] The second was the democratization of politics: the property tests fell, the franchise opened to nearly all white men, and an orphan from the Carolina backcountry became president, the country’s first zero-to-hero story. The third was people — a boom in population and, by the 1840s, the largest wave of immigration the nation had yet seen. Each engine produced prosperity, and each produced problems, because when a society increases in size and wealth it also increases in trouble. The result was the very first time American society looked at itself and said it needed to do better.
### Immigrants and Nativists
From 1820 to 1860, more than five million immigrants landed in the United States, Irish, German, and Jewish newcomers among them, chasing a fresh start and a better wage. By the Civil War, about one American in eight had been born abroad. The Irish took the hardest, dirtiest, and most dangerous unskilled jobs in the northern cities, and nativists answered by comparing them to African Americans; anti-immigrant newspapers drew them with the faces of apes.[[2]]
### The Labor Movement
Following the American Revolution, artisans and craftsmen embraced an ideology of production based upon individual liberty and equality. They saw themselves as small-scale producers, equal to one another, and empowered by the freedom of self-sufficiency. However, as the factory system spread, many workers became wage laborers, exchanging subsistence living for labor under the control of an employer. For young women, the mill often meant escape from their parents and from domestic service; for men, the foreman’s eye felt like a demotion. Male wageworkers defended their independence in small ways, too: they dropped the old vocabulary of master and servant and called the man who paid them “boss,” a word borrowed from the Dutch. As employees, they received meager wages and little job security. The republican ideal of self-ownership collided with the harsh realities of wage labor of the industrial, capitalist economy. Many skilled laborers, including carpenters and stonemasons, used their unique abilities over unskilled workers to form unions. As a unified group, unions negotiated with their employers for better pay and work schedules.
As labor unions, they faced a significant legal obstacle. According to English and American common law, unions were labeled as illegal “combinations.” Many courts viewed unionizing as an unlawful practice that disrupted a “master’s” authority over his “servant.” Proponents of the labor movement argued that powerful capitalists viewed their employees as menial and expendable, similarly to how Southern planters viewed their slaves. In order to demand higher wages and improved working conditions, unions used striking as a tool to negotiate. Most state courts viewed unions as illegal mobs, often ruling against them. However, by the 1840s, several state courts ruled in favor of unions, culminating in the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court’s decision in Commonwealth v. Hunt (1842), where the court set aside common-law precedent and ruled that workers had every right to organize and to strike against an employer. By invoking the “Spirit of ‘76”, which defeated the privilege of an aristocracy, labor unions destroyed the autocratic capabilities of wealthy industrialists.
### From Revival to Reform
As more Americans became actively engaged in religion, a new collective effort was formed to reform society, hoping to rid the nation of sin, vice, and suffering. The Second Great Awakening pushed past the first: every believer could weigh spiritual questions personally, and salvation stood open to anyone willing to reach for it through faith and good works. The revivalists rode the Market Revolution itself, its roads, canals, and printing presses, to spread the word. They raised money, logged long preaching tours by canal boat, steamboat, and rail, and papered the country with cheap printed tracts. To be sure, evangelical preachers were hardly champions of a market society. They regularly preached against greed and amassing of wealth, labeling such activities as sinful. Finney called selfishness, the extreme form of individualism that encouraged greed, “the law of Satan’s empire,” not God’s.[[3]] As a result, several movements were formed to help reduce immoral behavior, as well as achieve social equality for many of America’s oppressed people.
### The Promise and the Fear
Americans approached their new republic with unusual confidence. They believed the experiment without precedent in human history and assumed that, once begun, the path to further improvement remained open. The republic was not merely a political achievement to be guarded; it was a project that could be perfected. Jacksonian politics celebrated the promise of progress, and the nation’s symbols gave visible form to a growing faith in what Americans might accomplish. The future seemed to offer possibilities no earlier generation had possessed.
That confidence existed beside a persistent fear that the experiment could be corrupted. Resentment of British humiliations lingered, and the monarchies of Europe stood as reminders that republican government was neither secure nor inevitable. Material success raised its own danger: could a nation devoted to commerce and prosperity preserve the public virtue on which republican government depended? Immigration sharpened the anxiety. Newcomers brought different languages, customs, and forms of Christianity, feeding fears that unfamiliar populations might be manipulated by ambitious leaders and turned into instruments of corruption. The republic promised progress, yet progress itself appeared capable of undermining the principles that had made the experiment possible.
The tension produced two seemingly opposite responses. Some Americans pushed to reform society; others sought to withdraw from it. Both answers sprang from the same concern — the survival of republican virtue in a society many judged unstable and increasingly threatened. For some, the remedy lay in improving the institutions and moral habits of the existing order. For others, the danger had grown so great that preserving the republic’s founding ideals required separation from the world that endangered them. Colonial history supplied examples of communities organized around religious conviction, and nineteenth-century emigrant colonies continued to demonstrate the appeal of building new societies beyond the reach of the established one.
Certain reform efforts aimed to restore religion’s influence inside the existing social order. Churches, unsettled by the erosion of traditional authority, promoted social improvement as a means of reclaiming their place as moral arbiters. They advanced the argument that Christianity formed part of the common law and pressed for Sabbath laws that would give religious norms the force of civil legislation.[[4]] Behind these efforts lay the conviction that the republic required a moral foundation and that religious institutions possessed both the right and the duty to protect it.
Other religious movements reached a different conclusion. Rather than reform society from within, they withdrew from it, establishing separate communities where belief and practice could continue without interference. Some judged that spiritual purity could survive only through separation; others held convictions that made continued participation in mainstream society difficult or impossible. The Shakers practiced absolute celibacy under the teachings of Mother Ann Lee. The Millerites anticipated a literal escape from the corrupt world, expecting the Second Coming first within 1843–44 and at last on October 22, 1844. The Latter-day Saints, driven from Missouri, built Nauvoo into one of the largest cities in Illinois, and only after their prophet’s murder did they withdraw altogether, beyond the nation’s western border. Plural marriage, practiced in secret at Nauvoo and acknowledged publicly only in 1852, inflamed the hostility against them; it did not begin it.[[5]]
These movements expose a central contradiction in the American reform impulse. Americans celebrated a republic capable of continual improvement, yet many doubted whether the society that republic produced could preserve its own virtue. If the republic depended on the moral character of its citizens, political institutions alone could not guarantee its survival, and the struggle to preserve republican virtue became, above all, a religious struggle. Yet religion offered no single solution: some believers set out to transform the world around them, and others concluded that they could keep their faith only by leaving it.
## Part II · A Charter and an Order
### Heaven on Earth
Some Americans did not wait for the nation. They withdrew to build the perfected society in miniature, and the most consequential of these efforts came from the Burned-Over District itself. In 1830, the same year he published the Book of Mormon, Joseph Smith dictated a revelation describing the ancient patriarch Enoch and a city so righteous that God took it up into heaven — Zion, the City of Enoch.[[6]] The text is not the Bible’s: Genesis gives the name Enoch to a son of Cain, for whom Cain built a city, and to the righteous patriarch who “walked with God: and he was not; for God took him,” alone. The perfected city was a revelation of the 1830s, received in the same upstate country and the same years as the revivals, and it would carry Smith’s followers across the continent to the valley of the Great Salt Lake.
### The Market of Sects
Nowhere did the religious excitement of the age burn hotter, or produce more variety, than along the line of the Erie Canal. Within a generation, the people of upstate New York could choose among Methodist circuit riders and Baptist farmer-preachers, Shaker villages that forbade marriage, perfectionist communities that redefined it, and Millerites counting down to the end of the world. The market revolution had made Americans choosers in everything they bought; the Awakening made them choosers in what they believed, and the choosing could exhaust as easily as it liberated. That crowded market is where Chapter 14 left Joseph Smith, asking which of all the parties had the truth.
The answer he gave was that none of them had it, and that the true church had been restored. Of all the faiths the district produced, the church Smith organized at Fayette, New York, on April 6, 1830, became among the most demanding and the most persecuted, and it grew anyway. Its growth was no accident of zeal. It offered what the market of sects could not: certainty. A living prophet, new scripture, and a restored priesthood answered the question the competition kept open, and they answered it without closing the doors the Awakening had opened, because that priesthood belonged not to a seminary-trained few but to nearly every worthy man in the church. Mormonism democratized religious authority even as it concentrated it, and the historian Nathan Hatch counts it among the five great popular religious movements of the early republic. A church with no paid clergy and an ordained laity could send missionaries out within months of its founding, and in 1837 it sent them across the Atlantic to England.
The demands were not an obstacle to that growth; they were part of its engine. Converts were asked to gather, to give, to move, and to keep moving. A faith that cost so much screened out the half-committed, and those who stayed invested more in one another, the reason, the sociologist Laurence Iannaccone has argued, that strict churches are strong. Persecution worked the same way. The Saints read each expulsion through the story of ancient Israel: a chosen people driven from one promised land toward the next. Every mob confirmed the story it meant to end, and the mobs came early.[[7]]
### Zion in Jackson County
Zion soon had an address. On July 20, 1831, Smith announced a revelation naming the land around Independence, in Jackson County on Missouri’s western border, “the place for the city of Zion.”[[8]] For Latter-day Saints, the gathering there began the building of a holy city in preparation for Christ’s return. To the county’s older settlers it looked like a takeover. By July 1833 almost twelve hundred Saints lived in the county, and a few hundred more would have given them control of its politics. They traded among themselves at the church storehouse, and they taught that the Native peoples then being resettled just across the border held a sacred destiny. That same month the church’s newspaper, The Evening and the Morning Star, printed an article titled “Free People of Color,” and the settlers read it as an invitation to free Black converts in a slave state. The editor’s hasty denial changed nothing.
On July 20, 1833, four or five hundred men met at the courthouse in Independence. Their officers included the county judge, the constables, the clerks of the court, and justices of the peace, and the lieutenant governor, Lilburn W. Boggs, attended and encouraged them. Their manifesto accused the Saints of “tampering with our slaves” and of “inviting free negroes and mulattoes from other states to become Mormons, and remove and settle among us.” Before the day ended the crowd had destroyed the newspaper’s press, leveled the printing office, and tarred and feathered the church’s bishop, Edward Partridge. By November the Saints had surrendered their arms, which Missouri never returned, and crossed the Missouri River into Clay County. The government of Jackson County had not failed to stop the expulsion. Its officers ran it.[[9]]
The church’s other center fared little better. In Kirtland, Ohio, the Kirtland Safety Society, founded in the fall of 1836, issued notes in conflict with Ohio’s banking law and then collapsed in the Panic of 1837 that Chapter 14 traces. Smith and Sidney Rigdon stood trial and paid fines, and some Saints lost their faith along with their savings. Early in 1838 Smith left Kirtland for northern Missouri, where the Saints had regrouped around a new town, Far West.[[10]]
### A War of Extermination
The Saints had drawn a lesson from Jackson County, and it had nothing to do with patience. At the Fourth of July celebration at Far West in 1838, Rigdon warned that any mob that came against them would find “between us and them a war of extermination, for we will follow them, till the last drop of their blood is spilled, or else they will have to exterminate us.”[[11]] Missouri newspapers cited the speech for months as proof that the Saints meant war, and by September their neighbors wrote to the governor, now Boggs, warning of a coming war “which they are pleased to call a war of extermination.” That fall the violence ran both ways. Vigilantes attacked Latter-day Saint settlements, and Saint companies answered by burning dwellings and seizing goods. On October 25 a company of Saints charged what they took for an anti-Mormon mob at Crooked River. They had attacked a lawfully mustered state militia. Four men died, three of them Saints.[[12]]
Three days later Boggs wrote to his commanding general: “The Mormons must be treated as enemies, and must be exterminated or driven from the state if necessary for the public peace.” In 1838 the word could mean driving a people beyond the borders as well as destroying them, and federal officials had used it that way of Indian nations for a decade. The historian William G. Hartley put it plainly: for the Saints, “‘remove or be exterminated’ was not new.” Boggs’s own intentions remain uncertain. What followed does not.[[13]]
On October 30, more than two hundred vigilantes rode into the Latter-day Saint settlement at Haun’s Mill and killed seventeen men and boys, eighteen by some counts. Hours later, a mother searching among the dead found her seven-year-old son, Alma, wounded and feigning death under a pile of bodies, where he had lain since being shot, afraid to move or make a sound until he heard her calling. No evidence shows that the attackers knew of the governor’s order. They did not need it.[[14]] On November 1, Far West surrendered to some 2,500 state troops under Major General Samuel D. Lucas, who read the order aloud to the Saints’ negotiators. A court-martial sentenced Joseph Smith to be shot. The militia general ordered to carry out the sentence, Alexander Doniphan, refused and called it illegal. Instead Smith, his brother Hyrum, and four other leaders went to the jail at Liberty in December 1838, and most of them stayed there until April 1839.[[15]]
With its prophet in jail, the church organized its own retreat. On January 29, 1839, at Far West, Brigham Young moved that the Saints “enter into a covenant to stand by and assist each other to the utmost of our abilities in removing from this state, and that we will never desert the poor who are worthy, till they shall be out of the reach of the exterminating order of General Clark, acting for and in the name of the state.” A Committee on Removal managed the exodus. Over the following months the Saints crossed some two hundred miles east to Quincy, Illinois, on foot and by wagon, or went by riverboat through St. Louis. How many left remains disputed: the Saints’ memorials to Congress claimed as many as fifteen thousand, and the editors of the Joseph Smith Papers estimate eight to ten thousand.[[16]] The order stood until 1976, when Missouri’s governor, Christopher S. Bond, rescinded it and expressed “deep regret for the injustice and undue suffering.”[[17]]
### Washington and Nauvoo
Free again, Smith carried the Saints’ claims to Washington. In late November 1839 he and Elias Higbee met President Martin Van Buren, who, by their account a week later, “looked upon us with a kind of half frown and said, what can I do? I can do nothing for you,— if I do any thing, I shall come in contact with the whole State of Missouri.” Citizens stripped of their land and homes by their own state had appealed to the national government, and the national government declined to contradict a state. The question of who belonged in Missouri, Washington left to Missouri.[[18]]
Illinois chose differently, at first. At a village called Commerce on the Illinois bank of the Mississippi, the Saints laid out a city in 1839 and gave it a Hebrew name, Nauvoo, “beautiful”; the name became official with the post office in April 1840. On December 16, 1840, the Illinois legislature granted the city a charter of unusual reach. The mayor held judicial as well as executive power, a municipal court could issue writs of habeas corpus in cases arising under the city’s own ordinances, and the city could raise its own militia, the Nauvoo Legion. Converts kept coming, and by 1846 Nauvoo held about fifteen thousand people. Missouri had turned the power of a state against the Saints. Illinois handed them a share of that power, and within five years Illinois would take it back.[[19]]
## Part III · Perfecting the Individual
### A More Regular Society
The reformers’ vision also reflected the emerging industrial world and the values of the rising middle class. Order, discipline, thrift, hard work, and sobriety promised both personal improvement and a more regular society. The same culture that celebrated individual choice increasingly demanded self-control and disciplined conduct. The democratization of religion multiplied the number of people who could join the work of reform, while the new economic order helped define what that reform was supposed to achieve.
### The Temperance Movement
One area in particular need of reform was America’s use of alcohol. By 1830, the average American adult consumed more than seven gallons of pure alcohol annually.[[20]] Potable water remained difficult to obtain in large quantities and nearly every social gathering, from birthdays to weddings, featured vast quantities of whiskey and hard apple cider. For many Americans living on the frontier, excess corn and wheat were often distilled into whiskey for personal use and for sale. John Chapman, the fabled Johnny Appleseed, planted his apples not for eating, but for making hard cider.[[21]] Beer and rum had long been standard fare in American rituals, like patriotic ceremonies, work breaks, barn raisings, and games. Long before the arrival of spirit-drinking Irish and beer-drinking German immigrants, taverns dotted almost every block in working-class districts. In the 1820s and 1830s Americans drank more than ever, in fashionable parlors as much as in taverns. Workingmen paid the heaviest price, because they had the thinnest margin to cover what the bottle cost them. Alcohol was normally consumed throughout the day, including while working. Having a beer or drink of whiskey at 10:00am was common. But as people tried to improve themselves and their communities, alcohol became quickly recognized as a primary concern, as it inevitably led to drunkenness, which caused high rates of infidelity, domestic violence, and workplace accidents. Alcohol was viewed as the slippery slope, which could often lead to the degeneration of behavior and health. Famed minister Lyman Beecher called intemperance “the sin of our land.”[[22]] Indeed, the movement to ban alcoholic beverages from society, or prohibition, has roots well before the 1920s. By the 1850s, more than a dozen states had passed laws restricting the sale of alcohol, beginning with Maine in 1851.[[23]] Popular illustrations of the “drunkard” flooded American cities and rural areas, calling for an end to drunkenness.[[24]] The Temperance Movement was the most successful reformation effort of the mid-19th century.
### Education Reform
In the early 1800s most American children were taught in small local schools, private academies, or at home, and many had no schooling at all. School reform carried the Awakening’s ideals into the classroom, and Horace Mann, a Whig lawyer and politician who became secretary of the Massachusetts board of education, became the era’s leading voice for it. Mann embraced the new market economy, but he hoped that universal public schooling could restore equality to a splintering society by bringing the children of every class together in a common education. Education, Mann wrote in 1848, “beyond all other devices of human origin, is the great equalizer of the conditions of men,”[[25]] and a viable alternative to moving west to acquire a farm. Early labor leaders shared the conviction that free public schools offered a ladder upward, and they made common schools one of their demands. Mann argued that better schools would reinforce social stability by rescuing students from broken homes. Mann fought to lengthen the school year, established curricula and teaching standards, and recruited women, who he felt were better natured and more patient than men, to become teachers. Character building was just as important a function as the three “R’s,” reading, writing, and arithmetic. The classroom taught it by its very shape — a shape still familiar two centuries later: desks in rows, students in alphabetical order, the bell, and a raised hand before anyone may speak. What other organization runs that way? The military, on discipline and order — and the factory floor, which needed both. At its worst, the common school was a drone factory, cranking out worker bees trained and ready for the job, while the occasional gifted child was plucked out and sent on.[[26]] Mann still met fierce resistance from parents who refused to surrender their children’s moral upbringing to strangers paid by the state. Still, with unions, mill owners, and middle-class reformers pulling in the same direction, the common school won out: by 1860 every northern state paid for the education of its children out of public taxes. In addition, the new schools opened the first real profession to women, and women soon made up most of the nation’s teachers. The intellectual leader for female educators was Catharine Beecher, daughter of the famous preacher Lyman Beecher. She founded academies for young women in Hartford, Connecticut, and Cincinnati, Ohio. Beecher argued that women made better teachers than men, in moral lessons and in every other subject. By the 1850s, most teachers were women, both because local school boards agreed with Beecher’s arguments and because they could hire women at lower salaries than men.[[27]] As secular educators and as moral reformers, women were now part of American public life. And in the South, where a literate Black population was increasingly viewed as a threat to the social order, the planter elite refused to pay for public schooling even for poor white children, who fell far behind. This was one of many ways in which North and South grew apart.
Meanwhile, schooling handed middle-class children an early lead in the race for economic advantage. As artisans lost control of their trades, young men had more reason to stay in school and train for skilled work, and formal education mattered most for those who wanted an apprenticeship in retail or commerce. For young women, education offered the polish of a refined, genteel life. Alexis de Tocqueville, after his tour of the United States in the 1830s, admired how much independence young American women enjoyed. Young women of middling means also put their schooling to work as teachers in the growing common schools. Education opened respectable careers to middle-class youth, while poor children stayed on the margins, too pressed by want to profit from school at all. In essence, a child’s class decided what education and what work lay ahead. In colonial America, nearly every child followed a parent into farming or a craft. The Market Revolution let more children put off work. Americans began to prize a “Romantic Childhood,” a stretch of years when boys and girls stayed sheltered at home and learned in primary school. However, only families that could live without their children’s wages could afford it, and what a child lived through in those years often decided whether the grown man or woman found a respectable, well-paid position or ended in dependent labor with little hope of rising.[[28]]
### Asylums and Penitentiaries
Women became instrumental in reforming other institutions in American society. Dorothea Dix, who experienced her teenage years during the peak of the Second Great Awakening, was raised in Boston within a prominent family. However, her father, a Methodist minister, ended up an impoverished alcoholic. Dix survived an emotionally abusive childhood, and it left her tender toward suffering and fixed on a moral mission. She used money from her grandparents to set up charity schools to “rescue some of America’s miserable children from vice.” In 1841, Dix took up a new cause; humane care for the mentally ill. In the 19th century, healthcare for the mentally ill was virtually nonexistent, with most people categorized as “insane” being relegated and confined to filthy and primitive hospitals, poorhouses, and even prisons. Treatment largely included bloodletting, purgatives (medicines that caused vomiting and diarrhea), restraints, and medieval contraptions placed around the head. When she found mentally ill women jailed alongside male convicts, she lobbied Massachusetts legislators until they expanded the state hospital to take in poor patients. Encouraged by her success, Dix began a national movement to establish state-funded asylums for the mentally ill. By the end of 1845, she had traveled more than ten thousand miles in three years and visited eighteen state penitentiaries, three hundred county jails and houses of correction, and more than five hundred almshouses.[[29]] Her reports, and her refusal to let up, pushed one state after another to reform its prisons and asylums.
## Part IV · The Sin Written into Law
### The Abolition Movement
Turner’s Rebellion took place at the precise moment when southern cotton production was booming. The North’s insatiable demand for cotton rapidly fueled the expansion of cotton plantations into the Southeast and West, whose labor force also required significant bolstering. And with the United States no longer legally allowed to import slaves, a new domestic slave trade emerged to satisfy the growing demand for labor. Seeking new ways to acquire slave labor, most plantation owners engaged in sexual abuse of their female slaves and forced “breeding,” pairing together the enslaved people they judged strongest. Known as the Second Middle Passage, hundreds of thousands of slaves were trafficked and sold at a multitude of new slave auctions throughout the South. Indeed, between 1830 and 1860, southern production of cotton soared from 700,000 bales to nearly 5 million.
In 1860, cotton comprised 60% of all U.S. exports and 75% of the world’s cotton supply. And perhaps most astonishing was that by 1860, the total value of all slaves exceeded 3 billion dollars, compared to the entire worth of all Northern factories, industry, and railroads, which were valued at roughly 2 billion. However, the antislavery efforts inspired by the Second Great Awakening, blamed for inspiring Turner’s Rebellion, were not the beginning of the Abolition Movement.
Born in the rhetoric of the Revolutionary War, the ideals of liberty and independence resonated strongly among many Americans who opposed the institution of slavery. The valor of freemen who fought for the Patriot cause also boosted anti-slavery rhetoric. “It would be useless for us to denounce the servitude to which the Parliament of Great Britain wishes to reduce us, while we continue to keep our fellow creatures in slavery just because their color is different from ours,” wrote Dr. Benjamin Rush.[[30]] The earliest organization against slavery, the Philadelphia society founded in April 1775 that became the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, drew most of its members from the Quakers.[[31]] In England, Olaudah Equiano, the former slave who published the “Interesting Narrative” of his life in 1789, was among the first Africans to argue for abolition, insisting that the British had more to gain by trading with free Africans than dealing in slaves. The American Revolution set the nation, and the world, on a trajectory to abolish slavery. By the end of the Revolutionary War, several northern states had already either outlawed slavery or included an emancipation process. Abolition also gained popularity in England, as thousands of Loyalists returned to England with their slaves. One specific incident that ignited clamors to end slavery happened when the slave ship Zong threw 133 enslaved people overboard, claiming their monetary value through their insurance policy. By 1833, Parliament had voted to abolish slavery in all of its colonies.[[32]] In 1808, the United States officially ended its participation in the slave trade per the Constitution. And by the early 1800s, many slave owners had already emancipated their slaves under mounting pressure from antislavery groups.
But emancipation efforts quickly encountered stark opposition as questions emerged regarding how, and whether, America should assimilate African Americans into the society at large. Many Southern planters rationalized keeping their slaves because they feared a society in which free African Americans could live side by side with Whites. Thomas Jefferson referred to this paradox as holding “the wolf by the ear.” Then in December 1816, a group of prominent reformers and politicians, meeting in Washington chiefly at the instigation of the Presbyterian minister Robert Finley, formed the American Colonization Society to help transport newly freed slaves to Africa, in exchange for congressional compensation. The Society drew Henry Clay, John Marshall, and Daniel Webster to its cause, and it listed Andrew Jackson among its first vice presidents, by one account without his consent.[[33]] Many northerners, including a faction of the Abolition Movement, saw colonization as the only way to rid the nation of slavery. Southern supporters of colonization dedicated most of their energy to persuading free African Americans to leave the United States, arguing that they were a “degraded” group whose presence posed a danger to White society. Other supporters of colonization, including a young Abraham Lincoln, believed that slavery and racism were so deeply embedded in American life, that African Americans could never achieve full equality if allowed to remain in the country. However, Colonization received strong opposition from former slaves who were oftentimes many generations removed from their ancestral homelands. “We have no wish to separate from our present homes, for any purpose whatever,” Philadelphia’s free Black residents declared in August 1817. In the years that followed, a number of Black organizations removed the word “African” from their names to eliminate any possible reason for being deported from the land of their birth.[[34]] By the 1820s, abolition was losing popularity as resistance to colonization intensified and with the explosion of the cotton industry caused by the Market Revolution. Furthermore, states in the North began to systematically eliminate social and political freedoms for African Americans, namely the right to vote, serve on juries, and reside in White neighborhoods. Ironically, as democracy began expanding for poor, landless Whites and European immigrants, African Americans started to see their freedoms disintegrate.[[35]]
### Immediatism
However, as the Second Great Awakening proliferated throughout the United States, more people began to see slavery as a great sin and an obstruction to the moral progression of the country. But the abolitionist movement that arose during the Second Great Awakening differed profoundly from the antislavery efforts of the post-Revolution era. Drawing on the conviction that slavery was an unpardonable sin and a betrayal of the Declaration of Independence, a new generation rejected gradual emancipation and demanded an immediate end to the institution. And unlike the antislavery voices before them, they denounced slavery and slaveholders in incendiary terms and demanded citizenship for the freed, not deportation. A perfect society, they insisted, required the nation to tear out racism by the roots, along with slavery.[[36]]
Such an aggressive attitude became epitomized in publication of “An Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World,” by David Walker. “Tell us no more about colonization,” Walker wrote, addressing White readers, “For America is as much our country as it is yours.”[[37]] The militancy of Walker’s editorial and Turner’s Rebellion temporarily convinced southern planters to consider compensated emancipation and colonization, however a bill failed to pass in the Virginia legislature and a retaliation ensued, including the implementation of more brutal slave codes.[[38]] By 1830, the Abolition Movement was nearly dead, when William Lloyd Garrison, who had learned the cause in Baltimore under the Quaker editor Benjamin Lundy, moved to Boston to publish his own anti-slavery paper, The Liberator. Garrison, exhausted by the passive efforts of gradual emancipation and manumission laws, desired a quick and decisive end to slavery. Garrison preached against slavery, not as a detriment to the progress of Whites, but as an abomination and evil against Africans. Garrison gained popularity and formed the New England Antislavery Society in 1832.[[39]]
### Two Strategies
Walker and Frederick Douglass wanted the same thing and disagreed about how to get it, and the disagreement will return. Walker called his people to rise — and chastised them for their own servility while he did it. Douglass, who escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838, was discovered by Garrison at an antislavery meeting on Nantucket in 1841, and became the movement’s most powerful speaker, chose the other road: the founding documents, moral suasion, the vote, every legal means — and respect earned in public. He founded his own paper, The North Star, in 1847, and he refused to settle for emancipation alone, urging free Black Americans to use petitions and every form of lawful protest to break slavery’s shackles and win equal standing. Speaking at Rochester in July 1852, he asked what the Fourth of July could mean to the slave — and answered not by rejecting the Declaration but by holding the nation to it. The two strategies did not end with them. A century later, the same argument ran between Malcolm X and Martin Luther King Jr., and it is running still.[[40]]
### Law, Morals, and Power
Every movement for change reaches for one of three methods. Law sits on top, because it is the product of a process everyone agreed to — binding even on those who dislike the outcome. Morals are beliefs, and beliefs come from community, which is why a Texan’s and a New Englander’s need not match. Between them runs ethics, the unwritten code: no rule forbids blowing an air horn in the middle of a golfer’s backswing, but no one does it. And when law and morals fail, there is power. Walker reached for power; Douglass for morals and, increasingly, for law. The next chapter will set three men of the 1850s — Daniel Webster, William Lloyd Garrison, and Henry David Thoreau — on the same map, each answering an unjust law with a different method. Which method has worked best — in the women’s movement, the civil rights movement, or any campaign since? A century after Walker, King chose his words: family, brother, sister, God, country.[[41]]
### African American Abolitionists
In 1830, most of the free Blacks residing in the North lived under extreme prejudice and poverty, being relegated to live on the periphery of cities and confined to menial, cheap labor for wages. Alexis de Tocqueville observed that racial prejudice seemed stronger in the states that had abolished slavery than in those where it survived.[[42]] Despite having to endure a growing racist sentiment in the North, free African Americans remained focused on freeing their kinships and families who remained in slavery. In 1830, Richard Allen and other African American activists called a national convention in Philadelphia.[[43]] The delegates turned down Walker’s call for a slave uprising, and they turned down the old, patient program of uplift for free Blacks as well. Instead, this new generation of activists demanded freedom and “race equality” for those of African descent. The most prominent of all African American abolitionists was Frederick Douglass. Son of an enslaved woman and, most likely, her owner, and a runaway from Maryland, Douglass knew first-hand about the dehumanizing and brutal institution of slavery. Discovered by William Lloyd Garrison, Douglass quickly became a featured speaker among the growing abolitionist movement in Boston. Douglass would go on to found his own antislavery newspaper, The North Star. Douglass offered a new vision for abolitionists by refusing to settle for emancipation. He called on free Black Americans to use every lawful tool at hand, from petitions to public protest, to break slavery’s shackles and to claim equal standing. The primary weapon of the abolitionists was the printed word. To spread their message, the abolitionists turned to mass communication. Using new steam-powered presses to print over a million pamphlets, the American Anti-Slavery Society carried out a “great postal campaign” in 1835, flooding the nation, including the South, with its antislavery literature.[[44]] The abolitionists’ second tactic was to aid runaway slaves.
They provided housing and jobs for fugitive slaves in free states and created the Underground Railroad, an informal network of whites and free Blacks in Richmond, Charleston, and other southern towns that assisted fugitives reach the safety of several cities in the North, as well as Canada. Guides were known as conductors, safehouses were stations, and the runaways were called passengers. “Trains” northward were conducted at night, using the stars to navigate. Safehouses, which were often owned by sympathetic Whites, used different signals, usually porchlights or chimney smoke, to communicate whether or not the passengers were safe to stay. Many safehouses also contained secret compartments and hiding spaces, for when Southern detectives demanded to inspect their residences. The fugitives and conductors of the Underground Railroad faced constant threats from being tracked and captured by slave catchers and bounty hunters, who used bloodhounds to pursue the runaways. Harriet Tubman, herself an escaped slave, became the Underground Railroad’s most famous conductor, making roughly thirteen journeys back into the South. Like other fugitives who went back, Tubman risked her freedom and her life every time she returned South to bring others out. Tubman earned a reputation for toughness and grit, often carrying a pistol for protection and to make sure that no passenger lost their courage by returning. Late in life she claimed that she never lost a passenger. Because of the heroic efforts of the Railroad, about one thousand African Americans reached freedom in the North each year.[[45]]
### Anti-Abolition
Northern opponents of abolition feared that an egalitarian message would upset the hierarchy of social status and power. More importantly, the crusade against slavery would lead to higher Black populations and more competition for wages. The violence of the anti-abolitionists, combined with the increasing radicalization of William Lloyd Garrison, caused deep divisions within the movement. Garrison quickly became the target of threats and violence, specifically from New England Whites who felt his message of Africans as social equals went too far. Resistance to abolition was demonstrated largely by White factory workers and European immigrants, who feared losing their jobs to the millions of former slaves who would work for lesser wages. In addition, wealthy Northern textile merchants and factory owners depended upon Southern cotton production, which was fueled by slave labor. Above all, white Americans nearly to a person dreaded “amalgamation,” the racial mixing and intermarriage that Garrison’s integrated meetings of men and women seemed to encourage. Racial fears and hatreds led to violent mob actions. In northern towns, white laborers trashed the taverns and brothels where the races mingled, and they tore apart the Black churches, temperance halls, and orphanages that marked a “respectable” Black community. Violence against White abolitionists reached a crescendo in 1837, when a mob in Alton, Illinois, shot and killed Elijah P. Lovejoy, editor of the abolitionist Alton Observer.[[46]] By demanding emancipation and equality, abolitionists dragged into the open how deep racial prejudice ran, and they made Americans of every color more conscious of race.
In the South, racial solidarity was especially strong, where whites banned all abolitionists literature and sought legal means to punish any who publicly criticized slavery. Georgia’s legislature offered $5,000 to anyone who would arrest Garrison, bring him to trial, and prosecute him to conviction under Georgia law. A northern college student caught passing out abolitionist pamphlets in Nashville felt the vigilantes’ whip. In Charleston, a mob raided the post office and burned the abolitionist mail inside. After 1835, southern postmasters simply sat on any mail that looked abolitionist. In 1835, President Andrew Jackson, a longtime slave owner, asked Congress to ban abolitionists from using the U.S. Postal Service. Congress refused, but in 1836, the House of Representatives adopted the “gag rule,” which automatically tabled any antislavery petitions or bills, keeping the volatile issue of slavery off the congressional stage.[[47]]
### La Amistad
The rise of the Abolitionist Movement of the 1830s created significantly deeper divisions between the North and South over the issues of both slavery and representation in Congress. As the United States expanded westward, both sections fought desperately to secure an equal amount of state representation in the Senate and House of Representatives to prevent one side from gaining more political power and influence. The South grew increasingly anxious about the rise in runaway slaves despite the passage of the Fugitive Slave Law in 1793. The Missouri Compromise, orchestrated by Henry Clay, set southern fears aside momentarily by allowing the new state of Missouri to be admitted into the Union as a slave state. However, a Supreme Court ruling in 1841 caused the slave-owning South to fear that abolition was gaining national momentum. In 1839, a Spanish slave ship called La Amistad (friendship) was overtaken by a murderous slave revolt during its voyage along the coast of Cuba. Cinque, an African rice farmer who was chained up with 52 other captives, successfully picked the lock to his manacles and liberated his fellow men. Using machetes and sugar cane knives, Cinque and the other captives killed the captain and the cook and spared two Spaniards, whom they ordered to sail them back to Africa.[[48]] They drifted for days until a U.S. Navy ship discovered the ship off the coast of Long Island, New York, and imprisoned the entire crew. Word quickly spread through the region, prompting abolitionists to petition for the African’s immediate release. The leaders of the naval vessel demanded that they be awarded the human property, which created a legal dilemma for the courts. Former president, John Quincy Adams, offered to be leading counsel for the African’s defense, arguing before the Supreme Court citing the Declaration of Independence. After spending 18 months in a dingy, poorly ventilated jail, where curious people paid money to observe the African captives, the Supreme Court sided with Adams in 1841, declaring they be set free. Yet despite the Supreme Court’s ruling, a decisive victory for the abolitionists, the nation-wide movement to liberate the slaves remained plagued by Northern violence, Southern legal opposition, and internal divisions within the movement itself. Even Garrison and Douglass could no longer agree upon the direction the movement should take, resulting in the end of their partnership. Nonetheless, by the late 1840s, the Abolitionist Movement had reached unprecedented levels of influence upon the hearts and minds of the country.[[49]]
## Part V · The Slavery of Sex
### Separate Spheres
The economic independence of wage labor emboldened women to seek greater social and political autonomy. Participation in political and social life challenged the clear patriarchal hierarchy that had dominated the country up until this point. As factory wages became the primary source of income the home also became a refuge from the hard labor of the day, resulting in the separation of public and private spheres. The home, or domestic sphere, where men still expected women to monitor and maintain the household, became a battle ground over traditional gender roles and new social positions on female equality. As some women fought against being pushed into the sphere of domesticity, others fought for greater access to political and educational opportunities, oftentimes against their own husbands who were hesitant to relinquish too much control. The Market Revolution remade the work many women did, yet their legal standing barely moved. A woman had two identities: first she was her father’s daughter, and then, should she be fortunate, her husband’s wife. Where did she fall in between? Nowhere the law could see. Coverture, the doctrine that counted a married couple as one person — the husband — left a married woman without a legal existence of her own. Barring a special legal arrangement, her wages went to her husband, and so did her property; she could neither sue nor be sued. Whatever she earned or spent belonged by law to her husband; she shopped on his credit, and he could cut that credit off whenever he chose. A handful of states allowed divorce, and before this period only Congregationalist states such as Massachusetts and Connecticut, which treated marriage as a civil contract rather than a sacrament, had permitted it at all. Even where the law allowed it, divorce stayed costly, difficult, and rare. For most couples, marriage meant a legal bond for life.
American culture trained men and women for marriage and family along separate tracks. The ideology of “separate spheres” gave the public world of work and politics to men and the private world of the household and its purchases to women. (Women who earned no wages still worked: they shopped, cooked, sewed, cleaned, and taught their children. Because none of that labor brought cash into the house, Americans called it “domestic,” though no household could have survived without it.) Real life rarely matched the ideal, yet the line between a woman’s home and a man’s marketplace set the terms of the American gender hierarchy. The ideal also carried a class bias. Middle- and upper-class families proved their standing by keeping “their” women out of wage work, so that women would serve as mothers and teachers rather than partners in earning a living. However, only households that could do without a woman’s wages could live that way. Poorer women kept earning, as factory hands, pieceworkers making goods for sale, tavern- and innkeepers, and domestic servants.
Across the slave states, except on the few plantations that held dozens of enslaved workers, most white women still planted, harvested, and processed crops beside their families, whatever the culture said a lady should do. White southern households still made much of their own food and clothing. Even planters who grew cash crops for distant markets insisted that slavery and racial hierarchy made them morally better than the grasping, cutthroat merchants of the North. As a result, more and more Americans in each section came to see the other’s way of life as incompatible with their own.
### Women in Public
The prominent inclusion of women among the abolitionists, as well as other reformation efforts during the Second Great Awakening, reflected a significant shift in American gender norms. By joining religious revivals and reform movements, women experienced new important roles in public life. Their activism caused many gender issues, like sexual behavior, marriage, and family authority, to become subjects of debate. The debate entered a new phase in 1848, when some reformers, male and female, focused on women’s rights and demanded complete equality with men. As women in the post-Revolutionary era enjoyed the roles of “Republican Motherhood,” most men believed that female autonomy should be limited to within the home, and that they had no place in trade, politics, the courts, or the legislature. However, many middle-class women were redefining the notion of the domestic sphere by becoming active in their churches and various reformation groups, like abolition and the Temperance Movement. Church work strengthened these women’s hand at home and their voice in family decisions, down to the timing of children. Manuals such as Catharine Beecher’s “Treatise on Domestic Economy” taught women to run their homes as showcases of middle-class order and efficiency.[[50]] Farm wives from propertied families kept the same vigil and carried household concerns into public life. Some religious women developed a clear awareness of gender and became public actors. The Female Moral Reform Society, organized by New York City’s middle-class women in 1834, elected as its president Lydia Finney, wife of the revival preacher Charles Grandison Finney.[[51]] The society tried to end prostitution and to protect single women from moral corruption. In addition, they rejected the double standard of sexual behavior by demanding that men also be accountable for marriage infidelity. Furthermore, as more women entered the teaching profession and gained valuable experience from participating in the Abolitionist Movement, they quickly became aware of their own inferior status in society, as women were largely prohibited from speaking in public and enjoying the same social freedoms as their male counterparts. Offended by this criticism, which revealed their own social and legal inferiority, some women sought full freedom for themselves.
Angelina and Sarah Grimké, daughters of a powerful South Carolina planter, saw firsthand how their father treated the people he owned; repelled, they moved to Philadelphia and threw in with the abolitionists. In the 1830s they took to the lecture circuit, condemning slavery as women who had seen its cruelty with their own eyes. The Grimké sisters had forerunners, both as women who spoke in public and as targets of men who appointed themselves guardians of female modesty. Around 1830, the Scottish-born Frances Wright packed New York’s Hall of Science with talks on utopian communities, slavery, women’s rights, and the misery of the northern laborer. Critics denounced her for speaking in public at all. Maria Stewart, a Black Bostonian, broke that ground in 1832, the first American woman known to lecture before audiences of men and women together. She, too, received intense criticism. “I have made myself contemptible in the eyes of many,” Stewart wrote. “This is the land of freedom,” she had written, “and we claim our rights,” including the right to speak in public.[[52]] However, the opposition to female equality also came from other women, who asserted that such bold demands were contrary to God’s natural order.
### The Seneca Falls Convention
In 1840, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, a devoted member of the New York Abolition Society who along with her husband, helped secure safe passage for fugitive slaves on the Underground Railroad, traveled to London, England to attend the World AntiSlavery Convention. There, Stanton met Lucretia Mott, a founder of the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society. During the first few days of the Convention, male delegates debated whether women should take part in the massive gathering. After women were relegated to separate meetings, Mott and Stanton discussed the hypocrisy of gender discrimination existing within a movement dedicated to universal equality, and decided to hold a convention about women’s rights upon their return home.[[53]] The Stantons lived in Boston from 1843 to 1847, where Elizabeth came to know the city’s leading reformers, Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison among them. After a few years of planning, Lucretia Mott and Stanton organized the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, a women’s rights gathering in the small upstate town where Stanton had settled the year before. There Stanton presented the Declaration of Sentiments, modeled on the Declaration of Independence. But the document added “women” to Jefferson’s axiom, “all men are created equal,” and in place of Jefferson’s list of crimes by George III, it indicted the “injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman.”[[54]] The Declaration of Sentiments went after inequality in all its parts: women locked out of education and most work, husbands in control of their wives’ property, wages, and, after a breakup, children, the married woman’s legal identity swallowed by her husband’s, and the home assigned to women as their “sphere of action.” The early women’s movement rallied around equal rights, and equal rights meant a claim on every definition of freedom the country recognized. The vote headed the list of grievances, a right men had never let women exercise. It was at this moment during the Seneca Falls Convention that a significant amount of opposition was raised. For many women, the right to vote was a radical, overreaching proposition that threatened to discredit the entire movement. But Frederick Douglass, who had been invited to participate in the convention, addressed the conflicted crowd and echoed Stanton’s demand for women’s suffrage. Days later he wrote in The North Star, “In respect to political rights, we hold woman to be justly entitled to all we claim for man. We go farther, and express our conviction that all political rights which it is expedient for man to exercise, it is equally so for women.” But even with Douglass’ and other prominent male support, women’s suffrage would not be realized for more than 70 years.[[55]]
### The Slavery of Sex
In addition to fighting for equal social and political standing, the Women’s Rights Movement strongly advocated for more economic opportunities and additional avenues to obtain financial independence. At an 1851 convention in Akron, the former slave Sojourner Truth pressed the movement even further, to take up the cause of working-class women, for whom staying home had never been an option. Seneca Falls itself had been a middle-class affair, with no factory girls or domestic servants among the signers, yet its participants rejected the home as woman’s sphere; women, Paulina Wright Davis wrote in 1853, “must go to work” to free themselves from “bondage.”[[56]] The dichotomy of freedom and slavery shaped the movement’s whole vocabulary. Just as “wage slavery,” the workers’ phrase the Market Revolution chapter recorded, let northern workers challenge the market’s definition of freedom, the “slavery of sex” gave women an all-encompassing critique of male authority and of their own subordinate status.[[57]]
### Perfect or Destroy
The transition for women from abolitionism to securing their own freedom was a logical one: both enslaved Blacks and married women were “owned” by men, either as property or as their legal dependents. Ending a husband’s legal privileges therefore stirred as much controversy as ending a slaveholder’s property in human beings. Reformers had gone after customs and institutions sunk deep in American life, and plenty of Americans expected wreckage, not perfection.

How did the Market Revolution spark social change? Trace the three engines this chapter describes — the market, the ballot, and immigration — into at least two reform movements.
How did meanings of American freedom change during this period? Consider the workers’ “wage slavery,” the women’s “slavery of sex,” and the abolitionists’ demand for equal citizenship.
How did reformers use conceptions of national identity, democratic ideals, and philosophical, moral, and scientific ideas to challenge the dominant economic and social order — and how did their opponents use the same ideas to defend it?
Walker and Douglass wanted the same end and chose different methods. Using the chapter’s framework of law, morals, and power, compare their strategies — and decide which method has done more to change American society since, citing at least one later movement.
How successful were these reform movements? Which of them did the most to divide the nation it set out to perfect — and what evidence in the chapter would someone use to argue for a different answer?
## Then and Now
Walker and Douglass wanted the same thing and disagreed about the methodology. Walker’s Appeal of 1829 told Black Americans that the country was theirs — “America is as much our country, as it is yours” — and that they were entitled to take it by force if force was what it required. Douglass worked by moral suasion and by holding the founding documents against the men who signed them. Neither was arguing about the timetable; both wanted it now. They were arguing about what makes a nation move. The argument did not end with them. In the 1960s the same two methodologies had new names and the same single goal, one man writing from a jail in Birmingham and another telling audiences the choice was the ballot or the bullet.[[58]]
Test it. Does that pairing hold, or does it reduce four men into two positions? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a cause today where allies agree on the goal and split over the methodology, and say which methodology the record suggests actually changes a country.
