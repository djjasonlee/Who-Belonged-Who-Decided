CHAPTER 1
Pre-Columbian Indigenous Societies
The Americas before 1492
They were much more numerous, diverse, advanced, and complex than the textbooks admit.
This chapter asks how geography and environment shaped the migration, settlement, and social organization of the first Americans; how the peoples of the Southwest, California, the Eastern Woodlands, and the Great Plains adapted to landscapes that differed as much as the continents of the Old World; and why the “Pristine Myth” of an empty, untouched continent has collapsed under archaeological and historical evidence. It compares native social, political, and gender structures with those of contemporary Europe, and it argues that centuries of intertribal trade, alliance, and warfare prepared indigenous peoples for the encounter with Europeans far better than Europeans were prepared for them. Five claims run through it: that Indigenous peoples across North America built societies of remarkable variety, shaped by the lands they inhabited and reshaping those lands in turn; that long-held traditions of exchange and reciprocity governed how goods, technology, alliance, and status were obtained; that the adoption of maize and “three sisters” farming produced surplus, and surplus produced complexity; that Cahokia rivaled the capitals of contemporary Europe while Chaco Canyon anchored a ceremonial and economic network across the San Juan Basin. When those great centers declined, their peoples reorganized into smaller societies, carrying their norms, alliances, and rivalries into the world the Europeans would enter. Long before Europeans arrived to draw lines of their own, the peoples of this continent had drawn theirs: through kinship and adoption, councils and confederacies, trade and war, they determined who belonged to their nations and who did not. That authority rested with them. The Pristine Myth erased those answers. This chapter puts them back into the story.

### The Pristine Myth
The Pristine Myth: The history of the Americas has primarily been an ethnocentric story of European achievement — exploration, discovery, and colonization. Native Americans are largely portrayed as primitive, passive, and powerless: actors in someone else’s movie. The role of indigenous peoples in Europe’s overtaking of North America is either ignored or described briefly and then set aside. Contrary to that narrative, by the time the first Europeans set foot on American soil, Native Americans had established complex social, political, and economic systems shaped by centuries of intertribal trade, alliance, and conflict.[[1]]
Native peoples were neither passive nor powerless. In fact, it was the Europeans who yielded to native conditions. It was the indigenous people who dictated terms to newcomers who remained in desperate need of food, supplies, and guides. Native Americans resisted European advancement quite effectively, and when they negotiated, they negotiated from a position of strength. Their calculated motives and long-term strategies were the product of centuries of intertribal diplomacy and warfare — not improvisation in the face of the unknown. Once the fascinating, alien images of facial hair, glass, and guns subsided, Europeans were received as social equals, and in some cases as inferiors. From the native point of view, the Europeans were simply another tribe: a people who might supply new goods or serve as allies against old enemies.[[2]]
The histories and cultures of the indigenous peoples of the Americas are no less complex than those of Europe, Asia, or Africa. Yet the mistaken stereotypes persist. Where did they come from?

### Where the Myth Comes From
Where the Myth Comes From: For most Americans, the source is not a book. It is Hollywood: the headdress, the teepee, the bow and arrow, cowboys and Indians, good versus evil. It is a team logo. It is a decades-old television commercial about littering. It is Disney’s Peter Pan (1953), in which the Indians of Neverland explain the colour of their own skin in a song called “What Made the Red Man Red?” These images are not neutral, and they are not accidental.
The images are not even a composite. The headdress, the teepee, the horse, the buffalo hunt belong to the peoples of the Plains — one region among dozens, on a continent of hundreds of nations and hundreds of languages. There is a reason it was the Plains, and above all the Lakota. They were among the last Native nations to hold their independence of the United States, and theirs was the war the country read about. Two weeks before the fighting, at the sun dance, Sitting Bull had seen soldiers falling upside down into his village. On June 25, 1876, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer attacked that village on the Little Bighorn with five companies of the Seventh Cavalry — two hundred and ten men — and Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors killed every one of them. At Custer’s Last Stand the Sioux became the last Native nation he ever saw. His final vision of the indigenous world lasted longer than the Seventh Cavalry. When filmmakers began making Westerns in the 1910s, they did not have to invent the genre. Buffalo Bill had already supplied the script: the noble scout, the savage attacker, the damsel in distress, the sharpshooter like Annie Oakley, and a clear line between good and evil. Early filmmakers, including Thomas Edison and Hollywood directors, took the Wild West show from the arena to the screen, carrying its characters, pacing, and moral divisions with them. Hollywood inherited a mythology that Buffalo Bill had spent decades turning into a global spectacle, one that audiences had learned to recognize as American history. Everyone else was cast as the Lakota.[[3]]
They rest on an old idea. The Noble Savage held that indigenous peoples remained in an original state, free from sin, appetite, or the concept of right and wrong; that those deemed “savages” were not brutal but noble — uncorrupted, innocent, naïve. The savage and the noble savage are the same invention wearing different clothes. Both deny native people the ordinary complexity that every other civilization is granted without argument.
Two historical processes hardened that invention into common sense. The first was disease. Europeans introduced contagious illnesses to which the indigenous peoples of North America had no prior exposure, and waves of epidemics moved into the interior years — sometimes decades — ahead of face-to-face contact with any actual European. Colonists frequently arrived in a landscape that had[[4]] already been emptied, and reported a wilderness where there had recently been a nation. The second process was justification. Euro-Americans removed the native peoples they encountered as they pushed across the continent for land and resources, and that removal required a story. In the United States, the story was Manifest Destiny: the belief that it was the God-given destiny of Christian Europeans, and later Euro-Americans, to control the land and resources of the Americas. A people who never truly owned the land, and were too primitive to use it, are far easier to displace.

### The First Americans
The First Americans: For most of the twentieth century, scholarship on the first Americans argued that groups of nomadic hunter-gatherers arrived during the last ice age, roughly 13,000 years ago. These first settlers were said to have followed the migration of large mammals across the Bering Strait land bridge before spreading throughout North and South America.[[5]]
That account is no longer sufficient. In 2021, researchers reported fossilized human footprints preserved in the lakebed at White Sands National Park, New Mexico, dated to between 21,000 and 23,000 years ago — roughly ten thousand years earlier than the Clovis horizon, and squarely within the Last Glacial Maximum, the peak of the Ice Age. The claim was immediately contested. What settled it[[6]] was repetition: a 2023 study re-dated the site using conifer pollen and luminescence dating of quartz grains, and a 2025 study radiocarbon-dated the lake mud itself through an independent laboratory. All three methods returned the same range.[[7]]
The implication is significant. If people were living south of the continental ice sheets during the Last Glacial Maximum, they could not have walked in through an interior corridor, because no such corridor was open. They very likely came by sea, moving down the Pacific coast. Other claims push the date further still — excavations at Chiquihuite Cave in central Mexico have been argued to show human occupation beyond 30,000 years ago — but that evidence remains genuinely disputed, with a large group of specialists arguing the supposed stone tools are natural rock fragments rather than human work. The honest statement of the present evidence is this: humans were in the Americas by at least[[8]] 21,000 years ago, they very likely arrived by water, and how much earlier than that remains an open question.
The evidence also has a face. During the last Ice Age, the flooded caves of the Yucatán were dry. Sometime around twelve thousand years ago, a girl of fifteen or sixteen went into the dark, probably looking for water, and fell a hundred feet into a bell-shaped pit. She died there, among sabertooths and giant ground sloths that had made the same mistake. When the seas rose, the water sealed the chamber. In 2007, cave divers swam into a void so large their lights could not find the walls. Resting on a ledge was a small skull, upside down, with a perfect set of teeth. Researchers named her Naia, and she is the most complete early-American skeleton yet found.[[9]]
Naia did not tell us when the first Americans arrived. She told us who they were. Her skull has the long, narrow shape of the earliest Americans, different enough from later Native peoples that some scholars argued for a separate first migration, one later replaced. Her DNA says otherwise. Naia carries the same Beringian maternal lineage as modern Native Americans. In essence, the first Americans did[[10]] not vanish. They were the ancestors of the footprint-makers at White Sands and of every nation in the chapters that follow.

### Diverse Geography, Diverse Peoples
Diverse Geography, Diverse Peoples: There was never one Native American society, and treating Indigenous America as a single culture is itself a version of the myth. Geography shaped how different peoples lived, worked, and organized their communities. In the arid Southwest, peoples built irrigated fields, multi-story masonry pueblos, and ceremonial complexes suited to a landscape where water determined where communities could survive. Hohokam farmers engineered extensive canal systems to carry river water to their fields, while Ancestral Pueblo communities built settlements at Chaco Canyon that connected ceremonial, political, and trade networks across hundreds of miles.
Along the California coast and interior valleys, dense populations relied on acorn processing, fishing, hunting, and trade without adopting intensive agriculture, because their environments already provided abundant food. Acorns required careful preparation to remove their bitterness, then could be ground into meal and stored for later use. In the Eastern Woodlands, peoples combined farming with hunting and gathering. Corn, beans, and squash supported permanent villages and growing populations, while political alliances brought separate communities together. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, for example, joined five nations in a political union that Europeans would encounter during their expansion into the region.
On the Great Plains, communities followed the buffalo and organized their year around the herd. They hunted on foot, using coordinated drives, cliffs, and corrals to bring down animals that supplied food, clothing, shelter, tools, and other necessities. The horse, often treated as an ancient fixture of Plains life, arrived with Europeans and spread among Native peoples after contact. Its adoption would transform hunting, trade, warfare, and mobility, but that world came later.
The single most consequential development was agricultural. The widespread adoption of maize cultivation, and of the interplanted “three sisters” — corn, beans, and squash grown together so that each supports the others — produced reliable surplus. Surplus produced population. Population produced cities, hierarchy, specialized labor, monumental construction, and long-distance trade.

### Cahokia
Cahokia: Near present-day St. Louis, Cahokia was the largest city north of Mexico; at its height around 1100 C.E. it likely held between ten and twenty thousand people in its central precinct, with more in the surrounding settlements — a population comparable to contemporary London, and anchored by an earthen pyramid larger at its base than the Great Pyramid of Giza.[[11]]

### Chaco Canyon
Chaco Canyon: In the San Juan Basin, Chaco presents a different kind of complexity: a dozen monumental great houses linked by engineered roads to communities across tens of thousands of square miles, functioning less as a dense city than as the ceremonial and economic hub of an entire region.[[12]]

### Water and Sky
Water and Sky: The desert demanded engineering. In the Salt River valley, the Hohokam built and maintained a canal system unlike any other in prehistoric North America. Some canals stretched more than thirty feet across and ten feet deep. Generations dug them, cleared them, and rebuilt them over five to seven centuries, until the people left the valley around 1400. The canals carried water from the Salt River to fields where farmers grew corn, beans, squash, and cotton. Their construction required more than digging. Communities had to calculate the slope of the land, control the flow, clear sediment, and coordinate maintenance across miles of farmland. A canal that failed could leave an entire harvest without water.
At Chaco, the builders turned their attention to the sky. On Fajada Butte, three stone slabs stand against a cliff face marked by two spiral petroglyphs. As the Sun moves through the year, light passing between the slabs cuts across the spirals, marking the solstices and equinoxes. The effect, now known as the Sun Dagger, joined architecture, astronomy, and the calendar in a single place. The alignment required careful observation of the Sun’s changing position across the seasons. Chaco’s great houses, roads, and ceremonial spaces reflected a society capable of organizing labor and knowledge across a vast region. It takes a lifetime of watching to build a calendar out of stone, and generations to make a desert yield a harvest.[[13]]
And North America was not the hemisphere’s centre of gravity. South of the Rio Grande the Aztec and the Inca built states that would have dwarfed any kingdom in Europe — in population, in hierarchy, in roads and taxation and tribute. Inca surgeons opened skulls. On crania from the Inca period, between seventy-five and eighty-three percent of patients show the healed bone that proves they lived through the operation; the mortality rate for cranial surgery in the American Civil War, four centuries later, ran between forty-six and fifty-six percent.[[14]]

### Native American Culture
Native American Culture: Key to understanding how Native American and European relationships developed is how native societies actually functioned in comparison to European culture and social norms. The Europeans who arrived in America left societies plagued by autocratic rulers, famine, poverty, and disease. European society was highly stratified, tyrannical, and patriarchal. And while Europeans viewed natives as simple, primitive, and uncivilized, the indigenous perception of Europeans ran in the opposite direction. Natives marveled at European social inequality, rigid social norms, and gender roles, often wondering aloud why European men performed what they understood to be women’s work.
The answer lay in how each society divided life and death. Among the peoples of the Eastern Woodlands, men were bound to death. They hunted, they made war, and they carried the diplomacy that decided which wars would be fought. Women were bound to life. They bore children and worked the fields that fed the village. Neither role ranked above the other; each was a power the community could not survive without. Ironically, the givers of life held the final say over death: it was the clan mothers who decided whether a captive would be adopted, enslaved, or killed. So when English observers watched Native men rest while women worked the fields, they saw idleness. What they were looking at was a division of labor built around life and death rather than around the plow.[[15]]
Contrary to European cultural norms, many Native American societies were matrilineal, egalitarian, and governed by consensus — many, not all. The portrait drawn in this section and the next is a portrait of the Eastern Woodlands: the farming and confederacy-building peoples whom the English and French met first, and whom the next three chapters follow. It does not describe the whole continent. Indigenous America ran the full range of political forms, from consensus democracies to theocratic chiefdoms, exactly as Europe ran the range from Swiss cantons to absolute monarchies. Two counter-cases below make the point. The sections after them describe the societies whose rules the first colonists actually had to learn.
Native American societies were commonly organized into clans — groups tied by family and marriage. These societies placed significant value on every member. A tribe’s population was connected to both its physical and its spiritual strength, and native peoples understood themselves as part of the natural world rather than its owners. The death of a tribe member, whether by capture, killing, or disease, caused significant grief to the clan and tribe collectively, because it was understood as a weakening of spiritual strength.

### The Natchez
The Natchez: The Natchez of the lower Mississippi Valley stood at the hereditary end of that range, and kept one of the most stratified societies in the Southeast. At the time of French contact in the late seventeenth century, they occupied at least six villages near the Natchez Bluffs; one estimate places their population at about three thousand in 1700. Their ceremonial center, the capital the French called the Grand Village, contained three mounds and served as the setting for a political and religious order that European observers struggled to understand.[[16]]
At the top of that order stood the Great Sun, a paramount chief whose authority rested on sacred ancestry and ritual responsibility. He did not, however, control his own succession. That belonged to the Female Sun: she bore the Great Sun, and her daughter would become the mother of the next. Authority passed through the female line even as the visible ruler remained a man. Below the Suns the French described the Nobles, a hereditary elite tied to the royal lineage by blood or marriage; then the Honored People, an intermediate rank whose exact status is still debated; and at the base the large majority, commoners the French called Stinkards, who performed the ordinary labor of the nation. The arrangement joined political authority to kinship and religion. To govern meant occupying a place in a sacred order, not simply commanding the obedience of subjects. When a Great Sun died, the Natchez strangled retainers and kin to accompany him.[[17]]
The strangest feature of the system, as the French recorded it, lay in its rule of marriage. Custom required members of the higher ranks to marry commoners, and rank followed the mother: the children of a Sun or Noble father and a commoner mother dropped in rank, while the children of a high-ranking mother kept hers. The rule tied political rank to kinship and circulated royal blood through the whole society.
But the account comes with a problem. The Frenchmen who described Natchez society carried their own assumptions about rank, inheritance, and political authority with them. Antoine-Simon Le Page du Pratz, who lived in Louisiana from 1718 to 1734 and spent eight years among the Natchez, learned their language and left a detailed account of their society; his Histoire de la Louisiane, published in three volumes in Paris in 1758, became the foundation for later interpretations, and his engraving of the Great Sun carried on a litter appears in this chapter. In 1911 the anthropologist John R. Swanton systematized the evidence into an influential account of Natchez class and marriage rules. Scholars have since challenged it. If every noble had to marry a commoner, as the traditional account held, the[[18]] commoner class would steadily shrink while the lower ranks of nobility expanded — a hierarchy that would eventually consume the population it stood on. Later scholars have questioned whether the rules admitted exceptions, how consistently the Natchez applied them, and whether the Honored People formed a hereditary class at all or simply held an honor that commoner men could earn. The French may have translated a complex kinship system into the more familiar European language of fixed estates.
The distinction matters. The Natchez are more than an example of political complexity; they are a reminder that a historian’s description of a society is not the society itself. The evidence passes through witnesses, translations, and interpretations, each capable of imposing familiar categories on unfamiliar people.
Their destruction reveals the limits of the European account in another way. In 1729 the new French commander at Fort Rosalie, Chépart, demanded on short notice that the Natchez abandon the village that held their high temple and the bones of their royal ancestors, so that he could establish a plantation there. He offered no compensation. At the end of November the Natchez answered. A con[[19]]temporary French account described how they divided their forces among the fort, the village, and the surrounding concessions, traded poultry and corn for French guns and powder, then “attacked at the same time each his man, and in less than two hours they massacred more than two hundred of the French.” By most reports the dead numbered 144 men, 35 women, and 56 children.
French retaliation fell well beyond those responsible. Governor Étienne Périer had the nearby Chaouchas, who had done nothing, slaughtered as a warning to others. In February 1730 a French force made up mostly of Choctaw allies attacked the Natchez, who slipped away across the Mississippi and into the swamps. In the fall of 1731 the French captured many and sold them into slavery on Saint-Domingue. A few found refuge among the Chickasaws and Cherokees, and those nations absorbed them.[[20]]
The colony paid as well. Settlers abandoned promising agricultural concessions in the outlying districts, and the Company of the Indies, having lost its most flourishing settlement, gave up its monopoly; in 1731 Louisiana’s trade reverted to the French crown. A commander’s attempt to seize land for a plantation helped destroy the colony’s agricultural promise.
The Natchez nation suffered the destruction of its center, the dispersal of its people, and the enslavement of many survivors. But the end of a capital did not mean the disappearance of a people. Those who escaped carried their community into other Native nations, while the French account of Natchez society remained an imperfect record of the world they had tried to conquer.

### The Northwest Coast
The Northwest Coast: Indigenous societies of the Pacific Northwest Coast built permanent settlements, monumental art, social hierarchies, and systems of slavery without relying on farming. Everywhere else in this chapter, complexity grew from a field of maize; the Natchez, Cahokia, and the Ancestral Puebloans each built hierarchy on an agricultural base. On the Northwest Coast, salmon performed the work that maize performed for them. And salmon meant more than food. For the peoples of the Coast, and for the Yakama, Umatilla, Nez Perce, and Warm Springs peoples of the Columbia River inland, it anchored the economy and served as a cultural cornerstone.[[21]]
Pacific salmon spend years at sea before returning to the freshwater streams of their birth, swimming upriver to spawn and die. Their return delivers an immense, predictable surge of food in a narrow window of time — an organizational problem as much as an opportunity. Communities had to mobilize labor to harvest, preserve, and store a resource that arrived all at once, and survival depended less on the salmon itself than on the ability to keep enough of it to sustain a community long after the run had passed. Cedar forests supplied the rest: large plank houses, seaworthy canoes, elaborately carved objects. Permanent villages formed around these resources. Families established claims to fishing sites, accumulated wealth, and transmitted privileges across generations. Status could be inherited, property concentrated, and political authority organized around control of productive locations rather than the cultivation of fields.
Abundance alone, however, did not produce inequality. Resources created possibilities; institutions decided who commanded them. Among the Tlingit, the Haida, the Kwakwaka’wakw, and their neighbors down the coast to the Coast Salish and the Chinook, lineages occupied ranked positions, with inherited rights governing access to fishing grounds, names, ceremonies, and other sources of prestige. Houses themselves could signal standing, and families displayed their wealth by giving it away.

### The Potlatch
The Potlatch: Across most of Indigenous North America, exchange systems used reciprocal gifting to sustain alliances, peace, and kinship ties, and status followed the same logic: a European measured a man by what he had gathered, and a community organized around reciprocity measured him by what he had given away. The potlatch kept that logic and turned it. It served as a ceremonial institution among many Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast, including the Kwakwaka’wakw, Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, and Nuu-chah-nulth. These gatherings combined feasting, formal speeches, and the public distribution of wealth to establish social rank, affirm hereditary privileges, and maintain political relationships. A family or lineage demonstrated its standing by giving away blankets, food, carved objects, canoes, and coppers — shield-shaped plaques of hammered copper prized as stores of wealth — along with other valuables. The scale of the distribution carried weight: a host able to mobilize and disperse great wealth displayed the resources required to command respect. Recipients of gifts incurred obligations to reciprocate at future gatherings, binding families and villages through networks of debt, alliance, and competition. At its sharpest, the potlatch became what the anthropologist Helen Codere called “fighting with property”: a chief could humble a rival by giving away more than the rival could answer.[[22]]
Potlatches marked important transitions in social and political life — the assumption of a name or title, marriage, the raising of a memorial pole, the end of a mourning period, and the transfer of rights to fishing sites, crests, and ceremonies. The gathering made these claims public, allowing the community to witness and recognize them. Hereditary rank conferred authority, yet that authority also required demonstration through the capacity to assemble wealth, distribute it, and secure recognition from others. European observers often mistook the practice for wasteful extravagance. Canadian authorities banned the potlatch from 1885 to 1951, treating it as an obstacle to assimilation and economic “progress.” The ban had teeth. After Chief Dan Cranmer hosted a potlatch on Village Island on Christmas Day 1921, police arrested forty-five people, sent roughly half to prison, and seized some six hundred ceremonial objects, including masks and headdresses. Many ended up in museums in Ottawa, Toronto, and New York. The Kwakwaka’wakw have spent decades working to bring them home.[[23]] The institution served purposes its critics failed to understand. It redistributed wealth, preserved history through speech and display, and maintained the relationships that established political standing. Gifts can equalize or stratify, create partnerships or assert dominance. The potlatch shows what happens when the capacity to give is unevenly distributed and publicly performed: generosity itself becomes a weapon of hierarchy.[[24]] Reciprocity organized status differently than accumulation did; it did not abolish status. The potlatch converted surplus into social power.
These societies also practiced slavery. War captives and their descendants occupied the lowest positions, even in communities of considerable wealth and artistic achievement.[[25]] Slavery undercuts any assumption that wealth and sophistication arrived with freedom, or that political complexity followed a single path from farming villages to states. A society could sustain sophisticated institutions and extraordinary cultural production while denying freedom and status to some of its members.
The Northwest Coast therefore marks a limit in the familiar agricultural narrative. Maize supported population growth and political complexity across much of North America, yet it offered only one route to surplus. Salmon, cedar, storage technology, inherited rights, and organized labor could generate comparable forms of differentiation. The critical question never turned simply on whether a society produced a surplus, but on who controlled, transmitted, and distributed it. The same abundance that sustained a community could also sustain its hierarchy.

### Separate but Equal Spheres
Separate but Equal Spheres: Among the peoples of the Eastern Woodlands, tribes maintained councils that ruled by consensus, and those councils included both men and women. Men and women participated in religious ceremonies. Women consented to marriage and retained significant autonomy afterward, sharing in family decisions when they were not the deciding voice outright. Women freely divorced and remarried.
The clan system ran on the same axis. Men achieved higher status through war by taking captives and, less often, scalps. Children belonged to the clan of the mother, and looked to their mother’s brothers more than to their biological fathers.
A father, in this reckoning, was tenderness without authority. His children belonged to their mother’s clan, and he could no more command them than a guest commands a household — affection was his whole office. That single fact would later scramble a century of diplomacy. When the nations of the Great Lakes addressed the French governor as father — Onontio, they called him — the French heard a son’s word, spoken upward, promising obedience. But the word had come out of the longhouse, and in the longhouse it traveled the other direction: father was not what a ruler was owed. It was what a provider owed. A father gave presents, mediated quarrels, and commanded no one — and a father who failed to give was no father at all. So two empires spent a hundred years believing Native nations had submitted to them, while the nations believed they had adopted a generous and occasionally useful relative. The alliances of the next two chapters run on that misunderstanding.[[26]]
Children were frequently adopted by women who had lost children or could not bear them. After a miscarriage or an early death, women often waited years before trying again. Children were treated liberally, without the physical punishment common in European households.
European interpretations of native society ran directly contrary to European social and gender norms, and the collision produced real consequences. The English viewed native culture as inferior, appalled by what they read as the laziness of men and the liberties of women and children. Native society was casual where European culture revolved around strict behavior; indigenous people ate when hungry and wherever was convenient, while Europeans ate at appointed times in designated places. Natives bathed frequently and maintained high standards of cleanliness despite their supposedly primitive housing, while personal hygiene was all but absent among Europeans — one historian of the conquest notes that many Europeans of the age passed their entire lives without a bath. And the traffic[[27]] between the two worlds ran in a direction the authorities preferred not to discuss: at Jamestown, hungry men slipped away to live among the Powhatan, and the colony’s martial law answered by making flight to the Indians a hanging offense.

### Warfare and Captivity
Warfare and Captivity: Population was the strength of an Indigenous nation — physical and spiritual at once — which made war’s true prize the capture of prisoners, not scalps. Mass slaughter, destruction, and removal — total war — profited nothing in a world that counted wealth in people, and was rarely practiced among Native nations.[[28]]
Most wars were conducted to capture, not to kill. Taking men, women, and children was a means of supplementing population, especially in the wake of losing tribe members. Captives were brought to the mourning clan, where clan mothers decided their fate. Those captured might be enslaved, ransomed, or ritually tortured and killed so that their spiritual strength could be absorbed. Those designated for servitude, usually women and children, could over time assimilate and become full members of the society. Men more often suffered ceremonial death.
Adoption produced the era’s most quietly devastating statistic. English colonists taken young and raised in native families, when ransomed or returned by treaty, refused to stay returned — grown men and women slipping back to their adoptive nations at the first opportunity. Indian children raised among the English went home, nearly without exception. Benjamin Franklin marked the asymmetry: an Indian child brought up in English ways needed only “one Indian Ramble” with his relations and “there is no perswading him ever to return,” while ransomed whites “take the first good Opportunity of escaping again into the Woods.” Colonial authorities found the pattern intolerable and rarely advert[[29]]ised it. The difference was membership. A captive adopted into a mourning family was no longer a prisoner but a son or a daughter, with the same place in the clan as anyone born to it. Indian captives among the English found no such place, because English society had none to offer: membership there came by birth, baptism, and property, and a Native child could be a servant or a convert but never an Englishman. It was as close as the record comes to a controlled experiment on the two ways of living, run by the people who had seen both.
The English arrived in North America already shaped by the practice. During the sixteenth-century conquest of Ireland, English armies had laid waste to homes and fields and slaughtered men, women, and children without distinction — and had grown accustomed to cruelty and to the display of the killed, heads and bodies presented as instruction.[[30]] Early violent encounters between English militias and native warriors produced high casualties among natives entirely unfamiliar with this form of warfare. They had not misjudged their enemy’s strength. They had misjudged his rules.

### The First Democracy
The First Democracy: People often mistakenly believe democracy to be a product of the European Enlightenment, when in fact a democratic alliance of nations existed on this continent well before the drafting of the Declaration of Independence. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy — known to Europeans as the Iroquois — united five formerly warring nations under a common political structure: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca. “Formerly warring” undersells it. The five nations were locked in blood feud — a killing answered by a killing, the answer answered in turn, generation after generation: the Hatfields and the McCoys on the scale of nations. The mourning war described above kept the wheel turning, because every raid that replaced one family’s dead created another family in mourning, and every mourning family needed a raid. The Great Law was the decision to stop the wheel. Tradi[[31]]tion holds that the last holdout was persuaded when he was handed a single arrow and asked to break it, which he did easily, and then handed a bundle and asked to repeat the act, which he could not.
The Confederacy’s founding document, the Great Law of Peace, is best understood not as a grant of tribal autonomy but as a set of limitations on power. The Great Law did not exist on paper. Its words lived in wampum belts, where shell beads recorded agreements and gave later speakers a way to recall them. The Onondaga kept the belts and the responsibility for preserving the law they carried. The Haudenosaunee maintained a Grand Council of chiefs who were nominated and installed by the clan mothers of their lineages. Consensus was required for decisions. Chiefs who failed in their duties could be admonished and, if they persisted, removed by the same women who had raised them up. Land was understood as communal and held in trust, a conception that ran directly contrary to European ideas of property and that would generate conflict for the next four centuries.[[32]]
Nomination from below, required consensus, the recall of unfit leaders, and formal political authority vested in women: every one of those mechanisms would still have been considered radical in Europe three hundred years later. A woman given the eighteenth century and the choice of a civilization to grow up in would not have chosen a European one. A sixth nation, the Tuscarora, joined in 1722 after being pushed north out of the Carolinas, which is why the chapters that follow call the Confederacy the Six Nations.[[33]] Benjamin Franklin, for one, looked at the Haudenosaunee and saw a working demonstration that separate governments could bind themselves into a durable union — an argument he made in print in 1751 and pressed at the Albany Congress three years later.[[34]]

### Collapse and Reorganization
Collapse and Reorganization: The great centers of pre-Columbian North America did not last, and their decline is essential to understanding what Europeans eventually found. The story most often told is ecocide: Cahokia cut down its forests, flooded itself out, and died of its own success. The evidence does not support it. Cahokia reached its peak between about 1050 and 1100, when its population may have run five to ten times that of the century and a half before. Yet pollen from the region shows that much of the native forest “was cleared at ∼450 CE, well before Cahokia’s population maximum in the 11th century” — six centuries before the city’s peak — and the worst erosion came as people left, not before they did.[[35]]
What the record shows instead is a convergence. Around 1150, a Mississippi River flood arrived alongside a run of warm-season droughts that struck at the crops, and between about 1125 and 1250 three droughts each lasted decades. The ground records a society under strain: palisade construction, a sharp drop in new mound-building, the “final capping of some mounds,” the abandonment of outlying sites. A city that walls itself in and caps its own monuments has changed its politics. The scientists who assembled that record add a caution of their own: the climate fell “atop cultural problems such as economic decline and political strife that cannot be measured through sedimentary analysis.” By the fourteenth century, Cahokia’s population had fallen back to its level before the city rose.[[36]]
Chaco tells a similar story and demands the same caution. Great-house construction in the canyon stopped by about 1130, as the Colorado Plateau entered a fifty-year megadrought; from 1140 to 1162 western North America endured twenty-three consecutive years of drought. A second Great Drought, from 1276 to 1299, helped bring on the final depopulation of the northern Southwest. Yet the drought did not rid the land of its inhabitants all at once. In much of the Mesa Verde region, people kept harvesting timber and adjusting their communities through the worst of it, and at Pueblo Bonito the tree-ring record shows gaps of decades during which the great house stood continuously occupied. A gap in the record is not an absence of people. It is the same mistake illustrated by the Pristine Myth, written by tree rings rather than ink.[[37]]
The empty mounds, abandoned cliff dwellings, and silent great houses all seem to point toward the same conclusion: each civilization simply vanished. The evidence points somewhere else entirely — not to disappearance, but to dispersion: movement, instability, and the reorganization of large populations into smaller societies. Chaco’s builders are a case in point: they did not vanish. Their descendants are the Pueblo nations of the Southwest — Hopi, Zuni, Acoma and Laguna, and the pueblos along the Rio Grande — who live in the region still, even where tracing a particular pueblo back to a particular ancestral branch remains unsettled.[[38]] Those smaller societies were not remnants. For three centuries before Europeans arrived, the peoples these collapses set in motion moved, merged, and remade themselves: they joined other nations, took on new names and customs, made war and made peace, and grew used to absorbing strangers. A stranger might become a neighbor, and a neighbor a friend. They carried their cultural norms with them, formed new alliances, and competed for natural resources and political power on terms they set themselves. So when Europeans appeared, they presented a familiar problem rather than an alien one: another partner, perhaps, or another enemy. This is the political landscape that European ships sailed into — not an empty continent, and not a simple one, but a continent in the middle of its own long argument about power, land, and survival, conducted by peoples with centuries of diplomatic and military experience.

### What the Europeans Actually Found
What the Europeans Actually Found: When Europeans arrived, they did not encounter a wilderness, and they did not encounter innocents. They encountered nations. They encountered farmers whose maize would keep them alive through their first winters, traders who had moved goods across the continent for generations, and diplomats who evaluated the newcomers with the same cold calculation they applied to any other unfamiliar people.
Put these societies side by side and a different picture of political life emerges. Among the Haudenosaunee, clan mothers determined whether captives entered the community as relatives or remained enemies. Adoption made a stranger a son or daughter, with a place in the clan and obligations equal to those of anyone born into it. The same clan mothers selected their chiefs, could remove them when they failed, and exercised authority through a political system grounded in consensus. Along the Northwest Coast, the potlatch converted wealth into status through public giving and redistribution. Different societies, different institutions, but each had developed its own answer to the questions of who belonged, who held authority, and what obligations bound a community together. The Europeans who arrived after 1492 brought another set of answers, shaped by monarchy, Christianity, property, and empire. The rest of this book follows what happened when those competing understandings of the world met.
The story that followed was catastrophic for indigenous America. But the catastrophe was not the meeting of an advanced people with a primitive one. It was the meeting of two complex worlds, one of which carried, invisibly and unknowingly, diseases that would kill on a scale no European army could have matched. Killing, however, is not conquering. The nations those diseases struck were built — as the mourning-war complex described above shows — to absorb loss and rebuild from it, and for the better part of two centuries they did exactly that. Populations collapsed; polities reconstituted themselves, adopting captives, absorbing refugees, forming new alliances, and continuing to dictate terms to newcomers who could not yet feed themselves. Collapse describes bodies. Reconstitution describes nations. How those reconstituted nations met the Europeans, and what finally broke their leverage, is the subject of the chapters that follow. Everything that comes after depends on getting this right. If one begins with an empty continent, the rest of American history reads as settlement. If one begins with a populated one, it reads as something else entirely.

## Questions for Consideration

1. Identify the sources of the “Pristine Myth” — where the image of an empty, untouched continent actually comes from — and explain what that image makes it possible to believe about the rest of American history.
2. Explain what the archaeological and genetic evidence can and cannot establish about the first Americans, and why the chapter insists that Naia settled the question of who they were rather than when they arrived.
3. Compare the political and social organization of the Eastern Woodlands peoples with that of the Natchez and the societies of the Northwest Coast. Why does the chapter insist on the counter-cases rather than letting the Woodlands portrait stand for the continent?
4. Explain how a society that counted its wealth in people fought its wars, and identify what the captivity-return asymmetry — colonists who would not stay returned, Indian captives who went home — suggests about the two ways of living.
5. Identify the mechanisms of Haudenosaunee government — nomination from below, required consensus, the recall of unfit leaders, and formal political authority vested in women — and evaluate the chapter’s claim that every one of those mechanisms would still have been considered radical in Europe three hundred years later.
6. Explain the distinction between collapse and reconstitution, and why the chapter argues that killing is not conquering.
7. The chapter argues that the nations the epidemics struck were built to absorb catastrophic loss, and did — that for two centuries they set the terms of contact. Make the strongest case against that reading: that the epidemics ended Native power outright, and that what followed was a long aftermath rather than a balance. What evidence would decide the question?

## Then and Now

1. “Columbus discovered America” tells us as much about the person doing the remembering as it does about Columbus. The continent this chapter describes already held numerous peoples, with distinct cultures, governments, economies, and histories. Yet for five centuries, much of the story told about it began with an empty wilderness: a pristine land, in William Denevan’s phrase, inhabited, if at all, by savages, noble or otherwise. The noble savage and the uncivilized savage came from the same error. Both turned Indigenous people into characters in someone else’s story. The Land Back movement challenges that inheritance by placing Indigenous nations, their histories, and their claims to the land back at the center of the discussion. It also raises the question of what follows when the people once written out of the story begin telling it themselves.[[39]]
2. Test the same question against your own family. Where did your relatives come from? What did they believe, what work did they do, and what traditions did they carry with them? Find how people like them appear in a textbook, a film, a holiday, or a museum. What does that account get right? What does it leave out, distort, or misunderstand? Then ask who told the story, and whose version never made it into the telling.
