UNIT II
Empires and the Balance
Four ways of colonizing, and why Native leverage held in one place and broke in another
~
This unit sets four experiments in colonization side by side and asks why they produced different results. Chapter 3 follows four European powers into North America — Spain, France, the Netherlands, and England — and finds that each carried overseas the methods and ambitions it had developed at home: Spain with the Reconquista’s sword and mission, France with the trader’s marriage and gift, the Netherlands with the ledger, England with the Irish wars’ appetite for land and experience in coercive conquest. The differences were not cosmetic. They determined what each power needed from Native nations — souls, pelts, accounts, or acreage — and therefore what each was willing to offer in return. Native nations, accustomed to reading rivals long before any European arrived, often read the empires more shrewdly than the empires read one another. Where two or more powers wanted the same trade, the same alliance, or the same stretch of river, Native nations discovered that the space between rivals was itself a possession. They could set prices, obtain weapons, demand mediation, and punish any partner who forgot the terms of reciprocity. There was always another empire to turn to. For much of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, that leverage kept the Great Lakes in rough balance. It was real power — but it was positional power, and it would last only as long as the rivalry did.
Chapter 4 runs the same experiment with the variable changed. In the Chesapeake and New England, England planted two colonies for different reasons — profit and purity — but within two generations both had moved toward the same outcome: the seizure of Native land and the expansion of English settlement. Both faced a similar imbalance. Powhatan werowances and Wampanoag sachems could negotiate with the English, but no competing European power offered a strong enough alternative in their immediate territories to make English colonists fear losing the relationship. The first partnerships were genuinely reciprocal — Jamestown depended on Powhatan corn, Plymouth on the knowledge and assistance of Massasoit’s people — and they were genuinely brief. In Virginia, Opechancanough’s wars in 1622 and 1644 failed to stop English expansion. In New England, Metacom’s War of 1675–1676 ended in devastating defeat for the Wampanoag and their allies. The difference was not courage, numbers, or virtue. English population growth, the demand for land, and the colonists’ capacity to sustain settlement turned cooperation into conflict in both regions. Diplomacy is what set the coast apart from the Great Lakes: Native leaders had fewer alternatives, and English settlers faced less pressure to preserve alliances. The balance that imperial rivalry helped sustain in the interior broke decades earlier on the Atlantic seaboard.
Native leverage depended on imperial rivalry, and the Treaty of Paris brought that rivalry to an end in North America. On February 10, 1763, Louis XV’s foreign minister, the Duc de Praslin, signed away Canada and France’s claims east of the Mississippi, the Ohio River Valley among them, to Britain. The balance that had sustained Native bargaining power in the Great Lakes had broken. That is where Unit IV begins.
### In This Unit
Chapter 3, “Four Empires,” compares Spanish, French, Dutch, and English colonization, from St. Augustine and Quebec to New Amsterdam and Roanoke. It closes with the balance of power that imperial rivalry made possible. Chapter 4, “Founding and Conflict,” follows the establishment of Virginia and New England, the wars of Opechancanough and Metacom, and the collapse of that balance along the Atlantic coast nearly a century before it broke in the Great Lakes.
