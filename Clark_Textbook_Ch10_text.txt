CHAPTER 10

The Constitution

From the Critical Period to the Bill of Rights, 1781–1791
“The tree of liberty must be refreshed from time to time with the blood of patriots and tyrants.”
— Thomas Jefferson, Paris, 1787
During the Revolutionary War, the Continental Congress created a provisional government, the Articles of Confederation, which granted the states sovereignty over taxes, commerce, and foreign relations. The Articles unified the newly independent states under a central government with limited power. However, the lack of a central authority to regulate domestic and international affairs led to economic and political unrest. Between 1781 and 1786, that unrest threatened the “experiment of America.” In May 1787, the turmoil encouraged delegates from the states to gather in Philadelphia, where, through negotiation, collaboration, and compromise, they proposed a new constitution that gave far greater authority to the federal government. Slavery followed the delegates into the room: they haggled over how to count the enslaved for seats in Congress and over how much say the national government would have over slavery and the slave trade. In the debate over ratification, Anti-Federalists battled Federalists, whose principles appeared in the Federalist Papers. The price of ratification turned out to be a Bill of Rights, promised by the Federalists to spell out the people’s liberties and to set hard limits on what the federal government could do. The delegates determined who would hold power. In the Three-Fifths Compromise, and in the word they refused to write, they also settled who would count, and by how much.

## Part I · The Experiment


### Context, 1783
Context, 1783: After waiting months to hear about the Battle of Yorktown, Lord Germain received word about the Franco-American victory and the disgraceful surrender of General Cornwallis. The majority of Parliament groaned in despair, while King George III was reported to have maintained his even keel demeanor and announced that England must increase its efforts to squash the rebellion. But without the support from Parliament, which collectively believed that any further action to regain the 13 colonies was pointless, George III was powerless to continue. Within months, American diplomats Benjamin Franklin, John Jay, and John Adams gathered in Paris, where they drew up an official treaty to end the war. And by exploiting the rest of Europe’s discontent for Great Britain, the American delegation was able to secure very favorable terms, including ownership of all lands to the Mississippi River, fishing rights off Newfoundland, prisoner of war exchanges, and the fate of confiscated Loyalist property left to the individual states. All through the war, Americans spoke of a “rising empire,” one destined to spread across the continent and rule it. While European monarchies governed by force, the leaders of America vowed it would be different. And while the new republic had already defied all odds by defeating the most powerful nation on earth, a new set of challenges waited on the horizon.
The young republic started with a stronger hand than most new nations: an ocean between itself and Europe’s wars, a population young and certain to multiply, resources on a scale no European state could match, and, for white Americans at least, land and literacy within reach of an unusually large share of households. However, while America was larger than all of Great Britain, France, and Spain combined, controlling such a vast area of land was problematic. In addition, the new nation desperately needed labor to develop its numerous resources, or face the prospect of a potential invasion. Large areas west of the Appalachian Mountains remained in Native American hands, where they continued to clash with Americans along the frontier. Meanwhile, British garrisons lingered in forts along the Great Lakes, on ground the peace treaty had handed to the United States, and Spain held New Orleans, which meant it held the key to the Mississippi and to every western farmer’s route to market. Beyond the Atlantic coast the country stayed almost entirely rural; only a sliver of the population, under one person in thirty, lived anywhere with as many as 8,000 neighbors. Furthermore, America was among the most diverse nations in the world, consisting of numerous ethnicities, nationalities, and religious groups, as well as being home to nearly 700,000 slaves. With so many distinct groups, it would become incredibly difficult to create any sense of commonality, as local loyalties outweighed any need for national patriotism. As John Adams commented, “We have no Americans in America.” Indeed, as the fledgling nation entered into the “Critical Period,” it did so knowing that no republican government had ever been successfully established over so vast a territory or with so diverse a population.

### The Articles of Confederation
The Articles of Confederation: King George’s rejection of the Olive Branch Petition forced the Continental Congress to take three initiatives, which swung the pendulum from reconciliation to independence: The formation of a new centralized confederation, a diplomatic mission to France, and the creation of a declaration of independence, should the colonies unanimously decide to separate from England. Inspired by Enlightenment ideals, the first experiment of America prioritized liberty over authority. Fearing a centralized power, each state maintained ambitions of sovereignty, which hampered attempts to establish a powerful, central authority. Named by many historians as the “Critical Period,” the challenge of establishing and maintaining a new republic had not been successfully achieved since the Roman Republic. The embryonic nation of the United States would soon face challenges, which in many ways, posed more threatening than the power of the British Kingdom. An enormous national debt, the inability to regulate interstate commerce, an insistence upon state sovereignty, and violent insurrections, all under the predatory eyes of foreign powers, threatened to annihilate the “invention of America.” John Adams noted the opportunity and perils facing the new nation saying the colonies had been thrown into a period when, “The greatest philosophers and lawgivers of antiquity would have wished to have lived.” In addition, the colonial delegates of the Continental Congress realized the critical importance of securing with the ballot what had been won with the bayonet.
Americans were extremely fearful of a centralized power. Thus, even after six years working together to defeat Great Britain, the colonies saw themselves as independent states, and not part of a larger nation. Aptly named by the first colonial legislator to propose a colonial union, Benjamin Franklin, the Articles of Confederation were proposed in 1777, but not officially adopted until 1781. Inspired by the Iroquois Confederacy, Franklin recognized how the unification of several native tribes provided political and military solidarity in the face of territorial conflict. Franklin believed if the colonies could establish a confederacy, they could fight back against English imperialism. In addition, fearing the tyranny of the British unitary government, where ultimate sovereignty rested in a central political body, the congress favored a system of rule that could not coerce the states into obedience, “What was achieved in blood, must be preserved in ink.”
The creators of America’s first constitution pursued the following goals in creating a new governing body:
• A limited government.
• The rejection of a monarchy.
• To emphasize civic and public virtue (high moral standards).
• Egalitarian politics.
Lacking an executive and judiciary, “The United States in Congress Assembled” relied on the states to enforce its laws, as an executive was viewed as too similar to a monarch. Under the Articles each state had one vote (equal representation), major legislation required a two-thirds majority, and any changes or amendments to the Articles required a unanimous vote (13/13). Fearing the abuse of power recently exhibited by their former sovereign, the Articles lacked the power to tax, regulate interstate commerce, and maintain a standing military. Despite this lack of authority, the Confederation government achieved a significant degree of success in some areas.

### The States
The States: Independence remade politics inside the states as much as it remade relations with Britain. More men won the vote, and as the electorate grew, so did the demand that government actually represent the people who chose it. In addition, more ordinary citizens, the so-called “new men,” took seats in local and state government. The old social ladder shifted as well: Americans deferred less to the gentry, and birth counted for less while ability counted for more. Such progress however, was largely limited to white, adult males, as women and most free African American males were still refused the right to vote.
Fearing both the potential abuse of a federal power and the ignorance of a newly independent citizenry, the Continental Congress established special Constitutional Committees to create state constitutions. In other words, state legislatures that created governments could unmake them. Most states adopted a Bill of Rights, and designed their governments around a powerful legislature (deemed the most important aspect of government) with a limited executive and judiciary, or none at all. Many state legislatures determined the executive’s salary and appointed state judges, which could be easily manipulated or removed. Property rights were extended to white male landowners. The progress had its limits: women and most free African American men held no position in the governments that ruled them, nor a say in the laws that taxed them. New Jersey offered a rare exception. Its Constitution of 1776 extended the vote to property-holding “inhabitants,” a category that included unmarried women and free Black men who met the residency and property requirements. In 1807, the legislature narrowed the electorate to free, white male citizens, taking the vote from both groups. Elsewhere, exclusion meant that people could own property, pay taxes, and answer to the law while having no voice in choosing the lawmakers or the officials who enforced it. But judged against every other existing government, the state regimes stood as the most democratic institutions in the world.

### What the Articles Got Right
What the Articles Got Right: The new confederacy successfully unified the colonies in ways that previous attempts had failed. Under the Articles, The United States maintained its independence, Americans were allowed to move around the country freely, each state retained its global and domestic sovereignty, and both interstate and foreign trade were encouraged. Perhaps most importantly, the Articles resolved the issue of western lands. Dating back to their English charters, many states laid claim to their western territory, which for some theoretically extended all the way to the Pacific coast. The cession of those claims to Congress, and the passage of the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, resolved the dispute; the Ordinance established the process to admit new states, promoted public education, protected private property, and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory (The Ohio Valley). Ultimately, while some contemporary historians have criticized the creators of the Articles as representing an American aristocracy, the leaders of both state and federal governments consisted of many self-made men, including Alexander Hamilton and Benjamin Franklin, whose rise to prominence proved the success of the more democratic and egalitarian system.

## Part II · The Bill Comes Due


### The Price of Victory
The Price of Victory: The achievement of independence revealed the paradox of freedom. Everything the congress thought they wanted, wasn’t how they imagined. While the Articles provided unprecedented state sovereignty, they failed to establish the power to mandate interstate cooperation, finance day to day operations (mail, infrastructure), levy taxes, regulate commerce, and mobilize a military. Under the Articles of Confederation, each state maintained representation, with one vote, regardless of its size. Thus, Delaware had as much power as Virginia. All new laws required 9 out of the 13 states to agree, and any amendments made to the Articles themselves needed all 13 states to vote in the affirmative. Furthermore, the Articles limited the federal government’s ability to negotiate with foreign powers, including Native American tribes. In other words, too much liberty resulted in the potential for “Mob rule.” Between 1783 and 1787, several political, economic, and military crises exposed the debility of the Articles, resulting in the need to establish a government with a central power.
The Revolution dissolved more than the colonies’ political bonds. It broke the stranglehold of British mercantilism, freeing American commerce to trade with the world. Victory also opened western territories to American expansion and settlement, creating new domestic markets. Americans began manufacturing goods of their own rather than depending on British imports.
Victory would cost more than the price paid for soldiers and supplies. The British Navy destroyed most American merchant ships, and the fighting devastated farmland. Feeding the Continental Army stripped many farms of their crops, livestock, and equipment. British trade orders after the peace severely limited the export of American goods to Britain and prohibited its remaining Sugar Colonies from trading with its former North American colonies, eliminating two major areas of colonial-era commerce. Without the power to regulate currency, each state printed its own money, creating a disorganized and confusing economic system. The greatest adverse economic legacy of the war, however, lay in currency and debt. Continental currency had depreciated dramatically. The national government owed approximately $12 million in foreign debt and $42 million in domestic debt, and state governments owed approximately $25 million, mostly in war debts. The vast amount of war debt resulted in an economic depression.
To make matters worse, the Articles of Confederation lacked the power to impose foreign tariffs or levy taxes to generate revenue (12 of the 13 states favored a tariff, but Rhode Island failed to ratify). This forced the Confederation Congress to largely depend upon each state to contribute to the nation’s finances. The Articles further exacerbated these issues by allowing each state to pass their own debtor laws, which created intense economic and political conflict for those citizens subjected to unfavorable legislation. War Bonds sold by the Continental Congress to help raise money were not honored in some states, or sold at 10-20 percent of their original value, which made it impossible for many soldiers, farmers, and merchants to pay off their debts or receive loans.

### Newburgh
Newburgh: George Washington had become the nation's most trusted public figure, but even his authority could not repair the weaknesses of the Confederation government. By 1783, the army's officers had waited years for compensation promised during the Revolution. Congress lacked the power to tax directly and depended on state contributions, many of which never arrived. The resulting frustration nearly turned the victorious army against the government it had served.
The crisis came to a head in the winter of 1782–83, while the army remained encamped at Newburgh, New York. An anonymous address written by Major John Armstrong, an aide to General Horatio Gates, urged the officers to consider refusing to disband until Congress met its obligations. A second address called for a meeting and threatened consequences if their grievances remained unanswered. The language raised the possibility of military defiance at the very moment the country needed to establish civilian control over its armed forces. Washington called the officers together on March 15, 1783, determined to prevent the dispute from becoming a confrontation between the army and Congress.
Washington condemned the anonymous appeals as the work of an enemy to both the army and the country. He urged the officers to demonstrate “unexampled patriotism and patient virtue” by trusting Congress to fulfill its obligations. He then attempted to read a letter from a supportive member of Congress. His eyesight had deteriorated, and he struggled through the opening lines before reaching for his spectacles. “Gentlemen, you must pardon me,” he remarked, “for I have not only grown gray but almost blind in the service of my country.” The gesture moved the officers. The man who had led them through eight years of war had placed his own sacrifices before them without demanding anything in return. They abandoned the confrontation and voted to thank Washington for his leadership. The Newburgh crisis passed, but the underlying problem remained: a national government unable to pay the men who had secured its independence depended on the patience of those men to survive.

### The Predators
The Predators: The Confederation government faced threats beyond its own army. The western lands presented an opportunity to raise revenue, but they also exposed the nation's diplomatic and military weakness. In 1784, Virginia surrendered its claims to territory north of the Ohio River, helping Congress establish a system for surveying and selling western lands. The Land Ordinance of 1785 provided the framework. Yet American settlement west of the Appalachians, where tens of thousands of people already lived, brought the United States into conflict with Spain, which controlled New Orleans and Florida. American farmers and merchants depended on access to the Mississippi River and its outlet to the Gulf of Mexico. Congress lacked the authority and leverage to secure that access through a unified national policy.
Britain presented another challenge. Despite the peace treaty of 1783, British troops remained in military posts across the Northwest, claiming that American states had failed to restore property confiscated from Loyalists during the Revolution. The continued occupation gave Britain a foothold in territory the United States claimed, while British officials maintained relationships with Native nations resisting American expansion. The Confederation government had little military capacity to compel a British withdrawal or protect American claims. The Continental Army had largely disbanded, and Congress could neither raise the revenue nor command the forces needed to enforce its authority.
The states also competed with one another. Each retained control over its own commercial policies, imposing tariffs and trade restrictions that disrupted interstate commerce. The absence of a central authority capable of resolving these disputes became especially clear along the Potomac River, where Virginia and Maryland contested navigation rights. In March 1785, representatives of the two states met at George Washington's Mount Vernon home and reached an agreement on the river's use. The meeting demonstrated that cooperation could work, but it also exposed the problem: the states had to arrange privately what a functioning national government should have been able to coordinate. The following year, delegates from five states met at Annapolis to discuss commercial disputes. Their meeting produced a call for a broader convention in Philadelphia to consider revising the Articles of Confederation. The effort to settle a disagreement over commerce had opened the door to a much larger debate over the future of the Union.

### Shays’ Rebellion
Shays’ Rebellion: In 1786, many patriots who postponed their livelihoods to fight the British, were promised soldiers salaries. However, the Articles lacked the power to raise or levy taxes, and could not pay the veterans their deserved salary. Many former soldiers resumed their livelihoods as farmers, and without compensation for their service, found themselves owing back taxes imposed by the new Massachusetts legislature (Gov. James Bowdoin) and could not afford to pay for their land. Without any funds to pay their taxes, many state courthouses began foreclosing upon many farmer’s lands and livestock to collect the debts owed. The farmers now stood in need of protection from the very republic they had fought to defend. Many had served in the Continental Army, and for that service they received foreclosure notices instead of pay. In response, many disgruntled farmers organized, some armed, to surround the courthouse to prohibit any lawyers or judges to enter, effectively shutting down all court proceedings. These small insurrections, led by former Continental Army Captain, Daniel Shays, began spreading throughout Massachusetts, closing more courts, but each without the use of violence. These farmers used the same rhetoric from the American Revolution against the state of Massachusetts, but the Massachusetts government saw them as rebellious insurrectionists, who needed to be dealt with. Massachusetts petitioned the federal government for military assistance to quell the rebellion. But the Articles of Confederation lacked the power to provide any military response, forcing the Massachusetts governor to raise a privately funded army. Many western farmers saw this as a near replication of British tyranny, causing many to side with the rebellion. The private army, which was larger than the entire American army, marched into western Massachusetts. Upon hearing about the fighting force, Shays and other men decided to arm themselves with guns and ammunition at the armory, but were turned away with cannon fire. Shays and other leaders fled into Vermont, and a Massachusetts court sentenced them to death in absentia. Shays’ Rebellion confirmed two glaring deficiencies of the federal government; the power to tax, and the right to raise a standing army.

### The Road to Philadelphia
The Road to Philadelphia: The combination of economic, political, diplomatic, and military catastrophes encouraged representatives from each state to meet in order to revise the Articles of Confederation. Several influential leaders like Alexander Hamilton and Benjamin Franklin, began voicing their support for a stronger federal government, calling themselves “Nationalists.” By 1787, the ineptitude of the Articles of Confederation had become clearly evident, suggesting a need for more federal power. At Annapolis in September 1786, with delegates from only five states present, the meeting collapsed; in the months that followed, Virginia representative James Madison drew up what he called the “Vices of the Political System of the United States.” Madison listed 11 of what he believed were the largest failures of the Articles of Confederation. By the time Madison was finished assaulting the provincial government, any initial plans to simply revise the Articles now seemed vastly insufficient. As a result, the Annapolis Convention came to realization that the issue of interstate trade was merely one of the widespread “embarrassments” of the Articles of Confederation. Indeed, advocates for a stronger, centralized power argued for the need to strengthen the federal government by extending the authority to levy taxes, raise a standing army, regulate commerce and negotiate foreign affairs. This new government would decrease the power of the legislature, provide an executive and judiciary, eliminate equal representation (Rhode Island), and extend personal liberties. “Dictated by an anxiety for the welfare of the United States,” Alexander Hamilton’s report proposed a convention for all of the states to meet in Philadelphia the following year.
But the problems facing the Confederation did not exist apart from slavery. Taxation raised the question of whether enslaved people would count toward a state’s obligations. Commerce meant confronting a trade in human beings that sustained the economies of several states. The weakness of the national government raised questions about who would command troops, suppress resistance, and defend the Union. Representation brought the conflict into the structure of government itself: would enslaved people count toward political power, and who would exercise that power? Every institutional question the delegates carried to Philadelphia had a second dimension. Slavery already shaped the interests they represented, the compromises they would consider, and the government they intended to build.
For nearly 11 years, the Continental Congress not only guided the colonies through a revolution, but also managed to maintain enough stability during the most critical time in the nation’s infant history. The adoption of the Articles of Confederation reflected the need for a governing body, but were intentionally designed to provide ultimate freedom to the states. From 1781 until 1787, the Articles provided just enough cooperation and interstate cohesion to dissuade any predatory foreign power from capitalizing on the young nation’s vulnerability. Nonetheless, without a central power to regulate the country’s finances and maintain domestic order, the American experiment would soon fail.

## Part III · The Room


### Philadelphia, May 1787
Philadelphia, May 1787: By 1786, the Articles of Confederation had exposed the weaknesses of a government deliberately denied the power to tax, regulate commerce, or enforce its decisions. State legislatures had grown powerful, while executives remained weak. Economic disputes, political rivalries, and military failures threatened the survival of the Union. The Annapolis Convention called for another meeting, and Alexander Hamilton urged all thirteen states to send delegates to Philadelphia the following year. Shays’ Rebellion in Massachusetts gave the proposal new urgency. When delegates from twelve states assembled in May 1787, their official task remained the revision of the Articles. Whether revision could solve the problem had already become doubtful.
Two of the nation’s most influential political figures would be absent. John Adams had gone to London and Thomas Jefferson to Paris, both serving as diplomats. Their absence left room for two men whose backgrounds could hardly have been more different. Alexander Hamilton, born in the West Indies to an unmarried mother and a Scottish trader, had risen through military service and political ambition. James Madison, the son of a prosperous Virginia planter, brought years of study and planning to the convention. Both would help shape the debate over the nation’s future.
The convention also brought together familiar figures, including an eighty-one-year-old Benjamin Franklin and George Washington, who reluctantly agreed to attend. Washington had long warned that the Confederation government could not hold the states together. He called it “a rope of sand” and feared the country stood on the verge of “anarchy and confusion.” Foreign powers could exploit the divisions, and the states lacked the unity to defend their common interests. The delegates unanimously chose Washington to preside.
Washington’s presence gave the convention an authority no other delegate could match. In his military uniform, he sat above the assembly in a wooden chair whose back displayed a half-sun rising over the horizon. Franklin noticed the image and kept returning to it throughout the summer. At the convention’s close, he reflected that he had often wondered whether the sun behind Washington’s chair was rising or setting. Now, he said, he knew it was “a rising sun and not a setting sun.” The remark captured the hope that the delegates had accomplished something capable of outlasting the Confederation.
The convention reached a quorum on May 25, 1787, in the Pennsylvania State House, the same building where the Declaration of Independence had been signed. Washington became its presiding officer, and William Jackson was elected secretary over Temple Franklin, Benjamin Franklin’s grandson. Four days later, on May 29, the delegates began their formal consideration of a new plan for government. They would remain in Philadelphia until September 17. Of the fifty-five men who participated, thirty-nine signed the Constitution; thirty-three attended every session. Twenty-five owned enslaved people, and some delegates brought enslaved men and women with them to Philadelphia. The proceedings took place behind closed doors, shielding the debate from the press and the public. The men were designing a government for a nation, but the people who would live under it had no place in the room.

### New Men
New Men: By 1787, a new generation of political leaders had begun to replace many of the men who led the Revolution. Of the convention’s fifty-five delegates, only eight had signed the Declaration of Independence, while twenty-two had served in the War for Independence. Their experiences differed, but many shared a conviction that the Articles of Confederation had left the nation too weak to govern itself. The older revolutionary generation remained wary of concentrated authority and protective of state independence. Others, shaped by the war and its demands, looked toward a stronger national government.
The delegates differed in age and experience, but not in the social position they occupied. They were all white men, overwhelmingly prosperous, and drawn from the ranks of lawyers, merchants, planters, and large farmers. More than half had attended college, an extraordinary distinction in a country where fewer than one in a thousand Americans had done so. Critics of the Revolution argued that independence had simply transferred power from British hands to an American elite, leaving ordinary people with little more than a change of rulers.
There was some truth in that criticism. Yet the delegates differed from Europe’s ruling classes in one important respect: none held a royal title or inherited noble rank. Benjamin Franklin and Alexander Hamilton represented the possibility of rising through talent and achievement rather than birth. But a society without a hereditary aristocracy could still preserve sharp divisions of wealth and power. The convention’s immediate task was to strengthen the federal government and decide how its authority would be divided, exercised, and checked. Who would govern was largely settled before the delegates arrived. Men like themselves would make those decisions. Who belonged, however, remained unresolved. The men seated behind the baize-covered tables would define the purposes of a central government, the powers it possessed, and the mechanisms by which it operated, without ever identifying all the people included in the Preamble’s opening words: “We the People.”
The debate over the new government centered on two questions: how much authority should the national government possess, and how should that authority be divided? The nationalists, led by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, John Jay, and George Washington, argued that the government needed powers the Articles had denied it: to tax, regulate commerce, establish a national currency, and maintain an army. Their opponents feared that concentrating those powers in a distant government would weaken the states and threaten the liberties won in the Revolution. George Mason, Patrick Henry, and Elbridge Gerry became prominent defenders of state authority. Beneath the disagreement over the reach of federal power lay another concern: how to prevent any one branch of government from dominating the others. Several states had legislatures powerful enough to manipulate their executives and courts. The new government, its architects argued, needed a more deliberate division of authority.

### The Virginia Plan
The Virginia Plan: On May 29, 1787, Governor Edmund Randolph of Virginia presented a proposal that would transform the convention’s debate. The plan belonged to James Madison, who had spent years studying the weaknesses of the Articles and developing a stronger national system. Madison’s proposal confronted three major obstacles. Many delegates still regarded their states as independent political authorities and resisted surrendering power to a national government. Others doubted that a large, diverse republic could survive; they believed republican government worked best in smaller, more homogeneous societies. And for men whose understanding of liberty rested on resistance to British authority, the prospect of a powerful central government raised the specter of monarchy.
Randolph opened by detailing the failures of the Confederation government and arguing that the Articles should be replaced, not merely amended. Drawing on the political theory of Montesquieu, the plan divided national authority among three branches: a legislature, an executive, and a judiciary headed by a supreme tribunal. The legislature would have two houses, both apportioned according to population or financial contributions, and the national government could veto state laws that conflicted with federal authority. The proposal would shift power decisively away from the states.
The smaller states recognized the danger immediately. Proportional representation threatened their equal standing in Congress, while a national executive and a federal veto over state laws raised fears of a government that could overrule them at nearly every turn. Randolph’s proposal consumed most of the day, but its consequences would occupy the convention for weeks. The delegates had begun by asking how to strengthen the Union. Now they had to decide how much of their states’ authority they were willing to surrender.

### The New Jersey Plan
The New Jersey Plan: New Jersey delegate William Paterson, the most vocal opponent of the Virginia Plan, took the floor nearly two weeks later. At five feet two inches tall, the Princeton graduate was an unlikely candidate to lead the defenders of state authority. Paterson, who scribbled extensive notes during Randolph’s presentation, addressed the convention by arguing against an executive power, which Randolph had called the “fetus of monarchy,” and a bicameral national legislature that eliminated equal representation of the states. Paterson reminded the convention that they had met only to “revise” the Articles, not abolish the Confederacy for a powerful central government. Known as the New Jersey Plan, Paterson conceded the need for federal power to levy taxes, regulate commerce, conduct foreign business, and form a standing army. He next proposed that the federal legislature should consist of just one house, and each state would receive equal representation. He feared how a larger state could effectively bully a smaller state under the Virginia Plan. A small-state delegate voiced his support for Paterson’s rebuttal, declaring that he would rather submit to a foreign power than be thrown under the dominion of the larger states. While this ensured the support of the smaller states, the political failure of equal representation is what required a revision of government in the first place.

### The Great Compromise
The Great Compromise: The convention remained at an impasse for nearly three weeks, prompting the Deist Benjamin Franklin to propose that the delegates offer a collective prayer. Then on July 16th, the convention adopted a compromise of the two plans, proposed by Roger Sherman of Connecticut. Under the Connecticut Compromise, the federal legislature would contain two houses; one to represent the people and the other to serve the interest of the states. One house would be determined by state population (the House of Representatives), and the other would consist of equal representation (the Senate). Under the compromise, each state’s representation in the House depended on its population. The question: whose population counted? Another key provision of the Connecticut Compromise was the creation of a strong executive. Executives and Presidents had remained largely symbolic and weak, which was by design. Under the new proposal, the executive would have the power to veto legislation, command the army, and direct foreign affairs. Naturally, the old revolutionaries, many of whom saw an executive as another potential King George, vehemently objected. The objectors to the executive received two compromises in return; the Electoral College and the assumption that George Washington would become the new President. Because a state’s electoral votes equaled its House seats plus its two senators, every additional representative also meant another presidential elector. The three-fifths clause thus increased slaveholding states’ power in Congress and presidential elections by counting enslaved people who had no political voice of their own. In 1800, those additional electoral votes helped elect Thomas Jefferson.
The arithmetic of July 16 is not a museum piece. As I tell my students: “Add today’s House to today’s Senate, add three electors for the District of Columbia, and you get 538 — the number of electoral votes in every presidential election you will ever vote in; the magic number is 270.” And because nearly every state hands all of its electors to whoever carries it, a state whose result is never in doubt hardly sees a presidential campaign at all (a Utah voter can go an entire election without a single presidential ad), while a handful of close states see nothing else: in 2020 the Electoral College came down to 42,918 votes spread across Arizona, Georgia, and Wisconsin. When a candidate camps in one state and never visits another, that is the Great Compromise still allocating power, two and a third centuries after the room where it was cut.

### The Machinery
The Machinery: The Constitution also included a judicial branch, including a Supreme Court, whose members were to be appointed by the executive and approved by the Senate. The new plan of government attempted to address the inadequacies of the Articles, reserve state power, and provide political egalitarianism. The legislative process, according to Article 1, was designed to be slow and difficult. Early factions and disagreements among the delegates required that new laws truly represent the will of the people and that old laws could not be removed by temporary movements and fads, “Political winds are always changing.” Furthermore, the frequency of legislative and executive elections provides losing interests with optimism and incentives to campaign, without violence or revolution. Lastly, most delegates looked upon the general population as too ignorant to entrust with any critical decision making. Therefore, the new constitution allowed for each state to create its own voting laws.
Four months of argument produced a document of roughly 4,000 words, short enough to read in an afternoon and spare enough to leave the details of the new government to the people who would have to run it. Two ideas carry the whole structure: federalism, which hands some powers to the nation and leaves others with the states; and the “checks and balances” that pit the national government’s three branches against one another, the arrangement most Americans call the “separation of powers.” Next to the Articles of Confederation, the new national government looked like a different animal entirely. The president would carry out the laws and command the armed forces. Congress gained the powers the Articles had denied it: to tax, to borrow, to regulate commerce, to declare war, to conduct relations with foreign and Indian nations, and to provide for the “general welfare.” Madison went further still and asked for a congressional veto over state laws; the convention balked.
The Constitution did, however, declare all national legislation the “supreme Law of the Land.” It also put property further beyond the reach of state legislatures. States lost the power to issue paper money, to throw up barriers against one another’s trade, and to tax imports or exports. The new Constitution represented the most democratic plan for government in the history of the world, a democratic republic, however it also had some striking omissions. Whether a state could ever leave the Union went unasked, the Constitution never defined who counted as a citizen, and the issue of slavery remained unresolved. The Constitution drew two further boundaries around political membership. It disqualified “Indians not taxed” from being counted among a state’s population. It also gave Congress the power to set “an uniform Rule of Naturalization,” taking the terms of citizenship out of the hands of the individual states. In 1790 the First Congress used that power to restrict naturalization to “free white persons.”
When the delegates finished, they had designed a government without precedent anywhere in the world. It borrowed from the republics of antiquity and from English political tradition, added a few cautious democratic innovations, and tried to balance the power of the nation against the power of the states. Complicated and bitterly contested, the plan faced a hard fight for ratification.
Federalism divided authority between the national and state governments. The Constitution delegated certain powers to the federal government, reserved others to the states, and recognized powers both could exercise. The separation of powers divided national authority among three branches, each with means of checking the others: the president could veto legislation, Congress could impeach officials and confirm presidential appointments, and the courts would soon claim the power to review laws for constitutionality. These arrangements reflected two larger principles. Republicanism placed governing authority in elected representatives rather than a monarch. Popular sovereignty located the ultimate source of political power in the people themselves. But the language left the boundaries of that political community unsettled.

## Part IV · The Elephant in the Room

### The Elephant in the Room
The issue of slavery, as a national concern, first arrived during the Second Continental Congress, when delegates from South Carolina and Georgia objected to the slave-trade passage in the original draft of the Declaration of Independence and forced its removal. As the Revolutionary War continued, the rhetoric of freedom did not go unrecognized by the enslaved. Encouraged by both the British and Patriots to join their cause in exchange for freedom, African Americans actively pursued ways to achieve their own liberty. Several colonies also attempted to end slavery. Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and Pennsylvania, whose economies remained largely disconnected to slavery, included the purchase of slaves as part of their non-importation agreements. Other states proposed a measure to recruit slaves to the Continental Army, however it was blocked again by South Carolina and Georgia. After the war, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Rhode Island moved against slavery by court decision or gradual emancipation, and in 1782, Virginia made it lawful for slave owners to free their “property” by private act. Even many Southern delegates believed, as the economist Adam Smith, that slavery was an economically unsustainable institution, and would most likely disappear by the turn of the century. The unforeseen but inevitable issue of slavery brought the progress of the Constitutional Convention to an immediate stop. Both slave owner and non-slave owner alike recognized the outright hypocrisy that slavery presented to the democratic and egalitarian goals of the nation, yet slave labor had become so deeply entrenched in Southern society that the immediate termination of the practice was inconceivable. During the convention, George Mason and James Madison, both slave owners, denounced slavery and wanted to see its end, while Alexander Hamilton helped found an abolition society and Benjamin Franklin presided over one. However, the Southern colonies viewed slavery, not only as vital to their economic prosperity, but crucial to their political power as well. Because of the slave economy of the South, most of the colonies’ laborers and immigrants chose to live in Northern colonies. As a result, the North experienced higher populations and growth rates.

### Three-Fifths
Three-Fifths: Under the proposed constitution, Southern states would receive less representation, and consequently hold less power. Seeing their future political weakness, Southern delegates argued that their slaves count towards their state populations. Northern delegates objected, based upon the fact that slaves were not considered citizens, and therefore could not be counted. Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts famously asked why, if the South could count its slaves, the North could not count its cattle and horses. Again, the convention remained at an impasse, until James Wilson of Pennsylvania, proposed the Three-Fifths Compromise. Wilson’s formula counted three of every five enslaved people toward a state’s seats in the House, and with that arithmetic the slaveholding South gained more representatives than its free population could ever have earned on its own. Just as telling, the compromise kept any claim of property in human beings out of the text, an omission Wilson, an opponent of slavery, had fought to secure.
The sharper point is the contradiction: slaveholders demanded that enslaved people count fully when counting them increased slaveholding power, while denying them any standing as persons when rights or freedom came into question. The northern position ran in the opposite direction, seeking to exclude them from the population used to apportion representation. The compromise counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a person for that purpose. Sixty percent had nothing to do with human worth. It was the fraction of a population the Constitution allowed slaveholders to turn into political power.

### The Fugitive Slave Clause
The Fugitive Slave Clause: Adding to the growing sectionalism between the North and South, was the passage of the Northwest Ordinance, which banned slavery in the new territory. Ironically, the desire to end the spread of slavery forced Southern states to empower their slave owners. Lastly, slavery became further engrained into the political makeup of the Constitution with the “Fugitive Slave Clause.” During the war, many slaves escaped into Northern colonies, Canada, and England. Southern delegates saw this trend as a danger to their economic and political power, and proposed that “No person legally held to service or labour in one state escaping into another shall . . . be discharged from such service or labour . .” Like the other four clauses that dealt directly with slavery, this one avoids the word altogether, speaking only of “person[s] held to Service or Labour.” Article 4 says that states are to “give full faith and credit to laws and judicial decisions of other states,” which required criminals to be returned to the state where a crime was committed.

### The Slave Trade Clause
The Slave Trade Clause: The last significant compromise made over slavery is known as the Slave Trade Clause (Which allowed for the nation’s participation in the international slave trade to continue until 1808). Again, with 5 of the states depending upon slaves as their primary labor force, any attempt to end the slave trade was met with staunch opposition. As a result, the remaining 8 states offered to prolong America’s participation in the slave trade 20 years in exchange for letting Congress pass navigation acts by a simple majority, which benefited northeastern shipbuilders, merchants, and sailors. Again, the compromise over the slave trade was made only to gain universal support for the Constitution and was purposefully designed to ensure that America’s future involvement in the slave trade became temporary.

### Emancipation
Emancipation: The compromises made over slavery did not result in the complete abandonment of emancipation. In 1777, Vermont became the first state to abolish slavery in its state constitution. By 1804, 6 other states would also declare slavery illegal. Although many Northern states had successfully ended chattel slavery, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Georgia remained economically and politically attached to the institution. Emboldened by the concessions to slavery provided by the Constitution, southern politicians (many of whom owned slaves) felt justified in their practices, and in many cases, even more dedicated to preserving both their “right” to own other human beings and the legality of the practice provided by state and federal legislation.

### The Warning Shot
The Warning Shot: In addition to the growing social and political assaults on slavery by Northern states, the passage of the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 was like a warning shot fired across the bow of southern economic and political autonomy. Until this law, the debate over slavery’s geographical legality remained confined to the 13 states. However, by legally banning slavery in the Northwest Territory, the federal government set in motion a new, passionate debate over Westward expansion and the spread of slavery into new lands. The acquisition of new western lands presented two significant concerns for the less populated Southern states; the number of votes they could cast in the House of Representatives and the legalization of slavery in those lands. The sectional divide within the nation began to appear as clearly as the Potomac River, with each region no longer seeing compromise over the issue of slavery as acceptable.

### The Word That Isn’t There
The Word That Isn’t There: Modern critics of the Constitution wrongly view its failure to end the institution of slavery as a characterological evil, representative of the nation as a whole. They cite the document’s coded terminology and compromises on slavery as clever mechanisms to disguise a collective desire to perpetuate slave labor and white superiority. Search the Constitution for “Black,” “white,” “slave,” or “slavery,” and every search comes back empty. Wherever the text touches the enslaved, it calls them “persons,” and that choice affirmed their humanity rather than denying it. As James Madison remarked during the Constitutional Convention, it was “wrong to admit in the Constitution the idea that there could be property in men.” The document refers to the enslaved in three different ways: “other persons” (Article I, Section 2, Clause 3), “such persons as any of the states now existing shall think proper to admit” (Article I, Section 9, Clause 1), and a “person held to service or labor in one state, under the laws thereof” (Article IV, Section 2, Clause 3). Although these periphrases failed to root out slavery from America, they are important, as they denied constitutional legitimacy to the institution. Slavery remained legal, but a slaveholder could search the Constitution cover to cover and never find it vouching for the institution. Read together, those phrases left slavery standing on state law alone, an institution the federal government tolerated but refused to bless.
Lincoln read the concealment as shame and fear at once. The thing was “hid away, in the constitution, just as an afflicted man hides away a wen or a cancer, which he dares not cut out at once, lest he bleed to death.” Frederick Douglass, who had every reason to read the document against itself, reached a different conclusion. He insisted that the “language of the law must be construed strictly in favor of justice and liberty” and argued that no clause, properly read, required the Constitution to protect slavery. The third witness settled what the first two disputed. In 1861, the Confederacy wrote its own constitution, copying the United States Constitution almost word for word in many respects, but making explicit what the original had left unnamed: it guaranteed “the institution of negro slavery” in plain language.
The final witness is the key. The Confederacy’s wording does not, by itself, settle every question about the intentions of the framers in 1787. It does show that when slaveholders wanted constitutional protection for slavery without ambiguity, they knew exactly how to write it.

### Was the Constitution Proslavery? The Debate
Was the Constitution Proslavery? The Debate: The Constitution's relationship to slavery remains one of the most contested questions in American history. Its framers proclaimed the principles of liberty and popular government while protecting an institution that denied both to millions of people. The document avoided the word slavery, but its compromises gave slaveholding states political advantages, protected the return of people who escaped bondage, and permitted the transatlantic slave trade to continue until 1808. Those provisions did not emerge without opposition. Some delegates resisted them, and abolitionist organizations continued to challenge slavery after the Constitution took effect.
The conflict reflected a nation already divided. Slavery had begun to decline in several northern states, where religious and political opposition was growing. In the South, plantation agriculture and the wealth it generated had made slaveholding central to the region's economy and social order. The Constitution brought those competing interests into the same political arrangement. Its compromises secured agreement on a national government, but they also gave slavery protections that its opponents would continue to contest.
That contest reached Congress in 1790, when two Quaker delegations and the Pennsylvania Abolition Society petitioned the House of Representatives to end the nation's participation in the slave trade. The society's memorial carried the signature of Benjamin Franklin, its president. His name gave the petition particular weight. Franklin had opposed slavery for decades; in 1737, as a young Philadelphia printer, he had published Benjamin Lay's attack on slaveholding, an episode Chapter 5 recounts. Now, near the end of his life, he placed his reputation behind a formal appeal to Congress.
The petition forced the House to confront an issue the Constitutional Convention had largely debated behind closed doors. Georgia representative James Jackson defended slavery as sanctioned by the Bible. New York representative John Laurance challenged the argument, asking whether the Sermon on the Mount endorsed owning another person for profit. Supporters also invoked the Constitution's preamble to argue that Congress possessed authority to act against the slave trade.
Franklin had one final answer. In a satire published shortly after the House debate, he invented a speech by an Algerian corsair defending the enslavement of Christians, using arguments that echoed Jackson's defense of slavery. The reversal exposed the weakness of a defense that depended on who held power and who suffered under it. Franklin died on April 17, 1790, making the petition and his final satire among his last public acts.
The House did not abolish the trade or authorize federal emancipation. Its response reaffirmed the constitutional limits on congressional action: Congress could not prohibit the importation of enslaved people before 1808 or interfere with slavery within the states. The immediate political conflict subsided, but the underlying dispute remained.
So—proslavery document or antislavery document? The honest answer is that the Constitution was an armistice, and both sides signed it believing time would vindicate their cause. One side would discover its mistake only after the deadliest war in American history.

## Part V · Ratification


### The Campaign
The Campaign: On September 17, 1787, the convention adjourned, deciding that 9 of the 13 states were needed to ratify the new constitution. Still lacking a consensus, the delegates returned to their states and began campaigning for their respective causes. Virginia, New York, North Carolina, and Rhode Island left the convention opposed to the new plan, and everyone knew that unless New York and Virginia embraced the Constitution, it had no hope of ever working. Seeking public support and in hopes of easing popular, Whig suspicions of government, supporters of the Constitution called themselves Federalists. The Federalists outplayed their opponents from the opening whistle, and they started with the name itself, one that promised a league of equals. This left the old revolutionaries with no other choice but to label themselves merely as the Anti-Federalists. Hamilton, John Jay, and Madison undertook a propaganda campaign for the Constitution, called the Federalist Papers. These initiatives paid off handsomely. The Federalist Papers targeted state legislators, lawyers, and the business class, not the average citizen. Newspapers and handbills flooded the states with the pro-Constitution arguments, which appealed to the will of the people, by using democratically elected state conventions to determine ratification.

### The Case Against
The Case Against: The Anti-Federalists, led by George Mason and Elbridge Gerry at the convention and by Patrick Henry and Samuel Adams in the ratification fight, championed states’ rights and personal liberties, while they rebuked the establishment of a strong, centralized power. According to them, the Constitution contained six significant flaws:
1. The Constitution did not create a new, reformed federal government, but a powerful “monstrosity.”
2. The sheer size of the new nation would require too much oversight and responsibility, giving the federal government too much power.
3. The powers outlined in the Constitution (federal authority) were too vague, like the Necessary and Proper Clause and the Commerce Clause, which could be interpreted by the executive and judicial branches to favor the federal government.
4. The President and the Senate were given too much power, “sowing the seeds of monarchy.”
5. The federal government should not be given the power to raise and maintain a standing army.
6. The Constitution contained no Bill of Rights.
While these claims were popular among many of the delegates, unlike their Federalist counterparts, the Anti-Federalists lacked recognizable, famous men, and the power of a national media campaign. Of the near 100 newspapers in circulation, 88 sided with ratification.
Five states ratified the Constitution within four months of the convention. On June 21, 1788, the necessary number of states had ratified, while New York and Virginia remained in a highly contested debate. However, fearing legal exclusion from the newly formed nation, Virginia (with Washington’s strong encouragement) agreed to the Constitution four days later.

### The Bill of Rights
The Bill of Rights: Support for the Constitution had to be bought with promises to the states. James Madison had to promise to do nothing to establish a state religion, restrain freedom of speech, nor censor the press. The Constitution, argued Madison, would do nothing to meddle in a variety of state affairs. Therefore, the first measure of business conducted by the inaugural federal legislature was to amend the Constitution with specific, enumerated personal freedoms, known as the Bill of Rights. In June 1789, Madison, who had once dismissed such lists as “parchment barriers,” introduced the amendments himself; Congress sent twelve to the states that September, and ten became law on December 15, 1791. The Bill of Rights spelled out personal liberty in careful detail: the freedoms to speak, assemble, worship, and bear arms, as well as protections from unreasonable search, from cruel punishment, and from censorship. It never identified to whom those rights applied. The Anti-Federalists lost the fight over ratification, but they won the amendments.
Although each state eventually chose ratification, the Anti-Federalists maintained their distrust of a federal government, arguing that the nature of man is to act in their own self-interest. Thomas Jefferson, who was in Paris serving as the Ambassador to France during the Constitutional Convention, was now home, and openly opposed any effort to strengthen the federal government. Despite the passage of the Bill of Rights, the Anti-Federalists continued to champion state sovereignty and warn against the tyranny of a central power. Instead of stabilizing the political landscape of the United States, ratification of the Constitution ironically marked the beginning of intense and divisive partisanship.
Washington, who had carefully avoided the debates, called the outcome “little short of a miracle, that the Delegates from so many different states … should unite in forming a system of national Government.” Franklin, as the delegates came forward to sign, answered the question he had asked himself all summer about the half-sun carved on the president’s chair: a rising sun, not a setting one. Ironically, the people who live under the system he celebrated know the least about it. Most Americans can name five members of the Simpson family, but struggle to name five rights protected by the First Amendment. The rights written in 1787 and 1791 are kind of like electricity. Nobody thinks about them until they stop working.

## Questions for Consideration

1. When did the American Revolution truly begin — and when did it truly end? Defend a terminal date: 1783, 1788, 1791, or later.
2. What had Americans recently experienced as they designed their state and federal governments? Explain how that experience shaped the Articles of Confederation — what, exactly, was the first constitution afraid of, and was the fear reasonable?
3. Federalists and Anti-Federalists agreed the Articles had failed; where did they part ways over how the new government should be built and what it should be allowed to do? Which Anti-Federalist objections read as prophetic today, and which as paranoid? Use the six-count indictment from Part V.
4. Was the Constitution meant to preserve slavery, or to end it? Build the best case for each side from this chapter’s evidence — the three compromises, the missing word, the Northwest Ordinance, the 1790 petitions — then commit to a verdict and defend it against the strongest counterargument you built.
5. The Three-Fifths Compromise is routinely described as the founders declaring an enslaved person “three-fifths of a human being.” Using this chapter, state precisely what the clause did — and then explain why the correction does not acquit the bargain.

## Then and Now

1. The arithmetic of July 16, 1787 is still counting. The Great Compromise built one chamber where population decides and one where it does not, and the Electoral College was handed the sum of the two. Add today’s House to today’s Senate, add the District of Columbia’s three, and you get 538; 270 wins. But the line it sorts Americans along is no longer the line the delegates argued about. They were bargaining between large states and small ones. Because nearly every state now hands all of its electors to whoever carries it, the compromise works as a bargain between states that are in play and states that are not — a Utah voter can go an entire election without seeing a single presidential ad, while in 2020 the whole contest came down to 42,918 votes spread across Arizona, Georgia, and Wisconsin.
2. Test it. The delegates built the compromise to keep large states from running the small ones. Does it still do that, or has it traded the inequality they debated for one they never discussed? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a rule written for one purpose that now does something else, name who gains from the drift, and say whether anyone chose it.
