CHAPTER 18
A House Divided
The Failure of Compromise, 1848–1861
“Hear the nation’s call, freemen, one and all, / Hear poor Kansas’ earnest cry: / See her bleeding land lift its beckoning hand; / Sons of freedom, come ye nigh.”
— “Bleeding Kansas”
In 1855 Thomas Crawford, one of the era’s most prominent American sculptors, was asked to design a statue to crown the new dome of the Capitol, still under construction. He proposed a Statue of Freedom: a woman in classical robes wearing a liberty cap. The secretary of war, Jefferson Davis of Mississippi, one of the country’s largest slaveholders, objected. In Roman times the cap of liberty had been bestowed on emancipated slaves, and Davis regarded it as “the badge of the freed slave”; its use, he feared, might suggest a connection between the enslaved’s longing for freedom and the liberty of free-born Americans. Crawford replaced the cap with a helmet crested with an eagle’s head and feathers. Years later, when the plaster model had to be taken apart for casting at the foundry of Clark Mills in Washington, it was an enslaved man, Philip Reid, who worked out how to separate its five sections, and who then kept the fires burning under the molds. Reid was freed on April 16, 1862, when slavery ended in the District of Columbia. On December 2, 1863, while the nation was embroiled in civil war, the last section of Freedom was bolted into place atop the dome, where it can still be seen today. By the 1850s, even the nation’s symbols of liberty had become contested ground.[[1]]
The Market Revolution, the Democratic Revolution, the Second Great Awakening, and Manifest Destiny had together launched a massive expansion into the West and helped forge a new American identity, as settlers, immigrants, free Black Americans, Native Americans, Mexicans, and women competed for their place in a rapidly changing society. The Supreme Court would try to settle who among them could be a citizen, and the election of 1860 would settle who governed; when neither answer held, the question passed to the battlefield. But the most significant consequence of those processes was the hardening of sectional tension between the industrial North and the Cotton Kingdom of the South. Northern Whigs saw the West as space for industry, railroads, and commerce; Southern Democrats saw room to expand slave-based agriculture. Each new territory set the two visions against each other, and event by event the nation moved toward a war that would pit brother against brother. Its argument is this: every compromise meant to keep slavery out of national politics — 1850, popular sovereignty, Dred Scott — instead carried it into every Northern town and courtroom, until a question that law and morals had both failed to settle passed to the method that, as I tell my students, “shattered” the moral arguments: power.[[2]]
### The Compromise of 1850
The Missouri Compromise had calmed sectional tension for a generation. But the war with Mexico added vast new territories, and almost at the moment the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed, gold was discovered in California. By the end of 1849, the non-Native population of California had grown to almost 100,000 — enough to qualify for statehood, and the territory applied as a free state. Heated debate erupted over whether slavery would be permitted in the lands won from Mexico, and as American gold-seekers clashed with Mexican, Native, and Asian Californians, the racial rhetoric of Manifest Destiny returned to the floor of Congress.[[3]]
Four answers competed. John C. Calhoun argued that slaves were property protected by the Constitution and that Congress had no power to bar slavery from any territory, and his position drew wide support among Southern Democrats. Other Democrats favored a more moderate solution: extend the Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific, dividing the new lands between slavery and freedom. Senator Lewis Cass of Michigan proposed what he called popular sovereignty, letting the settlers of each territory decide; Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois would later make the idea his own. At the other pole, antislavery senators such as Salmon P. Chase of Ohio and William H. Seward of New York opposed any compromise at all and insisted that slavery be barred from every territory; Seward told the Senate that “a higher law than the Constitution” forbade its spread.[[4]]
A decade earlier, the House had silenced antislavery petitions with the gag rule. Now Congress could talk of little else. President Zachary Taylor, a Louisiana slaveholder, startled his own section by demanding that California be admitted as a free state at once, and he stood in the way of any broader bargain. Henry Clay, in his last great effort, proposed one anyway, and on March 7, 1850, Daniel Webster of Massachusetts rose to support it: “I wish to speak to-day, not as a Massachusetts man, nor as a Northern man, but as an American.” Then Taylor died suddenly in July 1850, and his successor, Millard Fillmore, favored compromise. When Clay’s single “omnibus” bill failed at the end of that month, Stephen Douglas broke the package into separate bills and assembled a different majority for each. The Compromise of 1850 included five provisions:[[5]]
(1) California was admitted as a free state; (2) the slave trade — though not slavery itself — was abolished in the District of Columbia; (3) a new and far stronger Fugitive Slave Act punished anyone who failed to cooperate in the recovery of runaways; (4) the boundary dispute between Texas and New Mexico was resolved; and (5) the Utah and New Mexico territories were organized under popular sovereignty, leaving slavery there to be decided by their settlers.[[6]]
After 1850, antislavery critics grew certain that enslavers had taken control of the federal government — that a Southern “Slave Power” meant to make slavery a national institution — and they pointed to the three-fifths clause, which gave the South more representatives than its free population warranted. Hailed as a great political achievement, the Compromise staved off secession for another decade, but only by accepting the South’s standing condition, advanced since 1787: no Union without slavery. Southern lawmakers insisted that the Tenth Amendment secured each state’s right to slavery without federal interference, and militants called Fire-Eaters met at Nashville in June 1850 to discuss secession. Antislavery Northerners, demanding freedom for fugitives and slavery’s containment, refused to accept its legitimacy. Since 1787, a few men in Congress had decided where slavery could exist. Popular sovereignty took the question from them and handed it to the settlers — on its face, democracy at its finest. But slavery was on the ballot, not a candidate, and there would be no future elections. It was zero-sum, and the settlers played for keeps. Where the voting stayed peaceful it still did not go for freedom: Utah’s legislature wrote Black servitude into law in February 1852, and New Mexico’s in 1859 adopted a slave code prohibiting emancipation outright.[[7]] But the Compromise’s most damaging piece was its new Fugitive Slave Law, which poisoned the relationship between North and South by commanding every citizen to help capture people who had escaped slavery — many of whom had built lives, friendships, and families in their new communities.[[8]]
### Runaways and the Law
The law of runaways was as old as American slavery. As early as 1640, a Virginia court sentenced a runaway servant named John Punch, an African, to serve his master for life, while the two white servants who fled with him received only added years. In Somerset v. Stewart (1772), an English court ruled that James Somerset, an enslaved man brought to England by his owner, could not be forcibly carried out of the kingdom and sold in Jamaica; the ruling encouraged the enslaved to seek freedom and fed slaveholders’ fears. At the Constitutional Convention in 1787, two South Carolina delegates, Pierce Butler and Charles Pinckney, proposed that runaways be “delivered up like criminals,” and the result was the Fugitive Slave Clause told in Chapter 10. The clause provided no means of enforcement, so in 1793 Congress passed the first Fugitive Slave Act, which authorized a slaveholder or his agent to cross a state line, seize an alleged fugitive, take the captive before any judge or local magistrate, and, upon proof of ownership, receive a certificate to carry the person home.[[9]]
The Second Great Awakening caused many Americans to engage in both individual and communal reform efforts. Among these reformations was the Abolition Movement. Despite the unequal social status of African Americans in the North, many northern communities became more active in efforts to end slavery. From the 1820s until 1850, many northern states attempted to provide some legal protections for escaped slaves who faced potential recapture by passing Personal Liberty Laws. In Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842), the Supreme Court overturned the conviction of Edward Prigg, a slave catcher who had violated one such law, and struck the law down: the recovery of fugitives was a federal matter, and no state could obstruct it. But the Court added that states could not be compelled to enforce a federal law with their own officers. Ironically, northern states justified the legality of Personal Liberty Laws by using the South’s own argument against it: states’ rights.[[10]]
In October 1842, George Latimer and his pregnant wife, Rebecca, escaped from slavery in Norfolk, Virginia. The couple hid below deck on a ship headed north, and eventually made their way to Boston. Shortly after their arrival, Latimer was spotted by his master’s former employee. Latimer was quickly arrested and placed in jail to await trial. Word spread throughout Boston’s Black community, which inspired several prominent abolitionists to assist in Latimer’s release. Latimer’s attorney argued before Massachusetts Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw, who held strong antislavery views, that his client was entitled to a jury trial; however, the judge denied the request, saying Massachusetts could not obstruct a federal law. “The laws of Congress,” said Shaw, were to be obeyed, “however disagreeable to our natural sympathies or views of duty.” Large abolitionist rallies, called “Latimer Meetings,” were held throughout the state, demanding new state laws to protect fugitive slaves from capture. In what became known as the “Great Massachusetts Petition,” abolitionists gathered 64,526 signatures on paper scrolls, rolled like barrels into the Massachusetts State House. The petition called for an end to all state legal responsibility in assisting slave catchers, and for the right of fugitive slaves to testify on their own behalf and receive a trial by jury. Latimer’s freedom was purchased, and on March 24, 1843, Massachusetts passed its “Liberty Law,” which denied the participation of state authorities or the use of state property in the capture of a fugitive. It became the model for similar laws across the North. The adoption of Personal Liberty Laws sounded off alarm bells for the slaveholding South, which hardened its resolve to preserve its economic and political autonomy.[[11]]
### The Fugitive Slave Act
As sectional conflict increased, the constitutional settlement that had prevailed among the northern states began to break down. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 empowered southern slave owners to recapture their runaway slaves with federal support by penalizing state officials and individuals who interfered with federal marshals. Unlike the previous fugitive slave law, the Fugitive Slave Act removed the ability of state courts to hear cases involving runaway slaves. Instead, it created special commissioners, before whom alleged runaway slaves had no jury and no right to testify on their own behalf. Furthermore, the commissioners received monetary incentives to rule in favor of the slave catchers, earning $10 for returning the accused to slavery and only $5 for setting the accused free. In addition, any person aiding a fugitive by providing food, clothing, or shelter was subject to six months’ imprisonment and a $1,000 fine. Enslavers needed only to supply an affidavit to a federal marshal to capture a fugitive from slavery. And the law conscripted the bystander: “all good citizens,” it declared, “are hereby commanded to aid and assist in the prompt and efficient execution of this law.” Since a suspected fugitive had no voice in court, the law resulted in the kidnapping of free Blacks into slavery. The Act adversely affected the prospects of escape from slavery, particularly in states close to the North. One study finds that the act raised slave prices in the border states by as much as 35 percent relative to the Deep South, because escape had become so much harder.[[12]]
I ask my students to picture the law as it reached a Northern town: “Imagine a man who has lived in Pennsylvania for years — your neighbor, someone whose children play with yours, whom you have known your whole life. A slave catcher arrives with a paper: five-ten, 180 pounds, Black male. That description fits a thousand men, and it is set against a whole life you know. The marshal can summon you to help, and if you refuse, or hide him, you can go to prison.” Before the Fugitive Slave Law, many in the North easily shrugged off their connection to slavery as complicit components of the Cotton Kingdom. However, as a result of being compelled to assist in the capturing of runaways, many northerners who had developed meaningful relationships with African Americans experienced the trauma of seeing African Americans, some of whom were close friends, shackled and sent back to the horrors of slavery. By disrupting individual and communal relationships between northern whites and African Americans, the Fugitive Slave Law validated the claims of abolitionists and personalized the issue of slavery to many northerners, resulting in greater resistance and more determination than ever to end involuntary servitude. Southerners believed that the compromise met its goals in stopping the South from seceding from the Union, at least temporarily.
### Northern Resistance
Not all Northerners opposed slavery on moral grounds. The expansion of slave-based agriculture posed a significant threat to the factory system and other industries by driving down wages and taking up available land that would otherwise be used for small farmers and businesses. This growing concern over wage labor encouraged the formation of the Free Soil Party, which campaigned not to end slavery, but to contain it to where it already existed. The Fugitive Slave Law inspired northern abolitionists and sympathetic northern communities to assist runaway slaves in their pursuit of freedom. In what can be labeled the first measures of “civil disobedience,” many northern communities organized vigilance committees, which hid runaway slaves within homes, churches, and farms until they could be transported to sanctuary cities and Canada, warded off slave catchers and kidnappers, and conducted spectacular rescues. Their network, the Underground Railroad, and its most famous conductor, Harriet Tubman, are described in The American Reformation.[[13]]
In addition, several northern states passed their own laws to provide sanctuary to runaway slaves and to those who protected them. Furthermore, in 1854 the Wisconsin Supreme Court ruled the Fugitive Slave Law unconstitutional. This ruling, in combination with the passage of multiple Personal Liberty Laws, set off alarm bells in the South as slaveholders witnessed Northern efforts to delegitimize the lawful return of their property. Since the Constitutional Convention of 1787, Southerners had carefully negotiated the preservation and promulgation of chattel slavery, and most slave owners now considered the actions of Northern legislatures a violation of all previous agreements. In 1859 the Supreme Court, led by Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, ruled unanimously in Ableman v. Booth that the Fugitive Slave Act was constitutional and that no state court could override it. The ruling had little effect on abolitionists, who continued to use state laws to thwart fugitive slave reclamations. Surprisingly, the most influential and effective effort to fight slavery came not in the political sphere, but in the publication of a book that would become immortalized in American history.[[14]]
### Uncle Tom’s Cabin
Published in 1852 and authored by Harriet Beecher Stowe, daughter of the famous preacher Lyman Beecher, Uncle Tom’s Cabin provided millions of people with a vivid and shocking account of slavery. While living in Cincinnati, Ohio, Stowe crossed the Ohio River into Kentucky, where slavery was practiced; there, tradition holds, she came upon a slave auction and was traumatized at the sight of women separated from their children, who were sold to other planters. Furthermore, as a resident of Cincinnati, she had come across many fugitive slaves, who related to her their personal stories of the hellish conditions they were forced to endure. When her son died of cholera in 1849, Stowe truly understood the loss that so many enslaved women felt. She then decided to write the book, using stories from the narratives of formerly enslaved people, including Josiah Henson, Lewis Clarke, and Frederick Douglass. As a white woman, Stowe was seen as less threatening to white readers than Black abolitionists, helping her novel reach more readers. While the book was fiction, Stowe included detailed anecdotes from former slaves and members of the Underground Railroad. She later claimed that God wrote the book through her.[[15]]
Stowe’s work illustrated the brutality of slavery in ways never before described; Uncle Tom is beaten to death on the orders of his master, Simon Legree. In response, Southerners claimed the book to be an exaggeration and false representation of slavery, and it was reviled across the South. It sold 10,000 copies in the first week and 300,000 in the first year in the United States, and even more in Britain. Stowe’s vivid characters and portrayal of their struggles opened readers’ eyes to the realities of slavery and the humanity of enslaved people. The book created empathy among those who remained indifferent, first for the characters and, in turn, for enslaved individuals, and encouraged others to speak out. Legend holds that upon meeting her during the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln greeted Stowe as “the little woman who wrote the book that started this great war,” but there is no reliable record of the remark. Only a small fraction of the nation’s enslaved people ever escaped to the North; however, the reaction to Uncle Tom’s Cabin generated unprecedented northern resistance to southern attempts to recapture their property.[[16]]
### The Case of Anthony Burns
Uncle Tom’s Cabin generated unprecedented publicity and national attention to the horrors of slavery and the methods used by bounty hunters to capture runaway slaves. Light was also shed upon the capturing of free men, who were unlawfully sent to the South to become slaves. In 1854, the highly publicized case of Anthony Burns further galvanized abolitionists and many northerners. Burns, a slave from Virginia, was allowed to hire himself out and also had the freedom to take on additional jobs, as long as he paid his master a fee. He joined a church, where he became a preacher. Burns learned to read and write. At an early age he had learned that “there [was] a Christ who came to make us free” and felt “the necessity for freedom of soul and body.” In February 1854, he took steps to find freedom. While working in Richmond, Burns boarded a ship heading north, to the city of Boston. As a fugitive, Burns wrote a letter to his brother, who remained in bondage, to inform him of his whereabouts and to encourage him to escape and join him. Burns’s owner, Charles Suttle, intercepted the letter and, with hired help and legal authority from the Fugitive Slave Act, traveled to Boston, where Burns was arrested on May 24.[[17]]
As Burns awaited trial in a Boston courthouse, nearly 200 abolitionists surrounded the courthouse and demanded Burns’s release. The crowd grew to about two thousand, when a small group of Black and white men, led by a white minister, Thomas Wentworth Higginson, charged the building and broke down the door, but they were shot at and forced back by armed deputies; in the struggle a temporary deputy marshal, James Batchelder, was killed. In several similar situations, Boston abolitionists had forcefully freed captive runaways and successfully transported them to Canada. Determined to see the Fugitive Slave Act enforced, President Franklin Pierce sent marines and artillery to assist the guards watching over Burns. Pierce also ordered a federal ship to return Burns to Virginia after the trial. The commissioner, Edward G. Loring, ruled that Burns was a fugitive slave. On June 2, 1854, an estimated 50,000 people lined the streets of Boston to watch a shackled Anthony Burns walk toward the waterfront and board the waiting ship. In 1855 abolitionists purchased Burns’s freedom for $1,300, allowing him to return to the North to resume his fight against slavery.[[18]]
### Three Answers: Law, Morals, and Power
The Burns case forced a question I put to every class: Can something lawful be immoral? Can something moral be unlawful? The American Reformation introduced the tool for answering it, the three methods any movement can reach for — law, the product of a process everyone agreed to; morals, the beliefs a community holds; and, when both fail, power. In the 1850s, three famous men of Massachusetts answered the Fugitive Slave Law with a different method each.
Daniel Webster chose law. His position, as I put it to my students, comes down to four words: “We voted on this.” The Compromise was the product of a constitutional process, the Fugitive Slave Clause was part of the bargain that made the Union, and a citizen who disliked the result was bound by it all the same. Webster paid for that position with his reputation in New England. Henry David Thoreau chose morals. In July 1846 he had spent a night in the Concord jail for refusing to pay a tax to a government that waged war on Mexico and protected slavery, and in 1849 he published the essay now known as “Civil Disobedience”: an unjust law need not be obeyed, and a just person must be willing to accept the penalty for breaking it. William Lloyd Garrison chose something closer to power: if the Constitution protected slavery, then the Constitution itself had to go. In my shorthand, Garrison’s answer was “burn it down,” and on one afternoon he did exactly that.[[19]]
On July 4, 1854, a month after Burns was marched to the waterfront, the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society gathered at Harmony Grove in Framingham, outside Boston. Garrison held up a copy of the Fugitive Slave Law and set it on fire. He burned Commissioner Loring’s decision in the Burns case, and the charge of a federal judge who had sought indictments against the would-be rescuers. Then he raised a copy of the United States Constitution, called it “a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell” — words from the prophet Isaiah — and burned that too. “So perish all compromises with tyranny!” he cried, and the crowd answered, “Amen!” On the same platform that day, Thoreau delivered the address later published as “Slavery in Massachusetts”: “The law will never make men free; it is men who have got to make the law free.” Two of the three answers stood on one stage. A century later, the same choices would return: Martin Luther King Jr., writing from a Birmingham jail, would stand in Thoreau’s line, and Malcolm X, demanding “the ballot or the bullet,” would echo Garrison’s willingness to break with the system altogether.[[20]]
### The Kansas–Nebraska Act
The Compromise of 1850 had left one problem untouched: the vast unorganized lands west of Missouri and Iowa, the future Kansas and Nebraska. Under the Missouri Compromise, all of it lay north of 36°30′ and was closed to slavery — and the South adamantly opposed any more free states.[[21]]
The railroad forced the issue. As California grew and more Americans headed west, plans for a transcontinental railroad took shape, and fortunes would depend on where its eastern terminal lay; Chicago and St. Louis were the leading candidates. Stephen Douglas wanted Chicago, and Chicago needed the Nebraska country organized first, which meant removing the Native nations that held it. To win Southern votes for a territorial bill, Douglas wrote popular sovereignty into it, apparently never expecting proslavery settlers to mobilize in earnest. The bill opened land long promised to freedom to slavery, and Northern politicians were outraged. President Franklin Pierce helped push it through, and on May 30, 1854, the Kansas–Nebraska Act became law. It repealed the Missouri Compromise, made popular sovereignty the law of the Kansas and Nebraska territories, and set off a race of proslavery and antislavery settlers into Kansas.[[22]]
### Strange, Discordant, and Even Hostile Elements
The two-party system of Democrats and Whigs had navigated the country through sectional tension since the age of Jackson. In 1854 the Whigs crumbled over slavery, and two new parties rose to take their place. The Know-Nothings, formally the American Party, drew many former Whigs into a crusade against Irish and German Catholic immigrants. The Republicans gathered disaffected Democrats, former Whigs, Free Soilers, and abolitionists into what Abraham Lincoln later called a party “of strange, discordant, and even hostile elements.” Not all Republicans wanted to end slavery; most adopted a policy of containment, allowing it to continue only where it already existed. Many opposed its expansion not on moral grounds but to protect Northern workers: slavery, they argued, drove down the wages of free men and degraded the dignity of manual labor. The United States, Senator William Seward of New York predicted in 1858, “must and will, sooner or later, become either entirely a slaveholding nation, or entirely a free-labor nation.” Lincoln, an ex-Whig from Illinois, agreed. “A house divided against itself cannot stand,” he told Illinois Republicans that June.[[23]]
Slavery splintered the Democrats too, though the party was bolstered by Irish and German immigrants whom nativists had driven away. By 1860 the Republican Party had absorbed Democrats and Whigs alike who felt alienated by Southern policymakers. The end of the two-party system produced a sectional, multiparty map: Republicans almost entirely in the North, Democrats in both sections but split between them, and smaller parties finishing off any hope of cooperation between regions. The election of 1856 previewed it. The Democrats nominated James Buchanan of Pennsylvania, a Northern man with Southern sympathies — a “doughface,” in the slang of the day. The Republicans ran the famed explorer John C. Frémont, “the Pathfinder”; the Know-Nothings ran the former president Millard Fillmore. To most Americans, the Republicans seemed too radical on slavery, and Buchanan’s support for popular sovereignty made the Democrats the safer choice. Buchanan won every slave state but Maryland, which went to Fillmore, along with five free states, and became the fifteenth president. But the electoral map carried a warning. Frémont had won eleven free states in the party’s first national campaign, and with Pennsylvania and one more state, the Republicans would have won the White House.[[24]]
### Bleeding Kansas
Before the Kansas–Nebraska Act, the Kansas Territory was thinly settled by whites; negotiations with the Kansa and other Native nations had opened only small settlements. After it, proslavery and antislavery forces rushed in, each hoping to win political control. Kansas elections were riddled with fraud, as armed Missourians — the “Border Ruffians” — crossed into the territory, if only for the day, to cast their votes. In the March 1855 election for the first territorial legislature, more than 6,300 ballots were counted in a territory whose census had found fewer than 3,000 eligible voters; a congressional investigating committee later concluded that most of the votes were illegal. Missouri’s slaveholders feared that a free Kansas on their border would draw their slaves to escape, and Southern alarmists warned that if Kansas fell, the whole West would follow like dominoes. Yet most settlers who came to Kansas from North and South only wanted to homestead in peace. They were not interested in the conflict over slavery, but they found themselves living on its battlefield.[[25]]
Antislavery irregulars, the Jayhawkers, collided with the proslavery Border Ruffians. The name has outlived the war. The University of Kansas still calls its teams the Jayhawks. I’ve written on American mascots, and I use the name to show my students how a word’s meaning gets settled after the fact. Where “jayhawker” came from, historians disagree; early on, it was applied to irregular troops and pillaging bands on both sides, and the cartoon bird that KU fans know was drawn in 1912. The winners decided what the word would mean. The other side’s banner flies on campuses too: at UNLV, the Rebels took their name in a student rebellion against the Reno flagship, and their first mascot was a wolf in a Confederate uniform. Both names are still on the jerseys.[[26]]
The religious division of the decade sharpened the political one. The Methodists and Baptists had already split along sectional lines, as The American Reformation recounts, and pulpits North and South quoted the Bible to support opposite positions. Sermons turned political questions into moral ones, and some ministers armed their side. The Reverend Henry Ward Beecher, brother of Harriet Beecher Stowe, raised money for rifles for free-state settlers, and the Sharps rifles shipped to Kansas became known as “Beecher’s Bibles.” On May 21, 1856, a proslavery force of some 750 to 800 men rode into the free-state town of Lawrence, hoping to drive its settlers from Kansas; they burned the Free State Hotel, smashed the presses of the town’s two newspapers, and looted homes, and one of the attackers was killed by falling masonry. Three days later, John Brown and a small band — four of his sons and his son-in-law among them — answered the sack of Lawrence by dragging five proslavery settlers from their cabins along Pottawatomie Creek and hacking them to death with broadswords. One of Brown’s sons was killed that August in the fighting that followed, and Brown left Kansas.[[27]]
### The Caning of Charles Sumner
On May 19 and 20, 1856, Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts, a fierce critic of slavery, delivered a speech in the Senate titled “The Crime Against Kansas.” He condemned the Kansas–Nebraska Act and mocked its champions, Stephen Douglas and Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina, in terms that violated the Senate’s customs. Slavery, he said, was the mistress to whom Butler had made his vows — “the harlot, Slavery.” Two days later, Butler’s kinsman Preston Brooks, a congressman from South Carolina, walked into the Senate chamber and beat Sumner over the head with a gold-headed cane as he sat at his desk. Brooks kept striking after the cane broke, until Sumner collapsed unconscious, while a fellow South Carolina congressman, Laurence Keitt, brandished a pistol and held off those who tried to intervene. Sumner did not return to his seat for more than three years; Massachusetts left his desk empty as a reminder. Southerners showed their support for Brooks by sending him new canes. In the North, “Bleeding Sumner” joined “Bleeding Kansas” as a Republican rallying cry.[[28]]
### The Dred Scott Decision
By the mid-1850s, slavery had poisoned the relationship between North and South: it had divided the churches, fractured the party system, and turned Congress into a place where senators were beaten at their desks. Millard Fillmore, Franklin Pierce, and James Buchanan were all Northern men who conciliated Southern lawmakers. With every other national institution failing, Americans looked to the Supreme Court as the last resort to settle the question of slavery — Republicans and antislavery sympathizers hopefully, on behalf of four million people in bondage. In March 1857, the Court ruled on one of the most consequential cases in its history.
The case began in the 1830s. Dr. John Emerson, an army surgeon, took the man he enslaved, Dred Scott, to posts in the free state of Illinois and then to Fort Snelling in the Wisconsin Territory, where slavery was barred by the Missouri Compromise. There Scott married Harriet Robinson, and their family began. Emerson later returned to Missouri, and after his death in 1843 the Scotts were held by his widow, Irene Emerson. On April 6, 1846, Dred and Harriet Scott sued for their freedom in St. Louis on the ground that their residence on free soil had made them free. The case wound through Missouri’s courts for a decade and finally reached the Supreme Court as Dred Scott v. Sandford. Much more than one man’s freedom was at stake: both sides seem to have understood the case as a test of the whole slavery question. How else, I ask my students, could an enslaved man who could not sign his own name know his legal rights and afford a lawyer? Scott signed his petition with an X. His first attorney, Francis B. Murdoch, had been the prosecuting attorney in Alton, Illinois, in 1837 when a proslavery mob murdered the abolitionist printer Elijah Lovejoy. The questions before the Court were whether Scott, as a Black man, could sue in federal court as a citizen, and whether his years on free soil had made him free.[[29]]
Two days after Buchanan’s inauguration, on March 6, 1857, the Court answered both questions against him, 7–2. President Buchanan, who had pressed the Court for a sweeping decision that would put the slavery question to rest, had privately urged a Northern justice to join the Southern majority. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney of Maryland wrote the opinion of the Court. Black Americans, he declared, were not and could not be citizens of the United States, and so Scott had no right to sue. Surveying the state laws in force when the Constitution was drafted, Taney concluded that they showed “a perpetual and impassable barrier was intended to be erected between the white race and the one which they had reduced to slavery”; at the founding, he wrote, Black people had been regarded as “so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect.” And Taney went further: Congress had no power to bar slavery from any territory, which meant that the Missouri Compromise — already repealed in 1854 — had been unconstitutional all along. Scott remained a slave. Within weeks his owners’ family freed him and his family; Dred Scott died the following year.[[30]]
The decision gave the South legal justification for holding people in bondage anywhere in the territories, and Southern editors hailed it as settling every question about slavery in the South’s favor. It also galvanized the antislavery movement as no previous event had. It cut directly against the Republican platform of stopping slavery’s expansion. What, Northerners now asked, would stop slaveholders from bringing their slaves into the North itself? The decision confirmed Northern suspicions of a Slave Power conspiracy and sank the reputation of the Court to the lowest point in its history. In February 1858, Buchanan asked Congress to bring Kansas into the Union as a slave state under the Lecompton Constitution, drafted by a proslavery convention, never fairly submitted to the voters, and boycotted by the free-state majority, who had written a free-state constitution of their own. Slavery, Buchanan announced, existed in Kansas “by virtue of the Constitution of the United States,” and Kansas was “at this moment as much a slave State as Georgia or South Carolina.” The Senate approved Lecompton; the House refused. When Kansans finally voted on it later in 1858, they rejected it overwhelmingly. Stephen Douglas broke with Buchanan over Lecompton, and the rupture would follow him into the most publicized Senate race in American history.[[31]]
The decision had one more consequence that Southerners did not foresee. In fueling the belief that the federal government had been captured by slaveholders, it led many Northern businessmen and lawmakers to wonder whether they needed the Cotton Kingdom at all. The expansion of transportation and commerce and the influx of European labor had convinced many that the North could prosper on its own. The idea of separating the two sections was not only a Southern one.
### Complex Characters: Roger B. Taney
The chief justice who wrote that Black Americans had “no rights which the white man was bound to respect” had once stood before a Maryland jury and called slavery a national disgrace. The two Taneys read the same founding document, the Declaration of Independence, in opposite ways, thirty-eight years apart.
Born in Calvert County, Maryland, in 1777, into a tobacco-planting family that had held enslaved people for generations, Taney studied at Dickinson College and built a law practice in Frederick. Beginning in 1818 he freed eleven people he held, and he provided for the elderly ones, whom Maryland law made difficult to free. He also served as an officer of the Maryland branch of the American Colonization Society. His opposition to slavery, in other words, ran gradual and paternal, joined to the hope that freed people would leave the country, and it never included racial equality.[[32]]
In March 1819 he defended Jacob Gruber, a Methodist preacher indicted for inciting insurrection after an antislavery sermon at a camp meeting. Taney told the jury, “A hard necessity, indeed, compels us to endure the evil of slavery for a time. . . . Yet while it continues, it is a blot on our national character, and every real lover of freedom, confidently hopes that it will be effectually, though it must be gradually, wiped away.” Until the nation could “point without a blush, to the language held in the declaration of independence,” he urged every friend of humanity “to lighten the galling chain of slavery.” The jury acquitted Gruber, and Taney, then a state senator, let the argument go into print.[[33]]
The language did not last. By 1832, as Andrew Jackson’s attorney general, Taney advised that free Black Americans formed “a degraded class” whose rights rested on “the sufferance of the white population.”[[34]] The record does not explain the change by itself. Maryland politics shifted, Taney joined Jackson’s party, and Nat Turner’s rebellion in 1831 hardened white fear across the South. He rose with Jackson, helping write the Bank veto, removing the federal deposits, and suffering the Senate’s first rejection of a cabinet nominee. In 1836 Jackson made him chief justice, a seat he held for twenty-eight years. In the Amistad case of 1841, he joined the majority that freed the kidnapped Africans without writing a word.[[35]]
In 1857 the old judge answered the young lawyer. His Dred Scott opinion read the Declaration’s “all men” as never meant to include Black people. Justice Benjamin Curtis answered with the founders’ own record: when the Constitution took effect, free Black men voted in five states, New Hampshire, Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, and North Carolina, and helped ratify it. Taney’s history left them out.[[36]]
Within two months the young lawyer’s words came back. On May 5, 1857, the Philadelphia Bulletin reprinted extracts from the Gruber argument “for the purpose of letting every one compare his former opinions with his recent decision,” and concluded that “Mr. Taney is, or at least was, a much better man, and a far more profound lawyer than one would suppose from reading his recent opinion in the Dred Scott case.” Later defenders read the same speech the other way, as proof that Taney had opposed slavery all along.[[37]]
His final years complicate the verdict. On March 4, 1861, Taney administered the oath of office to Abraham Lincoln. That May, sitting as a circuit judge in Baltimore, he ruled in Ex parte Merryman that only Congress could suspend the writ of habeas corpus. The administration ignored him, and Congress authorized suspension two years later. Taney died on October 12, 1864, as Maryland voters approved a new constitution that abolished slavery in his home state.[[38]]
Two strands of his Jacksonian faith pulled against each other all his life, rule by the people and the protection of property, and in 1857 he used the Court to settle a question the people’s representatives had argued over for forty years. Both readings of the Declaration came from the same man, and the second one became the law of the land.
### Abraham Lincoln
Abraham Lincoln was born into poverty in Kentucky in 1809 and raised in the woods of Kentucky, Indiana, and Illinois. He had no interest in farming. In 1832, at twenty-three, he volunteered in the Black Hawk War, a campaign to drive the Sauk and Meskwaki leader Black Hawk and his people west of the Mississippi, and his fellow volunteers elected him their captain — “a success,” he wrote years later, “which gave me more pleasure than any I have had since.” Afterward he worked on a riverboat and kept a store before teaching himself law and built a successful practice. He served in the Illinois legislature and for one term in Congress. He married Mary Todd, the daughter of a wealthy, slaveholding Kentucky family. Early in his career Lincoln was a Whig who idolized Henry Clay, and in 1852 he delivered a eulogy for Clay in Springfield.[[39]]
On slavery, Lincoln remained cautious and tactical. Like Clay, he supported gradual emancipation and the colonization of freed people abroad, in Liberia or elsewhere. He hated slavery, but he could not envision a future in which formerly enslaved people lived as equals among whites, believing white prejudice too strong to overcome. As a lawyer he took cases on both sides of the fugitive question. He believed that Black Americans possessed the natural rights named in the Declaration of Independence, yet he did not believe in the equality of the races — and in his own state, as the debates would show, that distinction had a body of law behind it. The Kansas–Nebraska Act changed him. Many historians see its passage as the turning point of Lincoln’s political career: once passive on slavery where it existed, he now set himself firmly against its expansion and spoke of the rights of all men described in the founding documents. The collapse of the Whigs carried him into the new Republican Party, which would one day nominate him for president.[[40]]
### The Lincoln–Douglas Debates
After the Dred Scott decision, Lincoln accused the Court and its allies of conspiring to transform the “peculiar institution” from an exception into the rule of American society; a Court that reached so far, he suggested, was no more legitimate than any other partisan body. In 1858 he got his chance to make the case in public, as the Republican challenger to Stephen Douglas for his seat in the Senate. In seven debates in seven Illinois towns between August and October, two of the country’s most skilled orators delivered provocative, reasoned, and morally serious arguments on the most divisive issue of the day.[[41]]
Douglas defended popular sovereignty, the right of a territory’s citizens to permit or prohibit slavery — a sacred right of self-government, he called it. Lincoln pointed out that Dred Scott had decreed that the citizens of a territory had no such power. At Freeport, Douglas replied that whatever the Court decided mattered less than the actions of the settlers: if a territory refused to pass the local laws slavery needed, no Supreme Court ruling could force it to exist. Many Southern Democrats felt betrayed, and the answer would cost Douglas dearly in 1860. Lincoln returned again and again to his theme that a house divided could not stand. Douglas answered that the founders had “left each State perfectly free to do as it pleased on the subject of slavery,” and that until now the nation and slavery had coexisted successfully. Lincoln insisted that slavery was a moral question, not merely a political one: he had no intention of disturbing it where it existed, but the nation had a duty to stop its spread and place it on the path to ultimate extinction. Black Americans, he argued, were entitled to the rights the Declaration named, “life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.” Douglas then accused him of believing that Black Americans were the equals of whites, a charge meant to burn in a deeply racist state, and Lincoln, at Charleston, denied it: “I am not, nor ever have been, in favor of bringing about in any way the social and political equality of the white and black races.” Douglas insisted that the founders had never meant to include Black people at all. The same founders who closed the Northwest to slavery in 1787 had allowed it to spread in the southern Louisiana Purchase; since then, nine new slave states had entered the Union, and the enslaved population had grown from about 700,000 to more than three million. The legislature returned Douglas to the Senate. But Lincoln’s performance won him national fame, and the debates became a rallying cry for the Republicans and a wedge among the Democrats.[[42]]
Charleston is also where Lincoln’s record on the Illinois Black Laws comes into focus, and it falls into three parts. Few free states treated Black residents more harshly. Illinois required them to register and prove their freedom, barred their testimony in cases involving whites, kept them from the polls, and promised public schooling to white children only; its constitution of 1848 directed the legislature to keep free people of color from settling in the state, and a law of 1853 fined any Black newcomer who stayed and, if he could not pay, sold him at public auction for his labor. Lincoln endorsed the substance of that order. In the same speech, he refused by name to make Black men voters, jurors, or officeholders, and he gave Douglas his “most solemn pledge” that he would “to the very last stand by the law of this State, which forbids the marrying of white people with negroes.” He could condemn such disabilities elsewhere: in 1857 he protested that New Jersey and North Carolina had taken the vote from free Black men and that New York had “greatly abridged” it — but Illinois, which had never granted it, was not on his list. And on the rest of the apparatus, the registration, the testimony bar, the auction block, no surviving document records a word from him, for or against. In 1860 the Black abolitionist H. Ford Douglas said publicly that Lincoln had refused to sign his petition to repeal the testimony law. When Illinois finally repealed the Black Laws in February 1865, after a campaign in which the Chicago tailor John Jones published, at his own expense, The Black Laws of Illinois and a Few Reasons Why They Should Be Repealed, Lincoln appears nowhere in the record. He treated slavery as a national question and the civil status of free Black people as a state one, and the second was the question he never took up at home. Whether that was conviction, calculation, or constitutional principle is where historians divide. Eric Foner put the question himself — which was the real Lincoln, the racist or the opponent of slavery? — and gave “the unavoidable answer”: both.[[43]]
### Harpers Ferry
John Brown had failed at almost everything he tried. He lost several businesses, went bankrupt, and struck many Americans as insane. But he hated slavery and dedicated his life, by any means necessary, to ending it. He approached the question with an Old Testament view, promising apocalyptic judgment on a nation that indulged in the sin. After Pottawatomie, abolitionists called him a hero and proslavery whites called him a devil, and he grew impatient with any strategy but violence. In the summer of 1859, living under an assumed name at a rented farm in Maryland, a few miles from the federal armory at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, he planned an insurrection: seize the armory, arm the enslaved who rallied to him, and lead a liberating army south through the Blue Ridge, freeing every plantation along the way.[[44]]
In August 1859, eight weeks before the raid, Brown met Frederick Douglass in a stone quarry near Chambersburg, Pennsylvania, and asked him to join. Douglass, who admired Brown, refused. Attacking the federal government, he warned, would array the whole country against them; Brown was “going into a perfect steel-trap, and that once in he would never get out alive.” Brown went anyway. On the night of October 16, 1859, he and twenty-one followers — sixteen white men and five Black men — seized the armory. No enslaved people rose to join him. Outnumbered, Brown and his surviving men took refuge in the armory’s fire-engine house. On the morning of October 18, a company of U.S. Marines commanded by Colonel Robert E. Lee, with Lieutenant J. E. B. Stuart at his side, battered down the door and overpowered the raiders, wounding Brown. Ten of his men were killed, two of his sons among them.[[45]]
As a raid, Harpers Ferry failed miserably. As propaganda, it was electrifying. Virginia tried Brown in its own court rather than a federal one — for murder, for inciting slaves to rebel, and for treason against the Commonwealth of Virginia, as though Virginia were already a country of its own. At his trial and in his letters from jail, Brown carried himself with a calm that transformed him, for the antislavery North, into a martyr. On the morning of his execution, December 2, 1859, he handed a guard a note: “I, John Brown, am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood.” Six of his followers went to the gallows after him. Ralph Waldo Emerson declared that Brown would “make the gallows glorious like the cross,” and church bells tolled across the North the day he died. During the Civil War, Union soldiers marched to “John Brown’s Body,” and Julia Ward Howe wrote the “Battle Hymn of the Republic,” published in 1862, to its tune. The white South reacted in horror. As Brown’s Northern financial backers came to light, Southerners became convinced that Harpers Ferry was not the work of one deranged man but of many white Northerners bent on destroying their way of life. Southern newspapers demanded action and declared the election of 1860 the most consequential of their lives.[[46]]
### The Election of 1860
In April 1860 the Democrats met in Charleston, South Carolina. The convention split over slavery in the territories and adjourned without a nominee. Six weeks later, in Baltimore, the party split for good. Northern Democrats nominated Douglas, whose popular sovereignty and Freeport answer had cost him the South. Southern Democrats nominated Vice President John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky on a platform demanding federal protection for slavery in the territories. In May the Republicans met in Chicago. William Seward of New York was the front-runner, with Salmon Chase of Ohio and Edward Bates of Missouri behind him, but Seward’s “higher law” and “irrepressible conflict” had painted him as a radical, and the convention turned to Lincoln, a moderate who could carry the doubtful Northern states. “My name is new in the field,” Lincoln had written that spring, “and I suppose I am not the first choice of a very great many.” He had made sure to offend no one. A fourth party, the Constitutional Union Party, nominated John Bell of Tennessee, a wealthy slaveholder and former Whig, on a platform that took no stand at all on the issues dividing North and South beyond the Constitution and the Union.[[47]]
The election exposed a nation in trauma. Two days after the polls closed, the Charleston Mercury declared, “The tea has been thrown overboard; the revolution of 1860 has been initiated.” Lincoln won 180 electoral votes, every one of them from the free states, though he took just under 40 percent of the popular vote; three of every five Americans voted for someone else. Breckinridge won 72 electoral votes, Bell 39, Douglas only 12. Lincoln was not even on the ballot in ten Southern states. A myth has grown up that the Democrats’ split elected him. It did not. Lincoln won outright majorities in enough Northern states that, had all his opponents’ votes been combined behind a single candidate, he would still have won 169 electoral votes, more than the 152 he needed. He was moderate enough to win the Republican nomination and just radical enough to unite the South against him.[[48]]
The South had long been accustomed to holding the presidency. Slaveholders had occupied the White House for roughly fifty of the republic’s seventy-two years, and Northern “doughfaces” sympathetic to the South had held much of the rest. Lincoln’s election sent shockwaves through the region. Fire-Eaters insisted that Lincoln and the Republicans would end the domestic slave trade, repeal the Fugitive Slave Law, and crush slavery altogether. Most white Southerners viewed the result as a real threat to slavery, but it was the Lower South that would react decisively.[[49]]
### Secession
Between his election and inauguration, Abraham Lincoln delivered over 100 speeches, reaffirming his commitment to leave slavery alone where it existed.[[50]] Despite Lincoln’s reassurances, Southern politicians and slaveholders alike saw his election and the Republican Party as a threat to their hold on the South’s non-slaveholding whites. Rather than live under Yankee rule, leaders of the Deep South chose independence. At stake, they believed, was not a single election, but an entire way of life. On December 20, 1860, a South Carolina convention voted 169 to 0 to leave the Union. Through the “secession winter,” Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed. These were the states of the Cotton Kingdom, where the enslaved made up a far larger share of the population than in the Upper South.[[51]]
For years Sam Houston, a slaveholder himself, had almost single-handedly held secessionist sentiment in Texas at bay. In 1861, still limping from the wound he had taken at San Jacinto, he watched a state convention vote Texas out of the Union. When the convention required every officeholder to swear allegiance to the Confederacy, Houston refused, and on March 16, 1861, he was removed from office.[[52]]
On February 4, 1861, delegates from the seceded states met in Montgomery, Alabama, to create the Confederate States of America, and they chose Jefferson Davis of Mississippi, a veteran of the Mexican War, an advocate of states’ rights, and a cautious latecomer to secession, as their president. The Confederate Constitution, adopted in March, followed the Constitution of 1787 closely, with one revealing difference: it said outright what the framers had refused to name. No law “denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves shall be passed,” it declared, and it guaranteed that in any new territory “the institution of negro slavery” would be “recognized and protected.” Davis’s vice president, Alexander H. Stephens of Georgia, was blunter still. Speaking at Savannah on March 21, 1861, he declared that the new government’s “foundations are laid, its corner-stone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition.” In Washington, President Buchanan watched state after state depart. He declared that states had no right to secede, but also that he had no constitutional power to stop them, and he left the crisis to his successor.[[53]]
### The Last Compromise
News of secession drew mixed reactions in the North. Some abolitionists welcomed the end of a Union bound to slavery, insisting that slavery survived only on the strength of Northern commerce. Merchants and industrialists panicked, stocks fell, and politicians with close ties to business scrambled for a compromise. Lincoln looked for the narrowest common ground. He would respect the Constitution’s protection of slavery where it existed and would not touch it there; the one thing he would not yield was its expansion.[[54]]
In December 1860, the outgoing Congress met to address the crisis. Senator John J. Crittenden of Kentucky, a former Whig and a disciple of Henry Clay, introduced what became known as the Crittenden Compromise: six proposed constitutional amendments and four resolutions meant to appease the South. The amendments would have preserved slavery permanently where it already existed, as well as restored the Missouri Compromise line, extending it to the Pacific and protecting slavery in all present and future territory south of that line. Congress would not interfere with slavery in the District of Columbia or with the Fugitive Slave Act. Lincoln and most Republicans rejected the plan, because it gave up the one principle on which their party had been founded, and because Lincoln feared that “hereafter acquired” invited the South to expand into Latin America and the Caribbean. While seven states of the Lower South formed the Confederacy, eight slave states of the Upper South and the border remained in the Union. There, and even among the non-slaveholding farmers of the Confederacy, many whites did not believe Lincoln’s election justified secession. Lincoln read Bell’s strength in the Upper South as a sign that most white Southerners wanted Union, not war, and he believed that secession might collapse from within. He acknowledged the possibility of violence, but he underestimated the South’s commitment to its new nation.[[55]]
In his inaugural address on March 4, 1861, Lincoln assured the South that he would not touch slavery where it existed, but he condemned secession as illegal and declared that he would use his power “to hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government” — the forts and arsenals in the seceded states. Like Andrew Jackson during the nullification crisis, he placed the Union above the acts of insubordinate states. And he placed the choice of war squarely in Southern hands: “In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war.”[[56]]
### Power
Throughout these years, the mainstream of the antislavery movement remained committed to ending slavery peacefully and gradually. But the secession crisis revealed that the South could not tolerate a federal government working to limit slavery’s expansion, and it chose to gamble on war.[[57]]
Through that framework, the decade is a story of methods failing one after another. Law failed: the Compromise of 1850, the Kansas–Nebraska Act, and the Dred Scott decision were each designed to take slavery out of national politics, and each drove it deeper in, into Northern streets and courtrooms, onto the prairie, onto the floor of the Senate. Morals failed, too, or rather multiplied: Stowe’s novel and Thoreau’s address converted much of the North, but the South answered with sermons of its own, and the churches had split before the nation did. What remained was power. As I tell my students: “We look at the Civil War, there weren’t a whole lot of moral arguments being heard. A few, but they got shattered down by the power.” By the spring of 1861, the fate of slavery, and the future of the whole nation, depended on war. Southern secession had made emancipation through war possible, however much Republicans and abolitionists alike had hoped it would never come to that.
## Questions for Consideration
1. How did interpretations of the Constitution and debates over rights, liberties, and definitions of citizenship affect American values, politics, and society in the 1850s?
2. How did the Compromise of 1850 galvanize the North? The South? Why did the Fugitive Slave Act, in particular, change minds that two decades of abolitionist argument had not?
3. Webster, Thoreau, and Garrison each answered an unjust law with a different method: law, morals, or power. Can something lawful be immoral? Can something moral be unlawful? Choose one of the three men and defend his answer — then identify a later American movement that made the same choice.
4. How did the Kansas–Nebraska Act and the Dred Scott decision affect American politics and the nation’s faith in its institutions? What effect did John Brown’s raid on Harpers Ferry have on sectionalism?
5. Which single event made the Civil War inevitable — the Mexican Cession, the Fugitive Slave Act, Bleeding Kansas, the Dred Scott decision, or Harpers Ferry? Nominate one, and make the case that the war could still have been avoided before it but not after.
## Then and Now
1. Two universities still call their teams the Rebels, and the word never meant the same thing in both. At Nevada Southern, now the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, it began as a jab at Reno — students in the south of the state defying the northern regents who ran its university, borrowing Confederate costume for a quarrel that had nothing to do with slavery. At the University of Mississippi it meant what it said. The Lyceum at the center of campus had served as a Confederate hospital, and nearly nineteen hundred wounded men passed through it before Appomattox. Colonel Reb reached the yearbook cover in 1937, and the Confederate flag and “Dixie” — written in 1859, inside this chapter’s own decade — came with him. Taking the symbols back down cost accordingly. In Las Vegas it took a student newspaper and a vote: Bert Babero Jr., whose November 1970 letter to the Rebel Yell was the first to name the nickname as racist rather than unpatriotic. In Oxford it took James Meredith walking in under federal escort in 1962; Robert J. Williams signing in December 1971 as the first Black man to play football for the Rebels; and John Hawkins, elected the first Black cheerleader in 1982 and expected to lead the team onto the field carrying the Confederate flag. Asked whether he would, Hawkins answered: “The answer is no. I will not.” He explained himself this way: “I wasn’t taught to hate the flag but I did have history classes and I know what my ancestors went through and what the rebel flag represents.”[[58]]
2. Test it. This chapter closes on a decade that could not settle what its own symbols meant and let force settle it instead. Does the comparison hold, or does it flatten two institutions that were never alike? Or set it aside and bring your own: find a symbol being argued over right now, name who is doing the arguing and what each side says it means, and say what changing it would cost.
