UNIT V
The Rules of the Game
What the Founders Built When the Confederation Ran Out of Money and Authority, and What the Constitution Left for Someone Else to Answer
~
Native leverage and colonial self-government had both depended on a rival empire and a distracted crown, and in 1763 both disappeared. The Revolution followed, and this unit examines the attempt to build a government on something more durable than distance, rivalry, or royal neglect: a document.
Chapter 10 is the attempt. The war for independence decided who would govern America, but not how. The Articles of Confederation created a government shaped by men who had just spent eight years fighting against concentrated power. For five years, the arrangement showed its limits: no power to tax, no army of its own, no federal courts, and no answer when farmers in western Massachusetts shut the courthouses. Fifty-five delegates went to Philadelphia to revise the Articles and replaced them instead. What they produced was a negotiation from its first line to its last: large states against small, federal power against states’ autonomy, and, most consequentially, the fight over slavery, an institution central to both economic and political power. The Constitution strengthened national authority beyond anything the Articles had permitted. It never once used the word slave and left the largest question in the room for someone else to answer. Whether that was prudence or complicity has never stopped being argued, and these three chapters do not settle it.
“The New Republic” is where paper meets people. What came out of Philadelphia was a machine of extraordinary ingenuity, but a machine is only a set of instructions. The Constitution said little about the men who would run it, how a cabinet should work, or what to do when the people who had written it together discovered that they wanted the country to become two different things. In a single decade the republic faced a new question: not whether the states could be bound into a nation, which Chapter 10 settled, but whether a nation this large, this free, and this divided could govern itself without tearing apart. The answer arrived as political parties, a form the founders had not foreseen and could not prevent. Then it arrived again, in the winter of 1800–1801, as something very few nations had ever done: power changing hands between enemies without a shot.
“The Jeffersonian Republic” follows the men who received it. They had campaigned against every piece of what they inherited: the bank, the debt, the army, the navy, the centralized state. They meant to take it apart, and for a while they did. Then the world refused to cooperate. The heirs of the Anti-Federalists doubled the size of the nation by using the very levers of federal power they had once vehemently opposed, laid the heaviest federal hand yet placed on American commerce, and blundered into a second war with Britain that nearly broke the republic in half. And every acre the republic gained came at the expense of Native nations. By 1823 the Republicans had chartered Hamilton’s bank anew and were declaring stewardship of an entire hemisphere.
Across these three chapters, the side arguing for federal power changes hands. Leverage can expire and a doctrine can be abandoned. The Constitution stayed where it was; what bent, every time the world pressed on it, was everyone’s account of what it allowed. Unit VI takes up the question Unit IV left open: consent from whom?
