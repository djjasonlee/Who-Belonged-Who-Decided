CHAPTER 5
Slavery in British North America
The Price, the Law, and the Making of Race
“The wretched situation was again aggravated by the galling of the chains, now become insupportable; and the filth of the necessary tubs, into which the children often fell, and were almost suffocated. The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying, rendered the whole a scene of horror almost inconceivable.”
— Olaudah Equiano, The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano (1789), describing events of c. 1756[[1]]
From the beginning, the American economy took shape through the interaction of markets, labor, private enterprise, technology, and government. As the economy changed, it reshaped colonial society and the laws that governed it. While the Navigation Acts channeled colonial goods through British ports, rising demand for raw materials required ever more labor to produce them. In the Chesapeake, shrinking supplies of available land and a growing population of poor, landless colonists produced a massive uprising in 1676; the rise of African slavery and the political instability that followed pushed the English colonies to rebuild social and political control on a new foundation, race. Chattel slavery became the primary source of labor, and the primary instrument of social and political control, in the Southern colonies and the Caribbean. And the people it enslaved maintained cultural autonomy and demonstrated, from the African coast to the Carolina rice fields, every form of resistance available to them. This chapter follows that sequence — trade, labor, uprising, law, resistance — showing how economic interests became political power and eventually hardened into law. Race became the line between freedom and slavery one statute at a time, as colonial lawmakers decided on which side of it each child would be born.
### Race Before Racism
From an anthropological and biological perspective there is only one race, the human race. Race has nonetheless become a social construct: a set of ideas and beliefs based upon a common set of physical features or shared geography.
Hierarchy itself was old; what was new was the ground it stood on. The ancient Mediterranean held slaves in enormous numbers and did not sort them by color. A slave was a captive of war, a debtor, or the subject of a conquest, and Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans ranked outsiders by culture rather than by descent — the Greek barbaros described how a man spoke, not how he looked. Medieval Europe[[2]] drew its sharpest line at religion, between Christian and Jew and Muslim, and religion was a line a person could in principle cross. In fifteenth-century Spain it stopped being crossable. In 1449 the city of Toledo rose in revolt under its chief magistrate, Pero Sarmiento, and issued the first statute of limpieza de sangre — purity of blood — barring Christians of Jewish descent, together with their children and grandchildren, from public office and from testimony in court. The doctrine behind such statutes held that blood carried moral qualities from parent to child, so that baptism changed nothing essential; the cathedral chapter of Toledo adopted its own version in 1547, and colleges, guilds, religious houses, and military orders followed. Adopting a statute and enforcing it proved to be different acts. Toledo’s city council protested that the measure would reopen the civil wars of the previous generation. The royal Council of Castile ruled it “unjust and scandalous,” and the University of Alcalá condemned it as “discord sown by the devil.” Prince Philip suspended the statute in September 1547. Nine years passed before it was allowed to proceed, and the confirmation, when it came, was a formality; the statute was widely ignored. Ultimately the doctrine outlasted the law that carried it. The English knew this his[[3]]tory. What they did not yet have was a law that assigned standing by color.
Historians now refer to the ideas of racism that developed in the late nineteenth century as biological determinism, eugenics, or social Darwinism — but those ideas had a prehistory, and the seventeenth-century Chesapeake is where it begins. Four facts should be fixed before the story is told. Slavery in the New World was by no means a unique practice, nor was it exclusively reserved for Africans: the English sold Native captives into the Caribbean, as the last chapter showed, and even Europeans who fell into the hands of native peoples became enslaved. Of the estimated 12.5 million Africans taken from their homelands, only about 388,000 ever reached North America; the vast majority arrived in the Spanish, Dutch, and Portuguese colonies of Brazil, Mexico, and the Caribbean. The[[4]] overwhelming majority of those captives were sold by African merchants in exchange for European goods, not captured by European slavers. And slavery in North America did not begin as a racial status: Africans and Native Americans owned their own indentured servants and slaves as well.
### Africa, 1600
Transatlantic trade originally involved Europe and the Americas; as the supply of Indian and white labor dwindled because of disease, the Portuguese, remembering the existing slave trades they had witnessed in Africa, partnered with African kingdoms in the exchange of manufactured goods for human labor. Two significant slave systems, Mediterranean and Sub-Saharan African, had existed for hundreds of years, north and south of the Sahara. The conquest of Constantinople established a new slave trade in the Ottoman Empire, mostly in North Africa, operated primarily by Muslim pirates in the Mediterranean. At the time Europe began colonizing the New World, several West African states — Benin and the Akan kingdoms of the Gold Coast, Kongo south of the Congo River, and in the interior the fragments of Songhai, which had fallen to a Moroccan army in 1591 — competed for regional dominance, conducting raiding wars on one another, with the captured sold in the various slave markets. Slavery as practiced in the Old World differed greatly from the chattel slavery that developed[[5]] in British North America. In Africa, Europe, and the Middle East it was largely a temporary condition, in which criminals, debtors, and prisoners of war served until they had satisfied the demands of their masters or governments; the spouses and children of slaves retained their freedom, often living with the slave owners. West African kingdoms were enticed to provide the Portuguese and Dutch with captives in exchange for manufactured goods and weapons, which they used to increase warfare upon one another in order to acquire more slave labor. This pattern — the destabilization of entire regions for trade goods — will recur throughout North America, as European nations tapped into the existing slave trades among Native American peoples and, as the previous chapters showed, turned the fur trade into an engine of war. By 1620, Spanish, Portuguese, and Dutch trading companies had come to depend upon African slave markets, and the transatlantic trade became triangular, with Africa a crucial partner.
### The South Atlantic System
The South Atlantic System brought wealth to the entire British and European economy and helped Europeans achieve economic dominance. The Navigation Acts kept the sugar trade in the hands of British merchants, who exported the lucrative product to foreign markets; by 1750, shipments of American sugar and tobacco into other European nations accounted for half of all British exports. Enormous profits also flowed into Britain from the slave trade itself. At its height in the 1790s, Britain annually exported three hundred thousand guns to Africa, while British ships carrying 300 to 350 slaves left an African port every other day. This commerce stimulated the entire British economy. English, Scottish, and American shipyards built hundreds of vessels, and thousands of people worked in trade-related industries: building port facilities and warehouses, refining sugar and tobacco, distilling rum from molasses, and manufacturing textiles and iron products for the growing markets in Africa and America. More than one thousand British merchant ships were plying the Atlantic by 1750, providing a supply of experienced sailors and laying the foundation for the supremacy of the Royal Navy. As the South Atlantic System enhanced European prosperity, it imposed enormous costs upon the people of West and Central Africa. Between 1550 and 1870, the Atlantic slave trade uprooted more than 12 million Africans, draining the continent of people and wealth, redirecting commerce away from the savannas and the Islamic world beyond the Sahara, and fostering militaristic, predatory states along the coast.[[6]]
### The Gold Coast
Following a successful raid, slave caravans marched hundreds of miles to the Atlantic coast with their prisoners bound together at the neck. African captives typically suffered death rates of 20 percent or more while being marched overland; observers reported seeing hundreds of skeletons along the caravan routes. All along the Gold Coast stood European forts where the captives were held in pens, known as barracoons, guarded by dogs. Captives could spend anywhere from days to weeks in small cages until European slavers inspected them, checking for symptoms of disease, for injuries, and for teeth, the primary indicator of health. Following inspection, the captives were labeled with names or numbers and loaded onto slave ships. To prevent organized rebellion, they were separated from family members and their nations and placed with unfamiliar people who spoke different languages. Once aboard, they faced the transatlantic voyage.
### The Middle Passage
Most of the records that document the experience of captives and crew come from the diaries and logs of the slave ships themselves. The most famous description of the Middle Passage is Olaudah Equiano’s: “I was soon put down under the decks, and here I received such a salutation in my nostrils as I had never experienced in my life: so that, with the loathsomeness of the stench, and crying together, I became so sick and low that I was not able to eat, nor had I the least desire to taste anything.” Slave ships were so notorious for the stench of bodily fluids and human waste that sailors often detected nearby vessels not by sight but by odor, which the Atlantic winds carried for miles. Captives spent four to five weeks aboard these floating prisons, given a handful of beans, corn, yams, rice, or palm oil — and not always fed every day. If there was not enough food for the crew and the captives, the enslavers ate first; those deemed too sick were not fed at all. From time to time the captives were brought on deck for sunlight and forced to dance, in the hope that exercise would mean fewer deaths.
On the main deck, some captives jumped overboard to drown rather than endure more. Those deemed too sick or close to death were chained together and sent overboard with an anchor. Others staged violent shipboard revolts: uprisings occurred on roughly two thousand voyages, about one of every ten Atlantic passages. Nearly 100,000 captives died in those insurrections, and nearly 1.5 million others — about 14 percent of all those transported — died of disease or illness on the month-long journey.[[7]]
### Arrival and Seasoning
For the captives who survived the overland march, the cages, and the Middle Passage, the sight of land offered a small relief — and then more trauma. Once the ships arrived, their human cargo was unloaded and prepared for auction; slavers washed, shaved, and rubbed the captives with palm oil to disguise the sores and wounds of the voyage. Captains usually sold directly to planters or to specialized wholesalers by auction. Families who had managed to stay together were now often broken up, and the bonds formed during the voyage were broken with them. Owners and overseers then set out to obliterate the identities of the people they had bought — to break their wills and sever every bond with their past — forcing Africans to adapt to new working and living conditions, a new language, new religions, and new customs. They called this process “seasoning,” and it could last two or three years. For people already weakened by the voyage, its brutality was overwhelming. Those who survived the Middle Passage soon faced another kind of danger, not to the body but to the mind: the death of who they had been. The plantation intended to strip the captives of everything they knew about themselves; the captives fought to hold on to their identities, their memories, and the ties that bound them to one another. Seasoning, the initial phase of servant breaking, marks the beginning of that struggle. How far it succeeded, and where it failed, is the story of Chapter 16.
The majority of enslaved Africans were sent to labor on sugar plantations, where heat, humidity, exhaustion, and malnutrition proved a lethal combination: ten hours a day under the tropical sun, flimsy huts, a starchy diet of corn, yams, and dried fish. As the enslaved came to outnumber their European masters and employees, they were subjected to harsh physical punishment intended to intimidate them into submission. With sugar prices high and the cost of slaves low, many planters simply worked their slaves to death and bought more. Between 1708 and 1735, British planters on Barbados imported about 85,000 Africans; in that same period the island’s African population increased by only 4,000, from 42,000 to 46,000. While the vast majority of the enslaved went to South America, Mexico, and[[8]] the Caribbean, roughly 388,000 arrived in North America between 1619 and the trade’s end (see note 4). Their arrival would transform the Southern colonies’ social and economic systems entirely, while the Middle and New England colonies involved themselves in the trade in other ways — shipping and financing.
### Jamestown, 1619
On July 30, 1619, Virginia Governor Sir George Yeardley and twenty-two regional representatives convened to establish the first representative government in the New World. Known as the House of Burgesses, the body was organized to advise the governor and the proprietors of the Virginia Company in England; because the Company was headquartered three thousand miles from Jamestown, it relied upon the Burgesses to provide law and order. The commonwealth that emerged featured a system of government and individual liberties, led by a new political and economic elite, designed to reward merit and to extend the protection of law to those in the lowest economic classes. In essence, the provincial government proclaimed that each inhabitant, regardless of religion, ethnicity, or class, possessed rights and remedies at law.[[9]]
### The First Africans
On August 20, 1619, the White Lion, an English privateer, arrived at Point Comfort, at the mouth of the James, carrying “20 and odd” Angolans. The only documentation of the event was produced by the tobacco baron John Rolfe. The Angolans had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship bound for New[[10]] Spain, but without any clear laws governing African servants in Jamestown, they were treated as indentured servants; the records show they were purchased in exchange for food and supplies. Under the House of Burgesses’ recent proclamation, all inhabitants — including slaves, captives, indentured servants, and “Negroes” — possessed legal rights and protections, and it was not long before many of these individuals recognized those rights and began economic endeavors of their own.
The 1619 Project opens its account of the nation with this ship, which “carried more than 20 enslaved Africans, who were sold to the colonists,” and treats their landing as the true beginning of America. The Angolans did come off a Portuguese slaver, and Rolfe records that the governor “bought” them for victuals. However, Virginia had no law of slavery to hold them under, and the record does not show whether they lived out their days in servitude. The status the 1619 Project assigns them in August 1619 took the Virginia Assembly another forty-three years to write into law.[[11]]
### Anthony Johnson and John Punch
An Angolan man listed in the records as “Antonio the Negro,” who arrived in 1621 aboard the James, later became known as Anthony Johnson. He obtained his freedom by a route the record does not preserve — he was still a servant on the Bennett plantation in 1625 and first appears as a free man in the 1640s — and began farming tobacco on his own land; in July 1651 Johnson laid lawful claim to 250 acres on the Eastern Shore under the headright system and owned several indentured servants, both English and African. He married an African woman and successfully sued in a Virginia court against a white man to regain a runaway servant. But in 1640, as African servants became the preferred labor[[12]] source, a Virginia court handed down a sentence no statute yet required. That year two of Hugh Gwyn’s other servants — Victor, a Dutchman, and James Gregory, a Scot — ran away to Maryland with an African named John Punch. Upon their return the General Court ordered thirty stripes for each of the three, then extended the two Europeans’ terms of service and sentenced Punch to serve for life.[[13]] For the next three decades the Virginia colony would pass a series of laws establishing race as both a social status and the condition for chattel slavery.
Thirteen days later the same court heard a harder case. On July 22, 1640, six servants of Captain William Pierce and Emanuel, “a negro of Mr Reginolds,” took Pierce’s skiff with corn, powder, shot, and guns and sailed for the Dutch plantation, until their capture on the Elizabeth River. The court reserved its harshest sentence for a European: Christopher Miller, “a dutchman,” received thirty stripes, an R burned into his cheek, a year or more in shackles, and seven more years of service to the colony. Emanuel received the same stripes, brand, and shackle, and no added service at all. Perhaps neither African carried a term to extend: the July 9 order names indentures for Victor and Gregory and none for Punch, and the July 22 order calls Emanuel simply “a negro of Mr Reginolds.” Either way, the General Court applied no single racial rule in July 1640. The standard account begins American slavery with John Punch. The record supports a slower story: one court sentenced individual men, and the law took sixty-five more years to build the category, with condition in 1662, category in 1691, and definition in 1705.[[14]]
### Barbados, 1640
English planters on the Caribbean island of Barbados had struggled to produce tobacco, which failed to thrive in the tropical climate; the arrival of Dutch refugees from Brazil showed them how to cultivate the far more lucrative sugarcane. The labor-intensive crop, combined with the malaria and yellow fever that arrived with the African trade, killed some six thousand people on the island between 1647 and 1650 — the yellow-fever epidemic of those years was the first great outbreak of the disease in the Americas, and servants died first — and planters turned to slaves purchased from Dutch traders at[[15]] competitive prices. By 1660 enslaved Africans outnumbered English planters, who began to fear uprisings. Unlike the French and Spanish, English planters discouraged the Christianizing of their slaves, which made it easier to enslave and abuse them as non-Christians. Without an existing body of law to govern the people they owned, the planter class of Barbados created the first comprehensive slave code in 1661. The act that replaced it, passed in 1688, “An Act for the Governing of Negroes,” established that enslaved Africans were to be treated as chattel — property — and imposed severe restrictions on movement, freedoms, and, in the name of fear and control, physical punishment up to death. Barba[[16]]dos became the blueprint for managing large plantations.
The act’s own words show the system at work. A slave who struck a Christian “by striking or the like” faced punishment that escalated: “for his or her first offence … [to] be severely whipped by the Constable,” and for a second, to “be severely whipped, his nose slit, and be burned in some part of his face with a hot iron.” Charged with a serious crime, an enslaved person lost the oldest protection in English law, because “being brutish slaves, [they] deserve not, for the baseness of their condition, to be tried by the legal trial of twelve men of their peers or neighbourhood.” And a master answered to no one for the violence of his own discipline: “if any Negro or other slave under punishment by his master … unfortunately shall suffer in life or member, which seldom happens; no person whatsoever shall be liable to any fine therefore.”[[17]]
### The Development of Slavery
From New England to the Caribbean, the demand for raw materials created a demand for labor, and as the supply of indentured servants from England decreased, the colonies were forced to seek an alternative. While the Portuguese and Dutch engaged in the slave trade, England had opted for indentured servants. England held a negative view of slavery — many English sailors had been captured by Muslim pirates and sold into North African and Sub-Saharan slavery — and because England suffered from a lack of economic opportunity and available work, slavery was deeply unpopular. Following the arrival of the twenty captured Africans, the African population of Jamestown had increased only to about 300 in nearly thirty years.
### Economics
Slavery, English economists argued, was less profitable than employing workers with prospects and incentives. Unlike indentured servants or wage laborers, who were motivated to work effectively, slaves had no reason to labor diligently. Native American slaves, familiar with the land, often escaped or quickly succumbed to disease; African slaves often performed poorly, finding ways to resist — feigning illness, breaking tools, poisoning the beasts of burden. Toward the end of the seventeenth century, however, the English Civil War reduced the number of indentured servants, raising their value and cost to English planters, and more Africans were imported into the North American colonies. But why not import more labor from Scotland and Ireland, where unemployment and poverty were extreme? European indentured servants spoke the language, shared cultural traits, and were more familiar with English farming techniques, whereas Africans lacked the knowledge and the means of assimilation — not to mention any motivation to work efficiently with no prospect of freedom or land. And as English economists predicted, the vast amount of American land meant laborers would want to leave their servitude for opportunities of their own. The answer was in the numbers: Irish and Scottish labor was cheap, shared a language and farming techniques, and produced more efficiently precisely because it was motivated by the prospect of eventual land — and it therefore left. Those destined to toil until death would have no such motivation, and no such exit.
### Disease
Most of the servants who reached the Chesapeake ranked among the lowest classes in England. Volunteers, vagabonds, and criminals alike, many already in poor health, endured a grueling journey across the Atlantic, and those fortunate enough to survive it found themselves in a land both unfamiliar and unforgiving. Their first year brought fever in the Chesapeake’s heat and humidity, which newcomers either survived or died of, and planters could expect little work from them until it passed. Climate, disease, and Native American hostilities combined to produce extremely high mortality rates. As planters had discovered in Barbados, European indentured servants died frequently of malaria and yellow fever, while Indian slaves escaped into lands they knew. The lethal environment of the Virginia coast showed which laborers were likelier to survive it. Long, hot summers and swamp-filled lowlands were ideal for mosquitoes, and the malaria and yellow fever that flourished in them killed Europeans while West Africans, carrying greater resistance to both — acquired through prior exposure and partly genetic — survived, worked, and reproduced at higher rates. In 1640 perhaps 150 Africans resided in Virginia and in 1650 about 300; in 1660 there were still fewer than a thousand in Virginia and Maryland together, and only in the 1680s, when their number nearly tripled, from roughly 4,500 to 12,000, did the trade turn decisively toward Africa. The cruel irony was what that resistance became: a better re[[18]]turn on investment. Planters read that resistance as a natural fitness for the work. It was not: enslaved Africans died in enormous numbers. What differed was not their capacity to suffer but the traders’ capacity to replace them — and the purchase of an English laborer, who might die in a season or walk away in seven years, had become the greater financial risk. By 1660, Spain and Portugal had committed entirely to African slave labor and had established durable relationships with several African kingdoms. England needed only to insert itself into the triangle trade.
Prejudice was present from the first ship; it was not sufficient. The same planters who held it ran their colonies on English servants for two generations. What changed was arithmetic — the rising price and falling supply of English labor, the Chesapeake death rate, and the greater resistance of West African workers to the malaria and yellow fever that killed Europeans. But economics and disease resistance, not prejudice, were decisive — and once planters had chosen, the law was rewritten to make the choice permanent.
### Social and Political Control
Until 1662, no statute made servitude and social status biologically determined in the Virginia Colony. Religion and property were the primary conditions upon which colonial society was segmented; indentured servants and free laborers alike lived unencumbered by skin color, socialized freely, and worked toward their own goals. The combination of environmental conditions, economic fluctuation, and political instability, however, created an opportune moment for the planter elite to retain their wealth and power through the institution of chattel slavery.
By the 1660s, new generations of English immigrants had acclimated to the harsh climate, producing for the first time positive birth rates and declining mortality; Africans, too, had shown greater resistance to the fevers of the Virginia lowlands, evident in the rapid growth of the colony’s African population. But conflict with Native Americans continued to plague the colony and blocked the acquisition of new land, and with nearly all of Virginia’s lands occupied, proprietors and wealthy planters violated their contractual obligation to supply their indentured servants with land of their own. The price of tobacco, meanwhile, had fallen substantially as supply outpaced demand, and with the passage of the Navigation Acts Virginia planters could no longer sell to the Dutch, Spanish, and French. The downturn, an inconvenience for the elite, was devastating for small farmers, who were forced into bankruptcy and saw their lands absorbed by larger plantations. By the 1670s, thousands of landless former servants, English and African, were voicing their frustrations to their local governments and to the king.
### Bacon’s Rebellion
In the same year that the New England colonies and their Native allies defeated King Philip, social and economic tensions in Jamestown reached a boiling point. The Atlantic market that had carried Native captives from New England to the slave markets of the Caribbean now brought enslaved Africans to Virginia in growing numbers, supplying a different labor force whose bondage would become the foundation of the colony’s plantation economy. By 1675 the planter class and political elite — five percent of the population — had established a highly stratified society, and the colony had reached its territorial limits, as the surrounding Native nations held all neighboring lands. Thousands of indentured servants, promised land upon completing their contracts, found themselves landless and without prospects, and competition among the lower classes, African and white, for what little land remained grew fierce. When petitioned for additional lands and permission to remove Native Americans, the planter elite chose to maintain favorable relations with the indigenous nations for their resources: furs and peace. In 1676 the tension erupted. Thousands of poor and disenfranchised servants, African and white, along with other free laborers, organized to defy the governor’s orders, and Nathaniel Bacon, a relative of Governor William Berkeley, rallied the landless and poor in war campaigns along the frontier, attacking the neighboring Susquehannock in order to seize their land. Berkeley ordered Bacon’s arrest. But the angry mob of landless servants proved too powerful, turned against Berkeley, and drove the governor to flee for his life.
Without English leadership, Jamestown was burned to the ground and fell into anarchy; Bacon died of dysentery. England soon reclaimed control of the rebellious colony — and recognized the power of the landless lower class, which consisted of both whites and Africans. In this pivotal moment the Virginia assembly and the planter elite seized the opportunity to weaken the disenfranchised masses by dividing them into separate and unequal statuses based upon racial identity. Virginia law awarded preferential legal status, economic opportunity, and positions within the colonial militia to its white citizens, while enacting a series of laws that over time made slavery a racialized and permanent condition. Skin color, not class, became the new form of status in the colony: the planter elite wrote race into the law of Virginia. By 1700 the combination of economic, biological, and social forces had formed the justification for Africans, and their posterity, becoming a permanent source of labor and a socially inferior people.
What Virginia built between 1662 and 1705 had never existed on this continent: a statute making bondage hereditary through the mother (1662); a law that baptism could not free a slave (1667); indemnity for a master who killed one “by the extremity of the correction” (1669); banishment for marrying across the line (1691); and, in 1705, a code that made enslavement the permanent condition of every non-Christian brought into the colony. Prejudice is a belief. Racism is what the planter elite did[[19]] with it once their plantations required a labor force that could never leave — and every one of those laws is dated.[[20]] Slavery based upon race provided, in the same stroke, the creation of “whiteness”: a sense of racial, social, and cultural solidarity and “superiority” that bound the poor white servant to the planter who had cheated him of his land.
### Some Doubts Have Arisen
Elizabeth Key’s mother was African, and her name does not survive in the record. Her father, Thomas Key, an English planter, had been fined for fathering her. In 1636, preparing to sail for England, he placed her with Humphrey Higginson for nine years. She had been christened, with Higginson as her godfather. Nine years became nearly twenty. She passed into the household of John Mottrom, and when Mottrom died in 1655 the inventory of his estate listed six servants and five “Negroes” separately — two categories pulling apart on a probate sheet before any statute told them to. Key sued, on[[21]] three grounds any Englishwoman could claim: her father had been free, she was a Christian, and her term had ended. In January 1656 a Northumberland County jury declared her free. In March the General Court — the same body that had sentenced John Punch — ruled against her. A committee of burgesses then found for her: “by the Common Law the Child of a Woman slave begott by a free-man ought to bee free,” and she could “give a very good account of her fayth.” On July 21, 1656, the county court freed her, with the corn and clothes owed any servant at the end of a term. The same day, she and William Grinsted, who had carried her case, posted their banns.[[22]]
Six years later the General Assembly took up the question her case had raised. Its 1662 act opened, “Whereas some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree,” and settled them by reversing English common law: “all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother.” The Dictionary of Virginia Biography judges her suit “probably responsible directly or in part.” Five years later the As[[23]]sembly closed her second door: “the conferring of baptisme doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or freedome.” Paternity, faith, contract — every ground Elizabeth Key had won on had fallen within a decade.
The colony’s own vocabulary tracks the same construction. In 1640 the General Court sentenced “a dutchman,” “a Scotchman,” and “a negro”; every European carried a nation, and only the African carried a color. The 1662 act named the moth[[24]]er’s condition and never used the word “mulatto.” In 1691 both words arrived in a single sentence, in the act that drew the line and punished the crossing: “whatsoever English or other white man or woman being free shall intermarry with a negroe, mulatto, or Indian man or woman bond or free shall within three months after such marriage be banished and removed from this dominion forever.”[[25]] In August 1705, after the minister of Blissland Parish refused to marry John Bunch and Sarah Slaydon because he called Bunch a mulatto, the Council asked Attorney General Stevens Thompson what the word meant. Thompson declared himself “wholly unacquainted with the Appellations given to ye issue of such mixtures,” unable to “resolve whether the issue begotten on a White woman by a Mulatto man can properly be called a Mulatto,” and reported that he had heard of another name, “Mustee.” Fourteen years[[26]] after writing the category into law, the colony’s chief legal officer could not say who belonged in it. That October the Assembly answered him, “for clearing all manner of doubts”: “the child of an Indian and the child, grand child, or great grand child, of a negro shall be deemed, accounted, held and taken to be a mulatto.” The definition did not stand alone. It sat inside[[27]] an act declaring that no “negro, mulatto or Indian” shall “bear any office, ecclesiasticall, civill or military, or be in any place of public trust or power.” Virginia defined the category in the same statute that barred it from power.
Virginia’s law of race opened in 1662 with doubts and closed in 1705 with an act to clear them. In between, the colony enforced a category it had not yet defined — and when it finally defined one, it wrote the definition into a list of people who would never decide anything.
### Resistance
Adam Smith, observing that a slave “can have no other interest but to eat as much, and to labour as little as possible,” judged slave labor the dearest of all[[28]] — and a bondage that could never end invited resistance as surely as it invited neglect. Slavery in the Old World had been an institution in which most captives served temporarily, to pay a debt or serve a punishment; slaves retained civil rights, including the right to own slaves; slavery was not hereditary, and the family of a slave remained free. Chattel slavery in the Americas revolutionized the institution as a permanent status, and it produced organized resistance from those captured, sold, transported, and enslaved. As African peoples learned more of the transatlantic trade, villages built fortifications and warning systems against traders and enemy raiders. Captives forced onto the ships resisted by hunger strike, by rebellion, and by suicide over the side rather than a life in slavery. The most famous shipboard revolt — aboard the Spanish schooner Amistad in 1839 — occurred not on the Middle Passage but on a coastal voyage in Cuba; the captives killed the captain and the cook, the ship was seized off Long Island, and in 1841 the Supreme Court ruled them free.[[29]]
In the sugar islands from the 1650s, and in South Carolina by about 1708 — where by 1715 the enslaved were roughly sixty percent of the colony — enslaved Africans came to outnumber the English and other European populations, a condition that never held for British North America as a whole,[[30]] and the planter elite’s greatest fear was to be butchered in their own beds by the people they owned. The fear expressed itself in brutal slave codes that legalized severe physical abuse, even rape. In response, the enslaved developed multiple ways to resist and to cope. As the Scottish economist Adam Smith predicted, slave labor proved less efficient than that of indentured servants, who retained the promise of freedom and land and so produced more for their masters. Field slaves sabotaged equipment, wounded or killed oxen and mules, feigned illness, ran away, and even inflicted crippling wounds upon themselves to gain a day or two of rest; house slaves broke equipment, worked slowly, and spat in the master’s morning coffee. Despite their numbers and the distances between plantations, the enslaved rarely rose in open rebellion. In 1739, slaves at Stono, South Carolina, revolted, killing many whites in the hope of reaching freedom in Spanish Florida; the insurrection was put down, the rebels killed, and more restrictive slave laws enacted. The destination had a name. In March 1738 the gov[[31]]ernor of Spanish Florida, Manuel de Montiano, chartered Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose about two miles north of St. Augustine — the first legally sanctioned free Black town in what is now the United States — and garrisoned it with a free Black militia under Francisco Menéndez, himself an escaped slave who had risen to captain. Behind the charter stood a royal edict of 1693, still in force, offering freedom to runaways from Carolina who accepted the Catholic faith. Twenty-six years after Virginia ruled that baptism could not alter a slave’s condition, Spain had made baptism a road out of it. The men who marched from Stono were walking toward a town not two years old. Two years later in[[32]] New York, between March and April, ten fires blazed in the city, four of them on a single day in early April. A grand jury concluded the fires were the work of enslaved African arsonists tied to a larger conspiracy to burn the city and murder its white inhabitants. Fearing a Stono of their own, the authorities brought more than a hundred slaves into the basement of the city hall on charges of burglary, arson, and insurrection. In interrogations that operated much like the Salem witch trials, the accused were beaten, harassed, and heckled — tactics that probably contributed to the confessions of eighty-one, of whom thirteen were burned at the stake, others hanged, and the rest sent to labor in the lethal sugar plantations of the West Indies.[[33]]
### A Society with Slaves and a Slave Society
The unique environmental, economic, and political circumstances of the Southern colonies created an endless need for African slave labor. Plantation work proved too deadly for European and Native American workers; the profitability of tobacco and rice demanded more labor, whereas the Middle and New England colonies had plenty of workers and no available land. Southern agriculture discouraged European immigrants, who refused to labor as indentured servants and live as a poor minority among a wealthy, autocratic elite. And it became more profitable to purchase slaves, whom planters could treat however they chose, who were more accustomed to the climate and the work, and whom they never had to release after seven years. Thus slavery became the economic and social foundation of the South.[[34]]
The Middle and New England colonies were governed by companies and proprietors who granted their citizens more freedoms, and the Quakers openly opposed and challenged the institution. Slavery was legal in New England and the Middle colonies, but the absence of cash-crop agriculture and available land rendered most slaves domestic servants, and a surplus of labor made slavery largely undesirable there. Where these regions profited from slavery was in carrying it: New England’s own merchants and shipyards, not the Royal African Company, ran the North American branch of the trade, and Rhode Island ran most of that — Newport was the continent’s leading slaving port by 1730, and Rhode Island vessels made 514 voyages to the African coast against 189 from all the other colonies together.[[35]] But as new religious and philosophical ideas arrived from England, the institution began to wane in these regions, and the first clamors for its abolition were heard — a story that belongs to the chapters on the Awakening and the Revolution that follow.
One of those clamors had a name. In September 1738 — one year before the enslaved rose in South Carolina — the Philadelphia Yearly Meeting at Burlington, New Jersey, the grandest Quaker gathering in the colonies, was interrupted by a man named Benjamin Lay. Lay stood barely over four feet tall, and he had come prepared. Declaring that God Almighty respected all peoples equally — rich and poor, men and women, white and black alike — and that slave-keeping was the greatest sin in the world, he drove a sword through a hollowed book concealing a bladder of blood-red pokeberry juice, spattering the slaveholding Friends nearest him and thundering that God would shed the blood of those who enslaved their fellow creatures. The Friends removed him from the meetinghouse. His book against slavery had found exactly one Philadelphia printer willing to touch it, the year before: Benjamin Franklin. Fifty-two years later, as we will see in Chapter 10, that printer put his own name to a petition against slavery before the first Congress of the United States.[[36]]

## Then and Now
1. Race went into Virginia’s law one statute at a time, and it came back out the same way. The 1691 act banished any “English or other white” person who married across the line; its descendant was still on the books in 1924 as the Racial Integrity Act, and it took until June 12, 1967, when the Supreme Court decided Loving v. Virginia, for that law and the ones like it in fifteen other states to fall. Two hundred and seventy-six years from the statute to the ruling.[[37]]
2. Test it. The chapter argues that prejudice was present from the first ship and was not sufficient — that racism is what the planter elite did with it once their plantations required a labor force that could never leave. Does the long life of the marriage ban support that reading, or complicate it? Or bring your own: find a law that took longer to repeal than it took to pass, and say who benefited from the delay.
