UNIT IV
Empire, Resistance, Revolution
The End of Salutary Neglect and the War That Made a Country
~
How did Britain’s greatest victory become, within twenty years, the loss of thirteen colonies? In 1763, France surrendered Canada and its claims east of the Mississippi, and the arrangements that had held North America together came apart. For two centuries, Native nations had protected their lands by playing one empire against another. For a century, the colonists had governed themselves while London looked the other way, a policy later called salutary neglect. Neither arrangement had been secured outright. Both had been sustained by a balance of power that France’s presence helped preserve. With France gone, Britain moved to assert control over the West and collect revenue from the East. Each move met resistance. Three chapters follow that collision: the victory, the quarrel it provoked, and the war the quarrel became.
Chapter 7, “Empire and War,” covers the French and Indian War, fought between Britain and France for control of North America. The fighting began in 1754, and news of George Washington’s defeat at Fort Necessity reached London that September, sharpening the debate over how Britain should respond. Britain won the war, but its victory came at a high price, both financially and politically. Without France, Native nations lost an important counterweight to British power, the Ohio country became land to occupy rather than a prize to bargain over, and a crushing war debt gave the Crown every reason to stop leaving its colonies alone. To raise revenue and administer its enlarged empire, Britain tightened enforcement of the Navigation Acts and imposed new taxes. By 1763, however, the colonies had developed distinct political and economic interests and grown accustomed to self-government. The British victory proved disastrous for Native nations. General Jeffery Amherst halted the French practice of distributing diplomatic gifts, insisting that Native people learn to live “without our charity.” Pontiac’s War followed in 1763. The Proclamation of 1763 restricted colonial settlement west of the Appalachians, seeking to contain frontier conflict and bring western expansion under imperial control. Land speculators and settlers resented the restriction. In 1768, the Treaty of Fort Stanwix established a new boundary through agreements with the Six Nations, opening vast lands south of the Ohio River to colonial claims. The colonists felt the change as well, in the stamps and duties of a Parliament done with neglect.
“The Rejection of Empire” traces thirteen years in which imperial policy turned colonial discontent into a quarrel over representation. Britain set out to convert a self-governing partnership into a revenue colony. The colonists, who had spent a century building their own governments under salutary neglect, read each new tax not as an expense, but as an insult to their rights as Englishmen. Sugar, stamps, tea: the same dispute underlay each. The taxes themselves threatened few ordinary colonists; the Tea Act actually lowered the price of tea. The men the taxes squeezed hardest were merchants and smugglers who had prospered for generations by dodging the trade laws and now faced the Crown’s aggressive enforcement of the Navigation Acts. The Tea Act struck directly at their business. It handed the East India Company a monopoly and put cheaper, legal tea on their turf. In essence, the Boston Tea Party was America’s first drug war. The colonists were the customers; the merchants and smugglers were the cartel, now facing new competition. Those men convinced the rest, and rightly to a point, that the cost was never the issue. Parliament was taxing colonists who had no voice in it, as if they were something less than equal subjects of the Crown.
The argument also carried a passenger nobody had invited. When Dickinson called taxation without consent a form of slavery, the word opened a debate its users had not intended, about the actual enslaved and their entitlement to natural rights; Southern political leaders, whose economies ran on slave labor, insisted the doctrine must not reach them, and the argument was tabled. It would not stay tabled.
“Revolution” is what the quarrel became. Three of its four parts are the war. They follow the Continental Army from the near-collapse of 1776 to Washington’s surprise victory at Trenton, and they explain why the odds favored the Americans more than it seemed: Britain had to win the war outright, while the Americans only had to avoid losing it. Then came the turning at Saratoga, the alliance with France, and the surrender at Yorktown. The fourth part, “Everyone’s War,” turns to the Americans the standard telling leaves at the margins. Women, Native nations, and African Americans did not merely witness this war. They fought it, supplied it, calculated their futures by it, and tested its promises. At Paris in 1783, Britain ceded to the United States its claims to everything east of the Mississippi, north of Florida, and south of the Great Lakes. Not one of the Native nations that owned and inhabited that territory sat at the table. The British defeat shattered the balance that had sustained the Iroquois League and weakened the position of the Cherokee and other Native nations, leaving them to confront an expanding United States without the British counterweight. And the enslaved, whose claim to liberty exposed the nation’s central contradiction most directly, watched the new nation write the argument into its founding document and file it for later.
Twenty years separate the peace that removed France from the peace that recognized American independence. The men who won that independence claimed that government rested on consent. The next unit asks: consent from whom?
