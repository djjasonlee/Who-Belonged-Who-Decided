CHAPTER 6
Colonial Society and Culture
Four Regions, Two Awakenings, 1660–1750
“Governments, like clocks, go from the motion men give them; and as governments are made and moved by men, so by them they are ruined too. Wherefore governments rather depend upon men, than men upon governments. Let men be good, and the government cannot be bad; if it be ill, they will cure it.”
— William Penn, preface to the Frame of Government of Pennsylvania, 1682[[1]]
By the early eighteenth century the English colonies had worked out different answers to the question of who belonged. New England admitted members by church covenant; the Chesapeake and the Carolinas sorted people by property and, increasingly, by race; the Middle Colonies offered toleration and found their diversity in it. Then two movements arrived that gave colonists a new vocabulary for contesting every one of those answers: the Enlightenment, which measured authority by reason and consent, and the Great Awakening, which measured it by conscience. The Chesapeake and the Carolinas organized themselves around exported cash crops — tobacco in the one, rice and indigo in the other — on rich plantation land, worked by indentured servants and, as the century turned, by a growing enslaved African labor force that the two regions adopted on different timetables. New England — Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Connecticut — was settled by Separatist and Puritan families seeking to practice their religion, on rocky soil that permitted only small-scale farming and pushed the region toward commerce, fishing, and shipbuilding. The Middle Colonies — New York, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and New Jersey — exported grain and became the most diverse of the regions: the Quaker William Penn’s toleration, commercial opportunity, and open colonial policies drew migrants from across Europe, as the Catholic Lord Baltimore’s had to neighboring Maryland. Anglicanism became the established church in several colonies but never the common one; Congregationalism shaped New England and pluralism held elsewhere, and the colonists showed their wish to remain English in the goods they bought and the print culture they built. The goals of English rulers and their colonial subjects diverged nonetheless, and British efforts to rein in colonial political and economic life, joined to feelings of neglect and conflict with Native Americans, set the colonies and England on a course toward conflict. In each region the founders had set the terms of membership, and religion most often decided who qualified and who did not. The Enlightenment and the Great Awakening changed how colonists interpreted those terms and gave them a new language with which to challenge them.
### Not One Turned Out as Planned
The beginning of British North America is a story of daring individuals fighting for survival against dire odds. Already weakened by illness and fatigue from a demanding journey, English immigrants seeking economic prosperity and religious refuge faced harsh and unfamiliar environments, a scarcity of resources, and the hostility of the land’s native inhabitants; inexperienced and ill-equipped, most succumbed within a few months. Nonetheless, English and other European settlers waged a historic battle across the seventeenth century on two fronts: to transform a “wild” countryside into a “pleasant garden,” and to establish a new political order. English charters and appointed proprietors offered the settlers a semblance of leadership and legitimacy, but the Crown provided little support and less instruction. Geography did the rest. Various climates and topographies produced cash crops here, fish and timber there, and as more English and non-English immigrants arrived they adapted to their particular environments to get the most out of them.
The original settlers did not see “purple mountain majesties” or “amber waves of grain.” Knowing only the English countryside, they found North America a rugged land of desolation by comparison; Captain John Smith called the rocky coast of Maine “a country rather to affright than delight one.”[[2]] Yet out of that country each region — New England, the Middle Colonies, the Southern Colonies, and the West Indies — developed a distinctive society and government, stemming from the purposes and the people that had founded it, and each in turn attracted the European immigrants who thought that society more conducive to their prosperity. Alongside them came Africans in bondage, more of them than all the European immigrants combined, and where they came in numbers they created slave societies with less democratic governments. Every colony tried to retain its English heritage while developing, without intending to, a separate colonial culture. The irony is that by 1750 not a single British colony had turned out as its planners intended. Massachusetts had all but subjugated the Native Americans around it and expelled its spiritual agitators while pursuing a “city upon a hill.” Pennsylvania did not become a Quaker paradise, and New York did not become Dutch. The colonies remained British, but their diverging religions, societies, economies, and governments provoked royal responses of support and intervention, and those responses bred mistrust, dissatisfaction, and, in the end, resistance.
### New England: The Bible Commonwealth
Founded by Separatists and Puritans, the colonies of New England were established on strict religious rules and beliefs. Puritans insisted that truth came through direct engagement with Scripture and through the preaching of educated ministers, not through sacraments dispensed by priests. In their view, priests stood more as obstructions than as conduits for the Almighty. They held to the doctrine of predestination taught by the Genevan theologian John Calvin: God had already sorted humanity into the elect and the damned, and no one could earn a place among the saved. Everyone sought salvation, yet prayer, good works, and offerings served mainly to ward off God’s anger and punishment. Puritans built their worship around the sermon, and by one estimate the average Puritan heard as many as 7,000 over the course of their life. New England featured a close-knit, homogeneous society centered[[3]] on clustered villages and the farmland around them, and it emphasized religion, family, and education — the towns were required to keep schools. Its population grew as no other region’s did, by natural increase in a family-oriented society rather than by importation. Without royal appointees or propriet[[4]]ors, the charter colonies enjoyed a greater degree of political autonomy from England than any others, and the town meeting — in Massachusetts Bay, open to adult male church members — provided more political participation and a more egalitarian society, with less class distinction and a narrower gap in wealth. Yet Massachusetts, though more democratic than its neighbors, remained highly stratified by status and wealth. “Some must be rich and some poor, some high and eminent in power and dignity; others mean and in subjection,” John Winthrop declared. The colony’s 1641 Body of Liberties allowed for slavery. The first enslaved Africans had already arrived, in 1638, aboard the Desire of Salem, which had carried Pequot captives to the West Indies and returned with “cotton, and tobacco, and negroes.”[[5]]
In Puritan society church and state were closely interconnected. Puritans considered excessive attention to the individual as a threat to the stability of the community. Neighbors monitored one another’s behavior, and those who challenged the church or civil authorities risked punishment or banishment. One New England town expelled Abigail Gifford for being “a very burdensome woman.” A colony founded so that its people might practice their religion freely, and that encouraged every believer to interpret Scripture, refused to tolerate any deviation from the Puritan faith and code of conduct. By professing to embrace individual insight, the Puritans sowed their own seeds of division.
### Dissent: Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson
The Puritans came to America seeking the freedom to worship as they saw fit, yet they refused to extend that freedom to others. In 1660, Mary Dyer, a Quaker banished from Massachusetts, was hanged on Boston Common for returning to the colony. A generation earlier, Roger Williams had voiced the heresy of religious liberty and freedom of conscience. A colony founded so its members could govern their own religious community decided, case by case, who could belong in it and who could not.
Puritanism had begun as a movement to reform the Church of England from within, not to separate from it. In John Winthrop’s famous phrase, the settlers aimed to build a “city upon a hill”—a shining Christian example that would expose the sins of England. In such a community, dissent and heresy had no place. Roger Williams, a London-born, Cambridge-educated minister who had become a Separatist, arrived in the Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1631 seeking like-minded believers. After brief stays in Boston and Salem, he settled in Plymouth. At the outset the Puritans welcomed him, but they soon judged his views too radical—especially his respect for Native peoples and his insistence that political authority must be separated from religious authority. Civil government, he argued, should punish crimes such as murder and theft; it had no rightful power over matters of conscience such as blasphemy.
In 1635 Williams was tried, convicted of sedition and heresy, and banished. He fled south in the winter of 1636, found shelter among the Wampanoag, and then acquired land at the head of Narragansett Bay from the Narragansett sachems Canonicus and Miantonomo. There he founded Providence. Its first inhabitants—Williams, his wife, and a handful of other families—agreed to obey only those orders made for the public good by majority consent, and only in civil affairs. They also pledged to uphold liberty of conscience. People of every faith were welcome, including Catholics and Jews.
Providence grew. In 1644 Williams secured a patent from Parliament uniting the settlements, and in 1663 his colleague John Clarke obtained a royal charter from Charles II for the colony of Rhode Island and Providence Plantations. The charter declared that no person in the colony would be “molested, punished, disquieted, or called in question” for differences of opinion in matters of religion.[[6]]
Anne Hutchinson arrived in Boston in 1634 and quickly became one of the colony’s most influential religious thinkers. The daughter of a minister and a disciple of the Reverend John Cotton, she found the preaching of Boston’s pastor, John Wilson, unsatisfactory. She began holding meetings in her home to discuss Scripture, challenging Wilson’s emphasis on the value of good works for salvation. Her arguments, by extension, called into question the authority of earthly law itself.
When the court banished her brother-in-law, the Reverend John Wheelwright, for a sermon attacking the colony’s ministers, it put Hutchinson herself on trial for sedition. Anne Hutchinson met her accusers on their own ground: Scripture. When charged with holding unauthorized meetings and teaching men, she answered with the Bible itself. Older women were to teach the younger, she reminded the court; the prophet Joel had promised that God’s spirit would be poured out so that daughters as well as sons would prophesy. If Scripture authorized the gift, she asked, by what right did the magistrates forbid her to use it?
She went further. On the second day of the trial she described how the Lord had opened particular passages to her, enabling her to distinguish ministers of the covenant of grace from those who preached a covenant of works. Pressed on how she could be certain it was God’s voice, she pointed to Abraham. Abraham had known by an immediate voice that God commanded him to sacrifice Isaac; she, too, had heard “the voice of his own spirit to my soul.” Jeremiah 46, she added, confirmed what God had shown her.
That claim of direct revelation proved decisive. In the Puritan order, God spoke through the written Word as interpreted by trained ministers. By asserting that the Spirit addressed her soul independently of that order, Hutchinson placed her own illumination above the colony’s ordained authority. The court heard heresy, and the sentence of banishment followed. She and her family settled in Rhode Island, where they helped found the town of Portsmouth. Later she moved to the outskirts of New Amsterdam, where she was killed in a Native raid.[[7]]
Williams and Hutchinson illustrated how intolerant Puritan society truly was, and it would remain so for some time. But by the end of the seventeenth century the religious zeal that had dominated New England life was losing its hold. To maintain Puritan social and political control, a synod of ministers in 1662 adopted the Half-Way Covenant, which allowed the children of baptized but unconverted parents to be baptized themselves — partial membership without the conversion experience full membership required. The compromise did not make Massachusetts a colony of religious freedom; it[[8]] was not. But as the appeal of full membership declined, political positions went increasingly to men who were not full members of the churches, and the merchant elite began to gain control of local politics.
### Salem, 1692
Between 1620 and 1725, 344 New Englanders were accused of witchcraft, nearly 200 of them in the single year of 1692. Late in 1691, several girls in Salem Village began suffering fits and nightmares, and the adults around them blamed witchcraft. The girls soon named three witches, among them Tituba, an Indian woman from the Caribbean enslaved in the household of the village minister, Samuel Parris. The accused could escape prosecution only by confessing their sins and naming other guilty parties, fueling more accusations and hysteria. By midsummer 1692, courts had charged nearly 150 people, most of them women. One feature made Salem’s proceedings especially dangerous: spectral evidence. Spectral evidence was testimony that the spirit, or specter, of the accused had appeared to the accuser. The accused had little practical ability to disprove an afflicted person’s testimony. Those who confessed survived. The nineteen who refused, fourteen women and five men, went to the gallows still proclaiming their innocence, and Giles Corey, who would not enter a plea, died beneath a pile of stones. The accusers reached well beyond the usual suspects, middle-aged women with a history of quarrels or marital trouble, and named the young and old alike, including four-year-old Dorothy Good. As the executions mounted, colonial leaders lost faith in the court, and late in 1692 the governor shut it down and freed the remaining prisoners. A few years later, Samuel Sewall, one of the[[9]] judges in the trials, publicly apologized for his role.
### The New England Economy
Most Puritans came from East Anglia, a cloth-producing region, and a majority of the emigrants were weavers, tailors, or farmers. Most of them left a depressed region with money in hand: they came from England’s middling ranks and paid their families’ fares rather than binding themselves as servants. They crossed the Atlantic for their faith, but they also expected land and a comfortable living. With no cash crop to match sugar or tobacco, New Englanders exported fish and timber, but most families farmed, growing their own food and selling what little they had left. New Englanders had plenty of labor and little available land, which made the importation of slaves pointless; although the Body of Liberties made provision for slavery, there were very few enslaved people in seventeenth-century New England. Indentured servants played a far smaller role in New England’s economy than they did in the Chesapeake. Families supplied much of their own labor: women worked in the household, children helped in the fields, and sons often remained unmarried into their mid-twenties as they waited for land from their fathers or the town, or moved elsewhere to acquire it. New Englanders possessed less wealth per person than Chesapeake colonists, but that wealth was more evenly distributed. In Boston especially a powerful merchant class arose and secured a near monopoly on imports from Europe. Massachusetts abandoned many of its early economic regulations by the 1640s, yet its leaders still held that trade and labor should serve the common good. By the end of the century merchants rather than[[10]] ministers held power in Massachusetts.
As New England’s population increased, its exportable surplus shrank. England’s mercantilism bolstered shipbuilding and manufacturing in the region, but New England lagged behind the more profitable Southern Colonies, Pennsylvania, and the lucrative sugar islands, and England moved to regulate what the region could buy and sell — the Navigation Acts, which routed colonial trade through English ports and English ships. For decades the acts were loosely enforced while England was mired in international conflict, and New England enjoyed a great deal of economic freedom under what would later be called salutary neglect; when enforcement tightened, it disrupted the flow of goods and the prosperity of the merchant class. The region developed high levels of urban poverty and widening class distinctions, and the more politically independent Massachusetts answered with resistance both legal and illegal — smuggling above all — setting the stage for a showdown with the mother country. The showdown had a rehearsal. In 1686 James II folded Massachusetts and its neighbors into a single Dominion of New England, ruled without an assembly, and in 1689 Boston overthrew it, a story Chapter 7 tells in full. What it left behind in Massachusetts was a new charter in 1691: a governor appointed by the Crown, an elected assembly, and a franchise based on property rather than church membership. The rule of political standing had changed: a man’s estate, not his church membership, now determined his right to vote. The Puritan hold on New England had loosened for good; by the[[11]] mid-1700s the merchant elite controlled local politics.
### Puritans and Indians
Perhaps as many as 100,000 Native people lived in New England when the Puritans arrived, yet many coastal communities had already been decimated by recent epidemics. The English therefore encountered fewer Native people along the coast than colonists found in other parts of the eastern seaboard. As settlement expanded, the balance shifted further: in the places where the English established towns, they soon outnumbered the Native inhabitants. Winthrop believed that uncultivated land could legitimately be taken, but he saw the benefits of buying it outright, so long as the Indians who sold it also accepted English rule and agreed to pay tribute. Most of those sales came after a town already settled the land. Puritan leaders regarded Indians as both a threat and a lure. Like Catholics, Indians in their eyes bowed to false gods through deceptive rituals, and ministers worried that colonists of weak character, drawn to an easier life, might slip away to live among them.[[12]] Unlike the Spanish and the French, who mounted missionary efforts, the Puritans discouraged fraternization and any attempt to assimilate native people into their communities; they saw obstacles to be removed rather than souls to be converted, and stories of native brutality and devil worship fed the animosity. Mary Rowlandson, who came to New England as a child with her parents, spent eleven weeks as a captive during the war of 1675–76 until her ransom. She admitted that her captors had treated her well and that she had suffered “not the least abuse or unchastity,” but throughout her narrative she writes above all of her longing to return to Christian society. In less than a generation the once amicable[[13]] and mutually beneficial relationship between Plymouth and the Wampanoag deteriorated into war. Where William Bradford and Edward Winslow had labored to ensure the survival of both peoples, their sons — William Bradford the younger, who commanded Plymouth’s forces, and Josiah Winslow, who governed the colony — took very different stances, and the Pequot War and King Philip’s War, narrated in Chapter 4, marked the end of Native American strength in the region.
### The Middle Colonies
With a more fertile and temperate climate than New England’s, and a healthier one than Virginia’s, the coast between Boston and Jamestown was overlooked by the English until the mid-seventeenth century. English neglect, chiefly a lack of money to colonize, allowed the Dutch and the Swedes to settle the Hudson and Delaware valleys. Unlike the French, Spanish, and Portuguese crowns, the English monarchs exercised little direct power over their colonies — the wars with Spain and then the Civil War saw to that — and entrusted the supervision and government of the colonies to wealthy proprietors and private interests. Subject to sporadic royal oversight, most charters went to joint-stock companies, like the Virginia Company, or to wealthy aristocrats, like Lord Baltimore. The colonists compelled their distant and weak proprietors to share power. Proprietors appointed colonial governors, but property-owning colonists chose the colonial assemblies, which held the highest civic power of all, the passage of tax law, and a satisfied colonial elite with a share in politics produced more revenue for the proprietor than a resentful one. The Puritans, uniquely, had secured a charter to colonize without a proprietor or direct royal oversight, and the Massachusetts Bay Company and its offshoots in Connecticut and Rhode Island operated virtually independent of royal intervention, with town governments and republican-style regimes. By the mid-seventeenth century both systems of colonial rule had become problematic for an England with higher economic ambitions for its colonies. Seeing a growing population and thriving agricultural economies, England sought to exercise more control through taxation and economic regulation; it also worried about foreign invasion.
By the eighteenth century the colonies drew people from across Europe, but they did not blend them; immigrants tended to settle and worship among their own. Only New England, which attracted few newcomers, remained overwhelmingly English. Everywhere else colonial society grew far more diverse than Britain’s, especially in religion, and nowhere more so than in the Middle Colonies.
### New Amsterdam and New York
Charles II assumed the throne in 1660, eleven years after his father was beheaded by the revolutionaries Oliver Cromwell had led; Cromwell was posthumously pronounced guilty of treason, exhumed, and hanged for public display. Desiring a renewed English presence abroad, Charles in 1664 sent a naval squadron with several hundred soldiers against New Amsterdam, the only non-English settlement between Virginia and New England and the Iroquois Confederacy’s profitable trading partner. Lacking a sufficient garrison, the Dutch conceded without a fight, and their colonists were allowed to keep their property and their institutions. Renaming the colony New York, the English replaced the Dutch as the Iroquois’ primary trading partners, profiting from the fur trade through Manhattan and Albany — the strategic ground between French Quebec and the English coast — and building the alliance later called the Covenant Chain against the French and their Algonquian allies. Charles gave New York to his brother James, Duke of York, and proprietorships in New Jersey and, later, Pennsylvania followed as gifts to the aristocracy. Under English rule the Dutch colonists showed greater cultural solidarity than they had under Dutch rule, building religious and social institutions that kept them apart from the English.[[14]]
### Pennsylvania
William Penn, the son of a distinguished admiral, received the grant of Pennsylvania in 1681 and quickly made it a refuge for the Quakers, the Society of Friends. Like the Puritans, the Quakers rejected the Anglican Church, which made them enemies of the Crown to be whipped, fined, and imprisoned; like the Puritans’ spiritual individualism, Quaker belief stressed the equality of all people before God and rejected a clergy. Quakers considered women spiritually equal to men and, as Dutch society did, allowed women to own property and operate businesses. In contrast to Massachusetts, he welcomed all religions and cultures, and Quakers would produce the first organized protests against slavery in the colonies — though Penn himself owned slaves, and the Society did not disown slaveholders until the 1770s. The climate and rich soil fostered abundant agriculture and a healthier life than disease-rid[[15]]den Virginia, and Penn sought fair relations with the Native Americans, dealing honestly in trade and in the purchase of land and recognizing the Lenape as the land’s natural inhabitants. Pennsylvania built a booming grain-export economy with Philadelphia as its port, and the Middle Colonies became a haven for Dutch, Swedish, Scottish, Irish, German, and Finnish settlers, Africans free and enslaved, and Anglicans, Calvinists, Puritans, and Catholics; by the 1750s Germans alone made up roughly a third of Pennsylvania’s population. Penn promoted his colony in pamphlets printed in English, Dutch, and[[16]] German, and settlers from across western Europe came for its religious freedom, healthy climate, and cheap land. Ironically, the European immigrants who sought freedom in Pennsylvania often denied it to others. As settlers poured into Pennsylvania, their demand for Lenape land outgrew the limits of Penn’s policy of fair dealing. The colony also drew indentured servants who might otherwise have gone to Virginia or Maryland. With fewer European laborers available, planters in those colonies increasingly turned to enslaved Africans to meet their labor needs.[[17]]
By 1750 greater religious freedom and agricultural opportunity had brought thousands of European immigrants of every religious and ethnic background to the Middle Colonies and made a “plural” society. In Pennsylvania the Quaker majority gave way to growing numbers of Catholics, Jews, and smaller Protestant sects, and the separation of church and state fostered more autonomy for religious minorities and for women. A German visitor of 1750 catalogued the colony’s religions: “Lutherans, Reformed, Catholics, Quakers, Mennonists or Anabaptists, Herrnhuters or Moravian Brethren, Pietists, Seventh Day Baptists, Dunkers, Presbyterians, Newborn, Freemasons, Separatists, Freethinkers, Jews, Mohammedans, Pagans, Negroes and Indians” — and pronounced Pennsylvania “the heaven of the farmers, the paradise of the mechanics, and the hell of the officials and preachers.” But pluralism — above all the influx of[[18]] Scots, Irish, and Germans — brought conflict over political power and over land. The Quakers held on to political power by allying with the Germans, who shared their fear of standing armies and militias, so the Scots and the Ulster Irish, made their own alliance as the “Scotch-Irish” along the frontier, intending to fight the Native Americans themselves. Penn had kept honest relations with the Lenape; after his death in 1718 his sons left Indian affairs to the colonial executive, which systematically reversed the policy. Land speculators bought great swaths and resold them dearly to the poor Scotch-Irish; to justify the encroachment, Europeans revived the Puritans’ argument that native people had failed to make good use of the land or improve it; squatters dealt dishonestly and sometimes violently with their native neighbors; and in the Walking Purchase of 1737 the proprietors used a doubtful deed and hired runners to strip the Lenape of the upper Delaware, then in 1742 enlisted the Iroquois to order the Lenape off it. It was a decision about belonging made through fraud and enforced by[[19]] others. The period of “brotherly love” was over. By 1750 the cultural diversity of the Middle Colonies was plagued by competition and mistrust.
### The Chesapeake
Founded by hard-driving merchants and middle-class planters, the Virginia and Maryland elite lacked the mystique of a legitimate aristocracy; many were the unaccomplished younger sons of English gentry, and most southern settlers looked on their leaders with contempt. Competitive and insecure, the planter leadership used brutal methods to compel allegiance from the lower orders — open critics of the ruling class faced whippings and the pillory, with their ears nailed to the post —. The Chesapeake colonies were ruled by distant governors or proprietors who had to share power with the planter elite, and they formed local governments in which landowning men could vote and legislate. Men dominated southern society in public and at home. The English law of coverture, a legal rule shared across the regions, left a married woman with neither an independent legal identity nor political standing apart from her husband, and the skewed ratio of the sexes in the Chesapeake, unlike the family migrations of New England and the Middle Colonies, made for a more unstable society. Where New England had labor and no land, the Chesapeake had land and desperately needed men to farm it. Efforts to enslave native people failed, as they fled to the interior or died of disease, and the harsh labor of the tobacco fields was done by indentured servants too poor to pay their own passage. The plantation society was highly stratified, a few elite planters ruling a majority of landless servants who made up three-quarters of the population, and few servants escaped poverty: half the men died before completing their contracts, another quarter remained landless, and only a quarter achieved property and respectability.[[20]] After 1619 the region would turn, as Chapter 5 showed, to enslaved Africans, and would write a series of new laws to divide the races.
### Maryland
Maryland, the second Chesapeake colony, had a different founder and stayed smaller than Virginia through the seventeenth century, but its settlers turned to tobacco as Virginia’s had, and tobacco planters came to dominate the colony. Chartered in 1632, Maryland began as a proprietary colony: the Crown granted the land, and the power to govern it, to one man, Cecilius Calvert, Lord Baltimore. Calvert’s charter gave him “full, free, and absolute power”: he controlled trade and wrote the laws, and the elected assembly could only approve or reject what he proposed. Calvert planned Maryland as a feudal estate, its land divided into manors whose lords paid quitrents to the proprietor. A Catholic himself, he also meant it as a refuge for England’s persecuted Catholics, a place where Protestants and Catholics might live together in a peace Europe had never known. Settlers died as fast as they did in Virginia. Half the seventeenth-century marriages in one county ended within eight years with the death of a husband or wife; nearly 70 percent of men did not live to fifty, and only half the children born in Maryland reached adulthood. Initially Maryland offered servants greater opportunity than Virginia, since freedom dues included fifty acres of land, but later in the century planters bought up the best land, and landless men had fewer chances to climb the socioeconomic ladder. Virginia’s leaders[[21]] backed Charles I in England’s civil war and, after his execution in 1649, recognized his son as king; a parliamentary fleet forced the colony to submit in 1652. Maryland’s settlers fought a civil war of their own, remembered as the “plundering time.” Catholics and Protestants carried England’s religious and political quarrels into the colony, and many settlers also resented the proprietor. In the 1640s Maryland came close to anarchy: forces loyal to Parliament attacked supporters of the king, and the rising Protestant planters tried to take power from the Catholic elite Calvert had installed. In 1649 the Maryland assembly passed the Act Concerning Religion, the Toleration Act, which guaranteed freedom of worship to all who professed belief in Jesus Christ. The Act offered protection to the Catholic minority, a protection the Protestant majority would repeal and restore as it saw fit. Religious toleration remained a privilege granted by the majority, not a right beyond its reach.[[22]]
### Virginia
In Virginia, class differences were eventually muted by the creation of racial supremacy and African slavery, as Chapter 5 showed. The House of Burgesses gave the colony a measure of self-government; with property a requisite for political participation, the planter elite exercised a disproportionate control, and the few wealthy planters dominated every aspect of life in the South. The Anglican Church dominated religious affiliation, but a chronic shortage of clergy left political leaders in command of colonial society, and women remained confined to the traditional roles of child-bearer and homemaker. Following the Powhatan uprising of 1622, in which nearly a quarter of the settlers perished, James I revoked the Virginia Company’s charter and in 1624 made Virginia a royal colony. The king and his ministers now appointed the governor and a small advisory council, retaining the locally elected House of Burgesses but stipulating that the king’s Privy Council must ratify all legislation, and the king decreed the legal establishment of the Church of England in the colony, which meant that residents paid taxes to support its clergy. These institutions — an appointed governor, an elected assembly, a formal legal system, and an established Anglican Church — became the model for royal colonies throughout English America.[[23]]
### The Carolinas
Chartered in 1663 and named for Charles II, Carolina meant to replicate the agricultural promise of Virginia and of Barbados, and like its neighbor to the north it made enslaved Africans its primary labor force and adopted slave codes to control the African majority and manufacture racial solidarity. Abundant land drew farmers from Virginia and the West Indies and immigrants from the north of England, Scotland, and Ulster, and by the later eighteenth century the Scotch-Irish made up the bulk of the migration to the western frontier, especially the rugged country of the Appalachians. The Carolina frontier offered new opportunities to those willing to fight Native Americans for them. As in the Chesapeake the planter elite encouraged raids on Indian lands and the taking of Indian slaves, and by pushing the gun and slave trades the Carolinas destabilized the native nations, pitting them against one another for access to English goods; as in New England, native societies exhausted their resources in pursuit of deerskins, conquered and enslaved one another, and grew dependent on English manufactures. Diminishing land, high mortality, and the preference for African slaves discouraged European immigration, and those who could choose — English, Scots, and Irish seeking opportunity, religious freedom, and cultural autonomy — chose the Middle Colonies, leaving the southern societies with a majority of Anglican, landless, single men. Toward the end of the seventeenth century the declining supply of white laborers and the expanding slave trade completed the shift from servants to slaves, and the growing enslaved population produced the fear of uprising that the southern assemblies answered with laws of perpetual slavery and brutal punishment.
Those slave codes provided racial solidarity, which reduced class conflict among whites, and the institution itself changed from a temporary contract into the hereditary condition called chattel slavery. Carolina’s founders had imagined a feudal aristocracy, but the men who rose to the top owed their place to slaves, not titles. The proprietors’ Fundamental Constitutions of 1669 promised slaveowners “absolute power and authority” over their human property, and the headright system counted imported slaves as settlers, so that any person who came to Carolina with slaves instantly acquired large new landholdings.[[24]] Eventually rice became the staple, and like Virginia’s tobacco it produced plantation communities in which a few wealthy planters governed the many.
### The British West Indies
England was a latecomer to the plantation economy, but from the beginning the prospect of a lucrative cash crop drew large numbers of migrants to the islands. The English settled Barbados in 1627, and after 1650 sugar — its cultivation learned from Dutch refugees out of Brazil — transformed Barbados and the other islands into slave-based plantation societies. The mechanics of that transformation, the epidemic of 1647–50, the turn to Dutch slave traders, the first slave code of 1661, and the sugar regime that worked people to death and replaced them, are told in Chapter 5. What matters here is the social order that resulted. By 1680, 175 planters — “inhumane and barbarous,” one antislavery writer of the day called them — dominated the island’s economy, owning more than half of its land, thousands of indentured servants, and more than half of its slaves; that year there were seventeen slaves for every servant, and by 1684 the island counted 19,568 white settlers against 46,502 enslaved Africans.[[25]] Sugar was a rich man’s crop because it paid only on large plantations, and the West Indies became the purest slave society in the English world — the model the Carolina planters carried with them to the mainland.
### Four Regions, One Crown
In the Chesapeake and the Carolinas the slave economy produced the highest standards of living in British America for the planter elite. In their effort to appear true English aristocrats, southern planters bought the finest European clothing and manufactured goods and lived amid imported furniture, fine wines, and silk, surrounded by house slaves in specially designed livery; in 1774 the per capita wealth of free colonists around Charleston was several times that of Philadelphia or Boston.[[26]] Like the elites of Philadelphia, New York, and Boston, southern officials held the British view of liberty: the power to rule belonged to those blessed with wealth and prominence, and the social order was held together by the powerful and the dependent. Despite their original desire for religious freedom and economic opportunity, English immigrants had wished to recreate the culture and lifestyle of their homeland. As more diverse peoples arrived, each colony evolved an identity of its own, and identity bred competition for political power and cultural supremacy; change was inevitable, and it induced stress and insecurity rather than contentment. The old social rules of England had given way to frontier living and diversity. European officials and clergy arriving in the colonies expected reverence and found communities of extreme religious and political autonomy — a reversal of the English patterns of hierarchy, order, conformity, and authority. By 1750, having achieved significant economic growth and political autonomy, the colonies were primed for two movements that would test England’s claim to be the ultimate political and cultural authority in North America.
### The Enlightenment
Through the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and into the age of exploration, European monarchs had ruled by the theory of divine right: chosen by God, they governed with absolute power, so that the monarch’s actions were God’s will and to criticize the Crown was to criticize God. Until the seventeenth century Aristotle was widely taken as the final authority on truth, whether moral, ethical, or political. But in the early 1600s European scientists, philosophers, and artists began to question the received account of the physical world and the workings of the universe, and to ask openly why anyone should believe the teachings of Aristotle or anyone else. In 1620 Francis Bacon published the Novum Organum, the “new instrument” of learning, and urged his readers to view the world empirically and obtain knowledge through their own inquiry. The discoveries of Isaac Newton and the philosophies of Bacon, John Locke, Voltaire, and the Baron de Montesquieu ushered in a new era of intellectual life in Europe and, in time, in British America.
As in other movements, leaders emerged who built on their predecessors. The Enlightenment, or Age of Reason, challenged the traditional notions of divine right and absolutism and Aristotle’s metaphysical account of the universe. England was unusually well prepared for it: it had experienced multiple political revolutions by the turn of the eighteenth century. Beginning with Magna Carta in 1215 the English nobility had chipped away at the king’s power, and twice it had removed the monarch altogether — Charles I, beheaded in 1649, and his son James II, forced into exile in the Glorious Revolution of 1688. England became a constitutional monarchy in which the bicameral Parliament, not the Crown, held primary political authority, and in 1689 Parliament passed the Bill of Rights, which secured a range of civil liberties and governed the conduct of the courts. That history of political progress made England the nation most likely to foster Enlightenment thinking.
Several ideas dominated Enlightenment thought. Rationalism held that human beings could use the faculty of reason to gain knowledge. Empiricism held that knowledge comes from experience and the observation of the world. And a belief in progress, unlike Calvinist predestination, held that through reason and observation human beings could make unlimited progress over time. Perhaps the most influential of the thinkers was John Locke, philosopher and physician. Called on to treat Anthony Ashley Cooper, the future Earl of Shaftesbury, for a liver abscess, Locke succeeded, and Shaftesbury hired him as personal physician, secretary, and political understudy. The philosopher of consent had also served as Shaftesbury’s secretary when the Lords Proprietors issued the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina. Locke’s role in drafting the document remains disputed, but the contradiction between the absolute power it granted slaveholders and the philosophy he later defended does not. Shaftesbury led the party that during the Exclusion Crisis of 1679–81 tried to bar the Catholic James from the succession — its enemies called it “Whig,” a term of abuse — and Locke wrote his Two Treatises of Government in that struggle, then fled to Holland as a political exile and published them anonymously in 1689, after the Glorious Revolution had vindicated their argument.[[27]]
Locke argued that the natural state of man was to be free, and so rejected the divine right of kings. Governments were created among naturally free people as social compacts; civil rulers derived their authority from the consent of the governed and held their power as a public trust; and rebellion against a government was justified if it failed to protect the natural rights of life, liberty, and property. Locke also embraced the Aristotelian idea that the mind at birth is a blank slate, a tabula rasa, so that human beings gain knowledge only through experience and the senses. He warned that a government with great power would be tempted to use it to control individuals — yet he also held that natural rights could be preserved only with a government. Echoing Locke, the Genevan philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau explained in The Social Contract (1762) that a people and its government were bound in a compact: citizens gave up some of their rights and obeyed the government’s authority, and in exchange the government protected their rights and kept political order. The people, not the government, were the source of political power — popular sovereignty — and Rousseau went further, to a direct democracy in which the whole citizenry contributed to the “general will” and could influence the state by voting or, in extremity, by revolution. The Baron de Montesquieu added The Spirit of the Laws (1748). Despotism, he argued, rested on fear and a republic on virtue, and an ideal government contained a separation of powers, the makers of the law kept separate from those who executed it — no one person to be judge, jury, and executioner. To avoid corruption and the consolidation of power, government should be divided into branches, each possessing only the power necessary to its function, and the key to a balanced and uncorrupted government lay in the virtue of its people.[[28]]
### The Enlightenment in the Colonies
As the Great Awakening spread through the colonies, the philosophies of the Enlightenment arrived in the Atlantic seaports, coinciding with the growth of higher learning in New England. The Harvard-trained Cotton Mather, a widely popular preacher and pastor of Boston’s Second Church, embraced Newton’s empirical and predictable laws of nature because they meshed with Calvinism: nothing happened by chance, only by natural law and order. Beyond print, a progressive fraternal society carried enlightened teaching, inquiry, and toleration — the Freemasons, who originated in London, were the personification of the Enlightenment in the colonies. Benjamin Franklin, Deist and Freemason, used his press to spread the principles and theories of the enlightened thinkers. Another suspected Deist and a scholar of English political history, Thomas Jefferson, embraced the ideas of Locke above all, and borrowed so heavily from him in the Declaration of Independence that the debt has been called plagiarism. Natural rights, self-determination, the social contract, and the separation of powers fundamentally changed the relationship between England and its colonies and ultimately supplied the political foundation of the Constitution; in the years leading up to, during, and after the American Revolution, the ideas of the Enlightenment were used and developed to justify independence and to build the United States.
### The Great Awakening
One of the great myths about the British colonies is that most early settlers were religious refugees who fled Anglican persecution to build communities of religious freedom and toleration. The truth is that most colonists were content with their religious affiliations and wanted to recreate English, Anglican societies; most believed that social order and harmony required a unified religion, and fines, imprisonment, and physical punishment were used to enforce conformity. With the exception of Penn and his Quakers, each colony tried to make a single religion — usually a form of Protestantism — the core of its society. Ironically, England after the Toleration Act of 1689 permitted greater religious freedom than most of its colonies did.[[29]]
Between the 1730s and the 1750s Europe and North America experienced a rise in spiritual consciousness called the Great Awakening. In North America it was tied to a massive rise in immigration, particularly of the Scotch-Irish and the Germans; the population of British North America doubled roughly every twenty-five years across the eighteenth century, with consequences throughout the colonies. The influx produced a diverse population of different beliefs and attitudes, and most immigrants quickly sought out a local congregation, hoping for belonging and something familiar from home. But clergy were in short supply; many congregations saw their pastor only a few times a year. In Europe the clergy had the financial and political support of long-established institutions and considerable control over their congregations; in North America most clergymen were supported directly by their congregations, who felt in return that they had a say in their own spiritual experience, and fewer clergymen meant looser relationships and more relaxed expectations. Of all the regions, New England was the most devout, with the most homogeneous, family-oriented, and educated communities and the most ordained ministers; the less egalitarian southern colonies were less educated and less organized and had fewer pastors, and the planter elite kept a greater separation of church and state, exercising power over any clergyman who challenged them. As colonial societies developed they grew accustomed to autonomy from Anglican England, and the church functioned as the primary institution for socializing, receiving news, and legislating local affairs. Yet the fusion of church and state in New England, with its strict social demands, produced a steady decline in full membership among men and the young, and by the 1740s women were roughly three of every five members in full communion. The Half-Way Covenant had been the Puritans’ first attempt to enlarge membership and keep social control; it had also served to alienate the unconverted from the full members.[[30]]
New England’s inheritance customs created a problem by the second generation. Families divided land among their sons, giving the eldest a double share rather than passing the entire estate to one heir. In Andover, settled in 1646, first-generation families raised an average of 7.2 children to adulthood, while fathers lived nearly seventy-two years. The land that had supported one household now had to support several. Younger sons faced the problem first. A man needed land or other means to support a wife and family, and those resources usually depended on his father. Second-generation men in Andover married at an average age of nearly twenty-seven. Eldest sons married roughly three years earlier than their younger brothers, reflecting their greater inheritance prospects. Nearly four-fifths of the second generation never left town. The settlement had grown, but its land had not. For many sons, adulthood meant waiting for an inheritance that might never provide enough.[[31]]
Declining congregations and a loosening social grip opened the way to new religious practices, and as Calvinist predestination and the old account of the physical world gave ground to the Enlightenment — to Newton’s natural laws, and to arguments for the free agency of human beings in spiritual as in physical outcomes — traditional sermons lost their intensity, and a new form of worship emerged that sought a more personal and emotional connection with God. Christian fellowship itself, George Whitefield preached, had been neglected: “Among the many reasons assignable for the sad decay of true Christianity, perhaps the neglecting to assemble ourselves together, in religious societies, may not be one of the least.”[[32]]
Protestants began to reflect on the spirituality of everyday life and to seek a deep and direct connection to God where the connection had once run through the church’s leaders. A new generation of colonists — young, landless, and anxious about their spiritual and physical futures — answered the revivals of fiery preachers like Jonathan Edwards, pastor at Northampton in western Massachusetts, whose passionate, soul-searching sermons rejected the claims of rationalism and reached for fear (eternal damnation) and hope (salvation) rather than reason: heart over head. Through the mid[[33]]-eighteenth century the new Protestantism spread across the colonies and across denominational lines, promoting interfaith and inter-colonial cooperation, and the growing print trade carried its message. The movement found its “big bang” in George Whitefield, an Oxford-trained minister who read Edwards’s account of the Northampton revival and, in 1739, came to America — with the cooperation of the Georgia trustees, to raise money for an orphanage in the southernmost colony — and worked his way north through Pennsylvania and New England, where his revivals grew so large they had to be held outdoors.[[34]] Short, round, and cross-eyed, Whitefield overcame his appearance with a booming voice and a theatrical presence the Shakespearean actors of London might have envied; eccentric, dramatic, and passionate, he captivated audiences as the entertainment superstars of a later age would, and he became the first transatlantic celebrity. Thousands flocked to his sermons hoping for conversion and the redemption of their forsaken souls.
Whitefield soon received assistance from the most unlikely of allies, the Deist Benjamin Franklin. Born and raised in Boston, Franklin grew up in his father’s candle-and-soap business, and, frustrated with menial labor, wanted to go to sea like his older brother Josiah; fearing that loss, his father bound him as an apprentice in his brother James’s print shop until the age of twenty-one. James proved abusive, and Benjamin ran away to Philadelphia, where he eventually established his own newspaper, the Pennsylvania Gazette, and grew so successful that he retired to pursue the career of a statesman and the status of an English gentleman. A rationalist, Franklin accepted the existence of God but believed the Almighty had stepped back from mankind, leaving humanity to reap the consequences of its actions; to show that the universe was governed by natural laws that man could manipulate he carried out his experiments, most famously the kite and key of 1752 that proved lightning to be electricity. Despite his beliefs, Franklin was captivated by Whitefield’s fame, attended one of his revivals, and, fascinated by the dramatic sermon, found himself emptying his pockets into the collection plate each time it passed. He decided to partner with Whitefield, publishing his journals and sermons throughout the colonies and in England by the tens of thousands.[[35]] The most famous sermon of the Awakening was not Whitefield’s but Edwards’s. “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God,” preached at Enfield, then part of Massachusetts, in 1741, depicted mankind treading an unsustainable path, doomed to fall into the pit of Hell for eternity unless it received a true conversion.
The efforts of Edwards, Whitefield, and the colonial preachers dramatically increased the full membership of colonial men and of the young, and the new spiritual activism empowered women, Africans, and Native Americans. Not since John Eliot’s praying towns in the first generation of Massachusetts had Native Americans engaged European religion in numbers; unlike the Puritans, who had required converts to abandon their native culture, the new Protestantism allowed the indigenous to personalize their spiritual experience and keep their identities. Women, empowered by new notions[[36]] of spiritual equality, became more vocal and took a larger part in religious meetings. But the more inclusive, egalitarian, and individual-centered worship created friction between the new generation and the old. Called Old Lights and New Lights, congregations in the Middle Colonies and New England split, usually along class lines, the wealthier merchant classes keeping to the structured and hierarchical tradition and the younger converts, farmers and laborers, going to the new; the Old Lights labeled the New Lights agitators and “enthusiasts,” a word that meant mindless, fanatical, even Satanic, and a threat to the social order. The division soon spilled into politics.
### The Balance, Turned Inward
The First Great Awakening had the social force of the civil rights movement combined with the energy of the college campuses of the 1960s, and its effect on both the religious and the political fabric of America was lasting. Reviving Winthrop’s desire for an exemplary society, it inspired many colonists to remedy the ills of their society through individual conversion and “good works.” Spiritually empowered colonists rejected predestination and openly questioned the authority of their political and spiritual leaders. New concepts of individualism, democracy, and social and spiritual equality challenged the traditional hierarchy and the power of the elite, and shaped the colonies’ mentality toward their ultimate political sovereign, England.
Four regions had grown into four social orders — a Bible commonwealth turning into a merchant republic, a plural society turning on itself over land, a planter oligarchy resting on slavery, an island slave society exported to the mainland — and by 1750 they shared a crown, a language, a taste for English goods, and almost nothing else. Then, within a single generation, the Enlightenment gave them a common vocabulary for the head and the Awakening a common vocabulary for the heart, and both vocabularies were about the same thing: the authority of the individual conscience against the authority of institutions. Neither was invented in America, but America was where they landed on societies already practiced, by a century of neglect, in governing themselves. Chapters 3 and 4 found that Native leverage against the empires rested on a position — the space between rivals — rather than on anything the nations possessed, and lasted exactly as long as the rivalry did. Colonial autonomy rested on a position too: the distance from London, and the Crown’s long indifference. It would last exactly as long as the indifference. Penn had said that governments go from the motion men give them. The men were about to give them a great deal of motion.

1. In 1953 Arthur Miller put 1692 on a Broadway stage in order to talk about the room he was sitting in. The Crucible is set in Salem and was written during the second Red Scare, when congressional committees asked Americans to name associates and the surest way to clear yourself was to accuse somebody else — which is the mechanism this chapter describes in Salem. By making confession and the naming of accomplices the only path to survival, the process trapped neighbors in a cycle of self-preservation. Each confession brought new accusations, and the suspicion spread through the community. Miller was called before the House Un-American Activities Committee in June 1956, refused to name anyone, and was convicted of contempt. Two panics, two and a half centuries apart, running on the same engine: accusation treated as proof.[[37]]
