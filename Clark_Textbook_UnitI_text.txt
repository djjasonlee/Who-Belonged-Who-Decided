UNIT I
Worlds and the Exchange
The Americas and Europe before 1492, and what the meeting changed
~
It’s quite telling that American history usually begins in 1492, when Europeans “discover” the New World, and not with the original inhabitants of this land. Around 1100, Cahokia, a city on the Mississippi near present-day St. Louis, rivaled London in size, yet few Americans know its name. What did civilization look like in the Americas before 1492? And what did it mean to be “civilized” in the first place? Most textbooks tell a simple story: a few scattered, primitive peoples met the might of Europe and fell almost overnight. Chapter 1 challenges that account. The lands found by Europeans were not empty, and their inhabitants were far from uncivilized. The Americas contained millions of people and a diversity of cultures. Like their European counterparts, they had developed complex economic and political systems, connected by extensive trade networks and negotiated alliances, and built advanced civilizations forged by centuries of cooperation, competition, and conflict. However, the Europeans who arrived brought a definition of civilization rooted in monarchy, Christianity, and private property, and judged every people they encountered by their own standards.
Chapter 2 challenges the second part of the story: the dominance of one world over the other. The demographic catastrophe that followed makes that claim seem convincing. Before epidemics devastated their populations, Native nations resisted European expansion from positions of considerable strength. Their geographic knowledge, trade networks, diplomatic alliances, and military capabilities gave them power and leverage that European technological advantages could not simply overcome. Populations collapsed. Nations did not. Through adoption, the mourning war, and the alliance structures Chapter 1 describes, they rebuilt themselves fast enough to set the terms of contact for two centuries, as Chapter 3 shows.
But the meeting had two sides, and the Europeans who sailed west were as much the product of their own history as the peoples they met.
## Unit I · Europe on the Eve
### Before Columbus
Christopher Columbus occupies a central place in American history textbooks as the European who “discovered” America in 1492. Yet Europeans had reached the continent five centuries earlier. Norse settlers established a colony in Greenland in the late tenth century, and from there, around 1000, Leif Eriksson and other Norse sailors explored lands to the west that they called Vinland. At L’Anse aux Meadows, on the northern tip of Newfoundland, archaeologists uncovered the remains of a Norse settlement. Wood cut with metal tools at the site dates to 1021, the earliest securely dated sign of Europeans in the Americas. The Norse presence did not last. Greenland’s colony survived for centuries, but the outpost in Newfoundland never became a lasting European foothold on the mainland. Nearly five hundred years passed before another documented European expedition reached the Americas. Claims of other pre-Columbian crossings from Africa, the Middle East, and Asia remain unproven. Across the Pacific, however, Polynesian voyagers made contact with Indigenous peoples of South America around 1200. The meeting left traces in Polynesian ancestry and in the spread of the South American sweet potato across the Pacific, though it produced no lasting settlement in the Americas. Who arrived first matters less than it seems. The Americas had already proved reachable. What changed was Europe.
### Feudal Europe, the Crusades, and the Reconquista
The collapse of Roman authority in the West left Europe a patchwork of kingdoms, principalities, and city-states organized under a feudal social system. Peasants worked for noble landlords in exchange for protection and the use of land; most were serfs, bound to isolated manorial estates and obliged to their lords. European society was accordingly stratified — the rich exceedingly rich and the poor exceedingly poor — and the Catholic Church held the dominant place in it. The Bible was available only in Latin, so people depended on the clergy to understand it, and anything beyond human control, good or bad, was read as divine consequence. Christianity’s first great rival arose in Arabia: about 610 a merchant of Mecca named Muhammad began to receive the revelations that became the Qur’an, and within a century of his death in 632 Muslim armies held Jerusalem, Syria, Egypt, North Africa, and most of the Iberian Peninsula. Europe’s answer came slowly and then violently. In 1095 Latin Christians launched the First Crusade, not only to reclaim the holy city from Muslim rule but to conquer the surrounding country, and a series of crusades followed across the next two centuries. In Iberia the struggle was older and longer: the Reconquista, the reconquest of the peninsula by its Christian kingdoms, ran nearly eight centuries, from the 700s to the fall of Granada in January 1492 — the same year, and the same court, that sent Columbus west. The Crusades and the Reconquista cemented religious intolerance, and Christians looked to colonization partly as a means of continuing religious conquests. The campaigns became a blueprint for Spanish and Portuguese colonizers in the Americas, shaping their determination to convert native peoples and to sanctify Christian dominion. The conquistador was the product of that history — a soldier formed by centuries of frontier war against Islam, in a Spain that in 1478 had founded an Inquisition to police its converted Jews and in 1492 expelled the Jews who would not convert. In England, Elizabeth I’s decision to bring Ireland under English rule involved tactics of comparable brutality, and, as Chapter 1 shows, they too became a pattern of conquest in America.
Before the Crusades, European societies rarely traded with African, Asian, and Middle Eastern merchants. As thousands of Christian soldiers entered the Levant, they found an extensive trade network linking the dominant civilizations of the Old World and saw advances in science, medicine, architecture, and art. European traders coveted goods and innovations they had never seen before and plugged themselves into the market; gold, fabrics, spices, and new technology became lucrative commodities as European demand rose. The overland routes to the Far East, later named the Silk Road, were ancient — they had carried goods between China and Rome — but the Crusades widened Europe’s access to them, and Italian cities, Venice and Genoa above all, grew rich as the western end of that trade.
### The Black Death
The discovery of foreign markets let the small city-states grow in size and strength, and as exchange became common between individuals, kingdoms, and continents, foreign goods flooded into Europe. But as cities grew more populous they grew more polluted. Close contact, a lack of fresh water, human waste in the streets, and growing rodent populations made unhealthy living commonplace, and by the 1330s Yersinia pestis, the bacterium that causes bubonic plague, had re-emerged along the trade routes of Central Asia, carried by fleas on the rats that traveled with caravans and ships. In 1346 a Mongol army of the Golden Horde besieged Caffa, the Genoese trading colony on the Crimean Peninsula, and the plague found the besiegers first. The dying Mongols, wrote the Italian notary Gabriele de’ Mussi, “ordered corpses to be placed in catapults and lobbed into the city in the hope that the intolerable stench would kill everyone inside.” Whether the catapults or the city’s own rats did the work, the Genoese who fled Caffa carried the disease home. In 1347 it reached Constantinople, one of the largest trading hubs in the world, then Sicily and Greece, and from those points it spread across the whole of Europe, as far as Scandinavia.
The Black Death devastated Europe. Between 1347 and 1352, plague killed perhaps one-third to one-half of the continent’s population — an estimated twenty-five to fifty million people. In some communities, so many died that the living could scarcely bury the dead. Trade and agriculture suffered, people fled infected towns, and entire villages disappeared. The crisis reached far beyond the immediate loss of life. It altered European religion, medicine, economics, and government. The Church could neither prevent the disease nor explain its cause, weakening an institution that had long claimed spiritual authority over European life. Labor shortages gave surviving peasants and workers greater bargaining power. In much of western Europe, serfdom gradually declined as landlords struggled to hold workers to the land. In eastern Europe, the opposite occurred: landlords eventually tightened their control over peasants. The plague also contributed to the emergence of stronger states and a shift in Europe’s economic balance. Over the following centuries, European scholars drew on Greek and Roman texts preserved and developed through Byzantine and Muslim scholarship. The Renaissance grew from this wider recovery of classical learning, while expanding monarchies, commercial networks, and competition among European powers reshaped the continent. The Black Death did not create the Europe that crossed the Atlantic. It helped change the economic and political conditions from which that Europe emerged.
### New Routes to Asia
European nation-states sought direct access to the lucrative trade of Asia, where goods such as silk, porcelain, and spices passed through long commercial networks before reaching European markets. Muslim traders controlled important sections of those networks, while Italian merchants, especially the Venetians and Genoese, dominated much of the trade reaching western Europe. Each intermediary added costs. The overland routes were long and exposed caravans to bandits, political conflict, and the hazards of travel. The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 strengthened Ottoman control over key eastern Mediterranean and Black Sea routes, although trade continued. European rulers and dealers wanted faster, cheaper access to Asian markets and, above all, a direct sea passage to the Spice Islands of present-day Indonesia.
By the fifteenth century, Portugal had emerged as Europe’s leading maritime power. Its sailors used increasingly capable ships, including the caravel, alongside navigational instruments such as the magnetic compass, quadrant, and mariner’s astrolabe. These tools helped navigators determine direction and estimate latitude from the sun or pole star. Prince Henry, later known as “the Navigator,” sponsored and encouraged Portuguese voyages along the African coast, opening new routes for trade and exploration. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope. Ten years later, Vasco da Gama reached India by sea.
Portuguese expansion also transformed the Atlantic slave trade. On the West African coast, Portuguese traders entered existing systems of slavery and trade in captives, then expanded the commerce to supply labor for their Atlantic colonies. On Madeira and later São Tomé, Portuguese settlers developed sugar production that increasingly relied on enslaved African labor. These islands became laboratories for the plantation system: European capital and markets, sugar cultivation, and coerced African labor combined in an economic model later carried to the Americas. Portugal’s growing commercial empire gave it a powerful advantage over its Iberian neighbor. Spain needed a route and an imperial venture of its own.
### Columbus
Christopher Columbus was born around 1451 in Genoa, the eldest son of Domenico Colombo, a wool weaver, and Susanna Fontanarossa. He received little formal education and went to sea young. By the late 1470s, he had settled in Portugal, where he married, sailed on voyages along the African coast and into the Atlantic, and studied the geographical works that shaped his ambitions. He read the ancient and medieval authorities, including writers who described the world as a sphere. Contrary to popular belief, Columbus did not conceive the idea that the earth was round. Educated Europeans had accepted that fact since antiquity. His error concerned the size of the globe and the distance to Asia. Drawing on flawed calculations, he underestimated the earth’s circumference and overestimated the eastward reach of Asia. He therefore placed Japan and the wealthy markets of Asia far closer to Europe than they actually were. The ocean between them seemed manageable.
Columbus petitioned King João II of Portugal in 1484 or 1485, but the king’s experts found that his distances and calculations did not withstand scrutiny. He turned to Spain in 1486, where Ferdinand and Isabella referred his proposal to a committee of advisers and geographers. They found the voyage impractical. Columbus continued seeking support, and in 1488 returned to Portugal at João II’s invitation, just as Bartolomeu Dias came back from rounding the Cape of Good Hope. Portugal now had a promising eastern route to Asia and little reason to finance Columbus’s western alternative. He returned to Spain, where his proposal faced further delays and another rejection in 1491. His fortunes changed with the fall of Granada in January 1492. The conquest ended the last Muslim-ruled kingdom in Iberia and freed the Spanish monarchs to consider other ventures. As Columbus prepared to leave Spain, Luis de Santángel, a senior financial official of the Crown of Aragon, helped persuade Isabella to reconsider. On April 17, 1492, the monarchs signed the Capitulations of Santa Fe, granting Columbus the titles of Admiral, Viceroy, and Governor of any lands he claimed, along with one-tenth of the wealth and profits they produced. The agreement offered Columbus extraordinary rewards in exchange for a voyage that promised commercial advantage, territorial claims, and the spread of Christianity. Columbus himself saw the expedition as both a path to wealth and a religious mission. For the Spanish monarchs, the prospect of reaching Asian markets, extending their power, and advancing Christianity made the gamble worth taking.
Most sailors hesitated to enlist for so strange a voyage until Martín Alonso Pinzón and his brothers, respected shipowners and seamen from Palos, agreed to participate. Their standing in the local maritime community helped Columbus recruit the crews. He received three vessels: the Santa María (originally the Gallega), the Pinta, and the Niña. The Santa María was a larger nao; the other two were caravels. Columbus left Granada for Palos on May 12 and spent the next several weeks preparing the expedition. On Friday, August 3, 1492, the three ships sailed from the bar of Saltes. The departure came three days after the deadline for Spain’s Jews to convert to Christianity or leave the kingdom. In the prologue to his journal, Columbus linked the monarchs’ expulsion of the Jews with their decision to commission his voyage. He placed both within the same moment of royal triumph: the conquest of Granada and the effort to extend Christianity beyond Europe. The two events fell in the same year, but the expulsion decree had been issued on March 31, with July 31 set as the deadline for departure. Repairs to the Pinta’s rudder kept the expedition in the Canary Islands for nearly four weeks. The fleet left Gomera on September 6, lost sight of land on September 9, and then sailed west for thirty-three days without seeing another shore.
### What Europe Carried
Between 1450 and 1500, European mariners and rulers assembled the ships, navigational knowledge, weapons, and commercial networks that allowed them to cross the Atlantic and sustain an overseas presence. The result was the colonization of the “New World.” The Columbian Exchange that followed moved plants, animals, diseases, people, technologies, and ideas between the Americas, Europe, and Africa. American crops and resources enriched European markets and helped finance further expansion. Over the following centuries, Atlantic trade and colonial exploitation shifted economic and political power toward western Europe, eventually making European states dominant players in a global competition for wealth and empire. The exchange brought profound changes to Europe, but its costs fell heavily on the Americas. European weeds, insects, rodents, livestock, and diseases disrupted native ecosystems and devastated Indigenous populations. European powers pursued colonization for wealth, territory, and religious conversion, creating centuries of encounter in which Native nations and European newcomers negotiated, traded, allied, resisted, and fought over land and power. As epidemics and warfare weakened Indigenous communities, European colonizers increasingly turned to enslaved African labor to produce the crops and extract the metals that enriched their empires. Between 1501 and 1866, an estimated 12. million Africans were forced aboard ships bound for the Americas. About 10. million survived the Middle Passage. Their forced migration became one of the foundations of the Atlantic colonial economy.
What Europe carried west, then, was more than caravels and compasses. It carried the Reconquista’s template of holy conquest, the Atlantic islands’ template of sugar and slavery, the demographic and economic pressures of a continent still recovering from the plague, and a conviction, hardened through centuries of shifting war and competition with Muslim powers, that Christian rulers could claim authority over peoples who did not share their faith. It also carried, invisibly, the diseases that would devastate populations on a scale no European army could have achieved. Chapter 1 describes the world those ships reached. Chapter 2 describes what happened when the two worlds met — and why, after the epidemics had done their worst, Native nations still remained, changed and often diminished, but far from finished.
