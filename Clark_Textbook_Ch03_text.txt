CHAPTER 3
Four Empires
Spain, France, the Netherlands, and England in North America, 1565–1700
“The Beaver does everything perfectly well, it makes kettles, hatchets, swords, knives, bread; and, in short, it makes everything.” He was making sport of us Europeans, who have such a fondness for the skin of this animal and who fight to see who will give the most to these Barbarians, to get it.
— A Montagnais man to the Jesuit Paul Le Jeune, 1634
This chapter compares the colonization efforts of Spain, England, France, and the Netherlands by considering their reasons for coming to the Americas and the objectives they hoped to achieve. It also examines their respective strategies and methods, as well as the extent to which they succeeded. Much of that success rested on what was perhaps the most consequential difference of all: their relationships with Indigenous peoples. Whether the objective was conquest or commerce, European success ultimately depended on how closely those ambitions aligned with the interests of Native Americans. Indigenous peoples were not mere observers of European expansion. They negotiated, traded, formed alliances, resisted, and pursued their own ends, which often determined the course and outcome of European colonization. Each empire had to determine what lands it could claim and whose authority would govern the people already living there. Mutual misunderstandings were frequent at first and led to hostility, but Europeans and Native Americans learned to adopt aspects of each other’s cultures, and extended contact with Native Americans and Africans produced debate among European leaders about the status and treatment of both. Above all, European encroachment upon native lands encouraged tribal cooperation, and economic and military alliances among native nations and with rival Europeans, as the means by which native power was maintained. Modern understandings of that power reject the stereotype of a passive, helpless, and inferior people.

### Four Inheritances
Four Inheritances: Imperial rivalries in Europe shaped how Europeans perceived and interacted with the Americas and their native peoples. Beyond new territory and wealth, the European nations competed for religious superiority and national pride, and whether they sought colonies, gold, or souls, the Americas became a battleground for global dominance in which each nation engaged in an entirely different pattern of conquest. Each came to the New World with unique histories and formative experiences that shaped its goals and strategies toward Native Americans — and each, as the sections that follow show, colonized the way it had already learned to fight at home. The Spanish caste system stands in stark contrast to the English and French methods. Rather than enslave Native Americans or bring them into their society, the English carried a pattern learned from centuries of oppression toward their Irish neighbors: violence to rid themselves of any non-English. The French established more egalitarian relationships with the indigenous, often marrying into native families, and sought to convert them by peaceful means. And the Dutch, who distinguished themselves as commercial leaders, relied on private companies rather than the state and, in the teaching of Hugo Grotius, held that the indigenous of the Americas shared the same human rights as Europeans — a belief the Dutch used to show themselves morally superior to Spain by refraining from violence and coercion.

### Spain
Spain: Gold, Glory, and God: Gold, glory, and God functioned as the primary motives and ideologies of Spanish colonization. Immediately upon landing in the New World, Spain implemented a two-pronged strategy of conquest and conversion, in which Catholic priests and conquistadors simultaneously built missions and forts to eradicate indigenous culture and strength. Centuries of warfare against European and Muslim enemies had produced a brutal pattern of intolerance and violence that glorified both king and pope, and the Reconquista and the Inquisition had allowed ambitious nobles to obtain wealth, titles, land, and status. That was not a disposition but a career path. The Reconquista had paid its captains in titles, land, and the labor of the people they subdued, and the men who sailed west expected the same terms from the same Crown. By the time Columbus landed, the Spanish gentry and clergy were in essence already programmed for conquest. The mission of nearly all Spanish ventures was to conquer, enslave, and convert the indigenous, as a means both to assimilate them into the larger society and to provide a dependable labor force for extracting precious metals and harvesting lucrative crops. Empowered with royal oversight and divine authority, Spanish colonizers used brutal tactics, along with disease, to establish political and social control over the Caribbean, Central America, Mexico, and the American Southwest, and an abundance of gold, silver, and sugar transformed Spain into the supreme European power. The Crown remained the ultimate voice, with viceroys appointed to execute the king’s will. Population growth in New Spain was slow — very few Spaniards immigrated, and native populations were decimated by violence, slavery, and disease — and native resistance to Spanish incursions was mostly unsuccessful, as superior technology, weaponry, horses, and dogs overwhelmed indigenous peoples. Only in the most remote areas of New Spain were native uprisings successful.
The remote areas were the borderlands, and the Spanish reached them by disaster as often as by design. In 1513 Juan Ponce de León claimed the area around today’s St. Augustine for the Crown, naming it Pascua Florida; he could not establish a permanent settlement, but by 1565 Spain needed an outpost to confront the French and English privateers using Florida as a base for attacking treasure-laden ships sailing from Cuba. The threat took a new turn in 1564, when French Protestants — Huguenots — established a small settlement they called Fort Caroline, north of the site of St. Augustine. With Philip II’s authorization, Pedro Menéndez led an attack on Fort Caroline, killed most of the men, spared the women and children, and destroyed the fort, eliminating both the privateers’ threat and the French claim. The killing did not end at the fort. The French commander, Jean Ribault, had sailed south to strike first at St. Augustine, which Menéndez had founded on September 8, but a hurricane wrecked his fleet along the coast below it. Menéndez found the first group of survivors a few miles south of his new settlement, stopped by an inlet as they made their way back toward Fort Caroline. Having lost most of their food and weapons, 127 Frenchmen surrendered. Menéndez’s chaplain asked that any Catholics among them be spared, but most refused to profess the faith. Menéndez killed 111 and spared sixteen: a few who declared themselves Catholic, several Breton sailors pressed into service, and four artisans needed at St. Augustine.
Two weeks later, the scene repeated at the same inlet. On October 12, Ribault himself surrendered with the next group, and 134 more Frenchmen were killed. The inlet has been called Matanzas—“slaughters”—ever since. The contest over Florida illustrates how European rivalries, and especially the religious conflict between Catholics and Protestants, spilled over into the Americas.
In 1527 the Narváez expedition sailed to establish settlements and garrisons in Florida; its crew of about six hundred met disaster almost immediately in a hurricane. Storms and currents forced them north to present-day Florida, where Narváez split the expedition into a land march and a sea voyage. The land party suffered attacks, disease, and starvation; the sea party, on makeshift rafts, was swept onto Galveston Island, where eighty stranded survivors were enslaved by indigenous nations. Only four of the original party survived the next eight years, among them Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca and an enslaved Moor, Esteban, who wandered through what is now the Southwestern United States and northern Mexico, preserved their lives by posing as medicine men, were traded from nation to nation performing near-miracles of healing and rainmaking, and finally encountered Spanish slave-catchers in Sinaloa in 1536. Esteban would later be charged with leading the first expedition into the American Southwest; years of captivity had taught him native languages and customs, and he again mesmerized indigenous peoples with a gourd filled with pebbles — until he abused his standing by hoarding turquoise and abusing native women, and was killed by the Zuni. Hernando de Soto, who had accompanied Pizarro in Peru, set out in 1538 to conquer Florida for the Crown, traveled nearly four thousand miles through what became the southeastern United States, fought repeated battles with Native Americans, became the first European to see the Mississippi, and died of fever on its banks in 1542. Between 1540 and 1542 Francisco Coronado led an expedition from Mexico into present-day Arizona and New Mexico and as far east as Kansas, seeking fabled cities of gold; he found the modest villages of the Pueblo, who urged him east toward a region they called Quivira, and the truth dawned on him as he crossed the plains that Quivira held no more gold than the land of the Pueblos. His expedition, labeled a failure by colonial authorities, had discovered the Grand Canyon.
Fifty years later, inspired by Coronado’s tales, Don Juan de Oñate led a heavily armed expedition into present-day New Mexico in 1598 and imposed Spanish rule on the Pueblo. Though his primary mission was to spread Catholicism, the discovery of silver turned him toward personal wealth and power; he demanded that the indigenous population pledge loyalty to Spain and the pope, an edict enforced by soldiers, and native persecution, motivated by economic and religious ambition, was brutal. Children were sent to Mexico to be raised by missionaries — some scholars believe they were sold into slavery — and soldiers under Franciscan direction destroyed native kivas and sacred objects. When the people of Acoma killed a Spanish party in December 1598, Oñate’s reprisal in January 1599 was swift and harsh: the pueblo was stormed, and the sentence Oñate signed ordered one foot severed from every man over twenty-five and twenty years’ servitude for every survivor over twelve — a sentence whose execution the record leaves in dispute.
The Pueblo answered eighty years later, and the answer had been building the whole time. At first the Pueblos took Christianity the way they took most new sacred power: as an addition. They built churches in their towns, let their babies be baptized, and carried the saints in procession, and they kept their kivas, their kachinas, and their old ceremonies alongside them. The friars wanted a replacement, not an addition. They had burned masks before; in 1661 they made it policy. That May, Fray Alonso de Posada, the Franciscan custodian, began hearing testimony as an agent of the Inquisition, and before the month was out he had forbidden the kachina dances and ordered the missionaries to seize and burn every mask, prayer stick, and effigy they could find — sixteen hundred by their own count. The Spanish could not even agree among themselves: the governor, Bernardo López de Mendizábal, had just ordered the dances revived. The Pueblos paid either way. Famine, disease, and Apache raids through the 1660s and 1670s read to many as judgment on their compromise. In 1675 Governor Juan Francisco Treviño arrested forty-seven Pueblo religious leaders for sorcery and hanged three; among those released was a man from Ohkay Owingeh, then called San Juan Pueblo, named Popé. Five years later Popé sent runners to the pueblos carrying knotted cords, one knot to be untied each day. When the Spanish caught two of the runners and tortured them for the cords’ meaning, the Pueblos rose a day early, on August 10, 1680. They killed some four hundred colonists, among them twenty-one of the thirty-three Franciscans in New Mexico, laid siege to Santa Fe, and drove the survivors south to El Paso. Spain did not return for twelve years. The rebels allowed many Spaniards to flee; when they could capture Franciscan priests, they killed them, sometimes after torturing them, and they destroyed Catholic images, tore down mission churches, and defiled the vessels of the Mass. Aside from a few such instances, native resistance to Spanish colonization remained largely an effort to retain culture and religion, often in secret, and Spain’s preference for assimilating native peoples — through conversion, slavery, and marriage — produced a gradual erosion of indigenous culture rather than its violent removal.
Spain’s dominance did not survive its own success. Galleons filled with native gold and silver flooded the treasury and made Spain the wealthiest and most powerful nation with the world’s most powerful navy — and rather than invest at home, the monarchy launched wars against French and English rivals and to hold the Netherlands. Spanish successes in the Caribbean attracted the other European nations; by the late 1500s France and England were claiming lands in the New World in ways drastically different from the Spanish, and in 1609 the Dutch won a truce from Spain that amounted to independence and joined the race. Spain’s gross mismanagement of American gold and silver rendered it nearly broke by the beginning of the seventeenth century, and with less and less metal to mine, Spain quickly lost its stranglehold on the transatlantic trade.

### France
France: The Empire of the Beaver: France, like Spain, persecuted the religions that emerged from the Reformation — specifically the Huguenots, whose exodus to North America is part of the Fort Caroline story above. Its claim to the continent dated from Verrazzano’s voyage of 1524 and Cartier’s of 1534, both described in the previous chapter; Cartier’s claim to the St. Lawrence then languished for three-quarters of a century, because sixteenth-century France had no monarch able to support it. It was Henry IV who finally brought political stability to a nation mired in religious dissension, and under him French involvement in the New World moved beyond pirating Spanish galleons. In 1605 and 1606 Samuel de Champlain explored the coast of present-day Massachusetts and encountered the Wampanoag, who willingly traded but violently opposed any permanent settlement; Champlain returned to France arguing for another site. In 1608 he sailed down the St. Lawrence deep into the interior and founded Quebec.
Without significant royal support, Quebec remained small, lacking the financial and military backing Spain enjoyed, and Champlain and his men were forced to seek peaceable relationships with native peoples as they expanded westward. The climate made agriculture nearly impossible, and unlike the Spanish he found no gold or silver. What the Great Lakes offered was furs — mink, otter, and beaver, in great demand in Europe — and trade with the Cree-speaking Montagnais, the Algonquian-speaking Micmacs, Ottawas, and Ojibwas, and the Iroquoian-speaking Hurons gave the French access to them. In 1627, after nearly two decades of exploration, Quebec had eighty-five settlers, all of them men. Champlain understood that the success of his colony depended entirely upon native cooperation, and he understood native customs of exchange and reciprocity well enough to earn strong relationships — a lesson the Spanish never learned and the English never fully accepted. The thinness of the colony was also the nature of the enterprise — a trading post wants customers, not neighbors. I put it to my students as a question: “If you are a businessman, how many other businessmen do you want in your neighborhood?” The few men Quebec did attract had an advantage of their own. Frenchmen married into native families, and in the kinship reckoning of the nations they traded with, the children of those unions belonged to their mother’s people. A French trader who fathered Huron children was no longer a foreigner; he was kin. No other nation would do this — and it is why the French accomplished so much with so few.
The French allied with the Huron, a mighty nation of the Great Lakes with a long rivalry against the powerful Five Nations — the Iroquois — of present-day New York, Quebec, and Ontario, who controlled the lands surrounding the French and became formidable enemies to the French and their Huron allies alike. To secure pelts from the Hurons, who controlled the trade north of the Great Lakes, Champlain supplied manufactured goods — and, in response to Iroquois attacks on his allies, he and two French companions accompanied a Huron, Algonquin, and Montagnais war party south to the lake that now bears his name in July 1609 and fired their arquebuses into the Mohawk line; by Champlain’s own account his first shot killed two chiefs and mortally wounded a third. Selling pelts, an Indian told a French priest, “makes kettles, hatchets, swords, knives, bread.” It also made guns, which Champlain sold to the Hurons, and European guns quickly became the most coveted commodity among native peoples. At first native peoples viewed foreign goods with wonder, admiring glass, steel, and other trinkets for their shine. What held their interest was utility: metal arrowheads and axes, farming tools, and copper kettles that cooked faster, traveled lighter, and outlasted clay. Goods that began as curiosities became equipment, and equipment that good was worth restructuring a year around. A season spent trapping beaver bought more than a season spent hunting and planting could produce, and native economies reorganized accordingly — labor shifted out of subsistence and into the trade, and the surplus that had once fed a village now came from a European warehouse. By the mid-1600s the calculation had hardened into a condition. A nation that had reallocated its labor toward furs could not simply reallocate back, because the skills, the stores, and the seasons no longer lined up. This was not carelessness. It was the ordinary logic of a boom, and it produced the ordinary result: the commodity ran out. And as more European goods became available from rival nations, native peoples adapted, becoming shrewd negotiators who paid lower prices by pitting European rivals against each other. Many native nations displayed real political savvy, negotiating from a position of strength and making the French, and later the English and Dutch, bend to their will. Furs, like glass, beads, and guns, became a de facto currency among the Iroquois.
The same goods that made native nations powerful negotiators made them deadlier enemies of one another. Native obsession with European valuables caused a significant rise in intertribal warfare, in which the more powerful nations inflicted unprecedented losses with guns; hunting for furs replaced traditional farming and hunting, and in the rush to trade native nations depleted their lands of nearly all their fur-bearing animals. As beaver populations diminished, nations were forced to encroach upon others’ lands, producing more conflict and death. European goods and weapons completely altered the native landscape by pitting indigenous nations against each other in competition for survival — and just as Native Americans adapted to European trading, so Europeans learned to exploit native rivalries to their benefit, while alcohol proved devastating in its own right. Every European liquor was fermented or distilled from grains and fruits foreign to native soil, and the nations of the Northeast had no drinking tradition at all — no protocols, no customs of moderation, nothing in the culture to put a fence around intoxication. Some drinkers read the experience as spiritual, an altered state in cultures that honored visions; and traders learned quickly that a commodity that consumed itself made the perfect trade good — cheap to supply, always in demand, and paid for in furs that had once bought kettles and cloth. The wreckage this began has not finished accumulating: alcohol remains among the leading causes of death on reservations today. The Hurons and Iroquois engaged in violent competition over French goods for regional dominance, producing increased levels of warfare, death, and captives. To answer the dramatic loss of population, native custom demanded a replenishing of people through further military expeditions, and the result was a devastating cycle of escalating retribution: the Mourning Wars. French diseases caused still more deaths, and still more political instability. The institution that the first chapter described as the means by which nations absorbed loss had become, under the pressure of the trade, a means of producing it.
The cycle was also a mismatch waiting to be exposed. A war fought to replace the dead is a war fought for captives rather than for ground, and it can run for generations without either side intending the other’s end — closer to a blood feud than to a campaign. Europeans fought for other objects. Spain wanted the indigenous inside its society, converted and subjected and held at the bottom of it. France wanted customers and in-laws. England wanted the ground, and the method it had already learned in Ireland had a name: total war, whose object is not to defeat an enemy but to eradicate one. Native nations meeting that strategy had no category for it, because their own warfare, even at its most destructive, aimed to take people, not to erase them. And while the mourning wars ran their course, the ships kept arriving — more settlers, more disease, more guns, more alcohol.
Unlike Spain, which prioritized the Catholic faith above all, French efforts at conversion were limited to a small order of missionaries, the Jesuits — the Society of Jesus, sometimes called “God’s Marines” for their reputation of accepting orders to live and proselytize anywhere on earth. Hundreds of priests fanned out to live in Indian communities; they mastered native languages and came to understand, and sometimes respect, native values. Many Indian peoples initially welcomed the “Black Robes” as spiritually powerful beings, but when prayers to the Christian god did not protect them from disease, they grew skeptical. A Peoria chief charged that a priest’s “fables are good only in his own country; we have our own, which do not make us die as his do.” When drought struck, the Indians blamed the missionaries: “If you cannot make rain, they speak of nothing less than making away with you,” one Jesuit lamented.
The suspicion could be fatal. Isaac Jogues, a Jesuit whom the Mohawks had captured and tortured in 1642, went back to them in 1646 as an ambassador for peace and left behind a small box holding winter clothes, the sacred vessels for the Mass, and gifts. Epidemic, drought, and famine followed that summer embassy, and the Mohawks found their cause in the box. They took him prisoner when he returned that fall, and on October 18, 1646, Jogues died of a hatchet blow to the head. The missions fell with the nation that hosted them. On March 16, 1649, more than a thousand Iroquois, armed through the Dutch trade at Fort Orange, struck the Huron mission villages, seized Jean de Brébeuf, the founder of the Huron mission, and Gabriel Lalemant at Saint-Louis, and carried them to Saint-Ignace, where both were put to death under torture. That June the Jesuits set fire to their own headquarters at Sainte-Marie rather than leave it standing, and a year later three hundred Hurons, the Jesuits with them, set out by canoe for Quebec. In little more than a year, France’s chief partner in the fur trade had abandoned its homeland. Rome canonized eight of the mission’s dead in 1930.
New France became an expansive center of fur trading and missionary work, but never of settlement. By 1698 only 15,200 Europeans lived in New France, against roughly a quarter of a million in England’s mainland colonies by 1700. Yet from that small population France claimed a vast inland arc, from the St. Lawrence through the Great Lakes and down the Ohio and Mississippi. Explorers and fur traders drove the expansion: Jacques Marquette reached the Mississippi in present-day Wisconsin in 1673; Robert de La Salle traveled the river to the Gulf of Mexico in 1682 and named the region Louisiana in honor of Louis XIV; by 1718 French merchants had founded New Orleans at the river’s mouth, and a network of some two dozen forts grew up in the Great Lakes and the Mississippi valley, with soldiers and missionaries using them as bases and Indians, traders, and their métis children creating trading communities alongside them. France’s claims on the map exceeded its power on the ground. Its forts and trading posts depended on Native alliances, negotiated, maintained, and renewed over time. The lack of precious metals and profitable agriculture led Louis XIV to focus on domestic wars and on Spain, which severely limited French immigration; France also colonized islands of the West Indies for sugar, on a smaller scale than Spain. And where the Spanish had been able to conquer the dominant civilizations of Mexico and Peru, the Iroquois remained a formidable enemy, always posing a threat to the French. Nonetheless, by cultivating strategic alliances with Native Americans, France would remain a powerful force in North America until the English victory in the French and Indian War.

### The Netherlands
The Netherlands: The Empire of the Ledger: In less than forty years the Dutch overtook Spain as the premier maritime trading power, and Amsterdam became the financial hub of all European banking, insurance, and shipping. With a liberal government and the financial support of private investors, the Dutch East and West India Companies became the largest private enterprises in the world, with access to Asia, Africa, the Caribbean, and the Americas. When it comes to exploration and colonization, I tell my students to think of the Netherlands as a corporation rather than a country. The East India Company was the first business in history to sell shares of itself to the public — the Dutch had no crown with cash, so the capital came from investors, each taking a piece of the voyage and a piece of the return — and the Amsterdam exchange built to trade those shares was the world’s first stock market. The instrument was powerful enough to survive its first mania: within a generation, Dutch fortunes were being wagered on tulip bulbs. In 1609 the Dutch joined the search for a Northwest Passage, sending the English navigator Henry Hudson to explore the east coast of North America. Hudson failed to find the fabled waterway but discovered lands flush with beaver, otter, and mink — a motherlode for fur trading. Hudson never returned: in 1611 his crew mutinied and set him adrift in the bay that now bears his name. The Dutch built on his findings without him, erecting Fort Nassau in 1614 and, a decade later, Fort Orange at present-day Albany, to trade for furs with the Haudenosaunee — rivals of the Huron and their French allies.
In 1621 the Dutch government chartered the West India Company, which founded the colony of New Netherland, set up New Amsterdam on Manhattan Island as its capital, and brought in farmers and artisans to make the enterprise self-sufficient. On May 4, 1626, Peter Minuit arrived as the Company’s new director, authorized to settle disputes with local nations over trading and land rights, and soon after he agreed with a local people for the rights to Manhattan. There is no title deed. The only evidence is a letter of 1626: “They have purchased the Island Manhattes from the Indians for the value of 60 guilders. It is 11,000 morgens in size.” A historian in 1846 calculated sixty guilders as twenty-four dollars, and that figure has been frozen in time ever since; a modern calculation by the International Institute of Social History in Amsterdam puts sixty guilders of 1626 at about 735 euros in 2011 values, roughly $951 at the time of the calculation — better than twenty-four, and still far too low a price for the island of Manhattan. Grotius offered the Dutch a principle they could use: Indigenous peoples possessed the same natural rights as Europeans, including rights to their land. Yet recognizing those rights did not mean recognizing their authority to govern the colony Europeans intended to build. The purchase of Manhattan, recorded at sixty guilders, exposed the contradiction. The Dutch understood the transaction as a transfer of title; the Indigenous people involved may have understood it in terms of access, use, or particular rights to the land. But the Netherlands, with only 1.5 million people against five million in Britain and twenty million in France, lacked the population to fill New Amsterdam, and compared with the vast profits of Asia and the West Indian sugar islands the North American colony simply did not merit support. Between 1624 and 1664 New Netherland became a melting pot of ethnicities, nationalities, and religions — the most diverse and tolerant of all the North American colonies. Unlike their European counterparts, the Dutch made no attempt to convert native peoples; as reformed Protestants they maintained a belief in religious tolerance and, unusually, in gender equality. Yet growing conflict with the Algonquian peoples, rivals of the Iroquois, led to brutal exchanges of violence that drastically reduced the number of Dutch willing to emigrate, and in 1664, with only some 2,500 residents, New Amsterdam fell to an English invasion and was renamed New York. Dutch colonization in North America was over — but the Dutch had become France’s leading competitor in the fur trade and for native alliances, and in the process had built the trade networks, banking, and insurance that made Amsterdam the financial capital of Europe.

### England
England: From Piracy to Plantation: As Spain and Portugal gorged upon American commodities, England remained mired in chaos. Henry VIII’s formation of the Anglican Church, the Reformation, and economic difficulty left the country weak and unstable, and it lacked the finances to support Atlantic expeditions. When Elizabeth I took the throne in 1558 she could not sponsor colonization, but she could sanction her navy to plunder Spanish and Portuguese galleons — and she did. Each year English privateers took more than £100,000 from Spain in this way; Sir Francis Drake first made a name for himself when, in 1573, he looted silver, gold, and pearls worth £40,000. The plunder carried its own moral license: the Black Legend — the Spanish cruelties catalogued by Las Casas and amplified in Protestant print — let English raiders sail as avengers rather than thieves, liberating treasure from the torturers of the Indians. England would be better, the story ran; it would intervene on behalf of the indigenous, not for itself. Drake’s “sea dogs” ranged from the Caribbean to the Philippines, and his plunder allowed Elizabeth to authorize England’s first colonizing attempt. In 1584 Sir Walter Raleigh was granted a royal charter to explore, colonize, and rule any “remote, heathen and barbarous lands, countries and territories, not actually possessed of any Christian Prince or inhabited by Christian People.” His three expeditions to the North Carolina coast — a reconnaissance in 1584, the Lane colony of 1585, and the settlement of 1587 — each ended in disaster within a year. The 1587 colony at Roanoke, 117 people who seem to have alienated the neighboring Croatan, struggled to survive; its governor, John White, returned to England for people and supplies in late 1587, and when he came back in 1590 the entire colony had vanished, leaving only the word CROATOAN carved into a post. Croatoan was the name of a nearby island; the settlers were not there either. Roanoke is still called the Lost Colony, and what happened to its people remains America’s first mystery. In one respect it was no mystery at all: England’s first attempts failed the way Vinland’s had six centuries earlier — not for want of courage but for want of resupply. Colonies die when the ships stop coming.
England had already developed the practice of military conquest long before it turned its attention to the Emerald Isle. Ireland is where the English refined patterns of conquest and rehearsed another method of colonization, the plantation: a system that joined land confiscation, English settlement, cultural and religious justification, and military force into a single colonial enterprise. English authorities declared Irish lands forfeit, classified the people who inhabited them as savage, uncivilized, and unfit to govern themselves, and planted English settlers on the territory they had taken. When the Irish resisted, English armies answered with campaigns that devastated communities, destroyed homes and crops, and made civilians bear the consequences of resistance. The objective extended beyond defeating an opposing army. It involved transforming who possessed the land, who occupied it, and whose laws and customs would govern it. Ireland became the proving ground for a plantation model that Englishmen would carry across the Atlantic. The next chapter picks up that model where it crossed the Atlantic.
Raleigh’s failure as a single private investor discouraged similar ventures as too risky for one man — but in the 1550s the Catholic financial rules that had limited private investment and corporate entities were replaced by a Protestant regime, and multiple investors could now pool their wealth in “joint-stock companies,” limiting the prospect of individual ruin. Meanwhile Elizabeth brought much-needed stability by accumulating wealth, allowing private enterprise to flourish, and centralizing Protestantism, and her rivalry with Spain intensified into a series of naval battles. In 1588 Philip II sent the Spanish Armada — the world’s largest fleet, some 130 ships and close to 30,000 men — against England; Elizabeth readied 197 vessels, 16,000 men, and 2,000 guns, and on August 8, at the Battle of Gravelines, the smaller, faster English navy, with the fortunate aid of a terrible storm, decisively defeated it. What remained of the Armada limped home around the British Isles. With the Spanish navy all but destroyed, England stood ready to take Spain’s place as the supreme naval power and to pursue its own colonization.
After Elizabeth’s death in 1603, the throne passed to her cousin, King James VI of Scotland, who ascended as James I ready to oversee a new wave of colonization. Before the new king licensed colonies, he unlicensed raiders. In 1603 James ordered an end to privateering and issued “A Proclamation to repress all Piracies and Depredations upon the Sea,” and in 1604 the Treaty of London ended the war with Spain. The commissions that had turned robbery into patriotism were withdrawn; the men who had sailed under them were not. Many kept the trade and dropped the commission — the same ships, the same prey, a different name — and one of them brought the habit to North America. Peter Easton, an English seaman the records call an “arch-pirate,” reached Newfoundland in 1612 “with ten sayle of good ships well furnished and very rich,” made Harbour Grace his headquarters, built a fort there, and filled out his crews “by persuasion, and, if necessary, by force.” He raided the harbours from Trinity Bay to Ferryland, and the fishing fleets put their losses at £20,400. A royal pardon was granted that February and never reached him; in March 1613, still waiting for it, he sailed into Villefranche in Savoy, the free port of the pirates, bought a palace, and took the title Marquis of Savoy. The Black Legend had given English raiders their license; peace took the license away and left the habit. Every Atlantic war would repeat the pattern — privateers made in wartime became pirates in peacetime — and the end of the next great European war, in 1713, would fill the Caribbean with them. In 1606 James issued a royal charter to the Virginia Company, one of England’s first joint-stock companies, in two branches: the Virginia Company of London, granted the coast between 34 and 41 degrees north latitude, and the Plymouth Company, granted the coast between 38 and 45 degrees. Both sailed in 1607. The London Company went first and planted Jamestown on the James River in May. That August the Plymouth Company planted its own colony on the coast of present-day Maine, chosen because it was expected to share London’s climate. It did not. Because of the atmospheric “waves” of cold air from the polar north, the average January temperature in Boston is 29 degrees Fahrenheit; in London it is 44. The Maine settlement lasted one winter and was abandoned the following autumn. In 1620 a second English colony followed at Plymouth: not an economic venture but a settlement of religious dissenters — Separatists, known to us as the Pilgrims, who had broken with the Church of England entirely and lived for a time in Holland — blown off course from Virginia to Cape Cod, where, before landing, they signed the Mayflower Compact, the first written framework of self-government among the English colonies. The Puritans, who sought to purify the Church from within rather than leave it, would found Massachusetts Bay a decade later. The founding of those colonies, the relationships they built with the Powhatan and the Wampanoag, and the wars that ended them are the subject of the next chapter.

### The Balance
The Balance: Set the four side by side on the same criteria, and the pattern is unmistakable. Spain conquered, enslaved, and converted, because that is what the Reconquista had taught it to do; it broke the dense empires of Mexico and Peru and eroded the cultures of the borderlands, and it was the only one of the four that never had to negotiate. France, lacking settlers and silver, learned reciprocity because it had no alternative, and built an inland empire out of alliances. The Netherlands ran a colony as a ledger entry and closed it when the entry went negative. England arrived last, by way of piracy, with a pattern of violence toward the non-English learned in Ireland and a preference — once tobacco made it possible — for replacing native peoples rather than governing them. For the English, French, and Dutch settlers of North America, understanding Native American culture was paramount to survival, and for decades Native Americans, with superior numbers and military force, largely dictated terms and imposed their demands upon the weaker European colonizers. Each side navigated these volatile relationships with trade, marriage, alliance, and war.
What made that native leverage real, and what made it last, was the rivalry itself. European encroachment on native lands encouraged tribal cooperation and alliances with rival European nations as the means of maintaining power; where there were two or more empires to bargain between, native nations priced their alliance and made the empires pay. The first chapter argued that the nations disease struck were built to absorb loss and rebuild, and they did. But the fur trade had changed what their power was made of. Reciprocity-era power had been endogenous — population, subsistence, the web of obligation. The trade spent that base — furs for subsistence, guns for tradition, dependence for self-sufficiency — and replaced it with positional power: the capacity to stand between empires and set the price. Positional power is real power; it held the continent in balance for two centuries and kept the Iroquois a formidable enemy of France to the end. But it is leverage without collateral. It rested on a position rather than a possession, and it would last exactly as long as the rivalry did. What happened to it when one empire drove the other from the continent in 1763 — and again when the next rivalry ended — is the story Chapters 7 and 9 tell.

## Then and Now

1. The nations of this chapter negotiated as nations. The Huron chose the French, and the nations around them negotiated from a position of strength, making the French — and later the English and Dutch — bend to their will. For two centuries the space between rival empires was a possession that Native diplomats used as shrewdly as any European court. Tribal sovereignty today is the same claim in a different form: nations within the nation, with their own governments, their own courts, and their own treaties with the United States, negotiating alliances, defending their interests, and asserting the right to determine their own futures.
2. Test it. This chapter argues that Native leverage rested on the rivalry between empires. Modern sovereignty rests on law. Is law a firmer footing than rivalry, or a weaker one? Use this chapter’s evidence and what you know of the treaties. Or bring your own: find a case where a tribal nation is exercising its sovereignty right now, and say what it is negotiating and with whom.
