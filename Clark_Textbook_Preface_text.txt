Preface
American History to 1865 · Volume I
For Grandpa Jack — father, grandfather, World War II veteran, college basketball star, PhD, teacher, coach, and my mentor. The man I wanted to become. A leader who was never loud. A teacher who never lectured. Always a coach, never a critic. He had a gift for getting the best out of people. He knew how to say “You’ve come a long way” and “Job well done” while letting me know, “You’re not quite done; the best has yet to come.” Love you, Gramps.
American history can be read as a series of final scores, headlines, and game summaries. Historians have written about the teams, the players, and the coaches, carefully documenting who won, who lost, and by how much. But the meaning of those outcomes has never been settled by the final score. That is where historiography, the history of how history is written, becomes important. It asks questions that a box score cannot answer: What game was being played? What were the rules? What were we playing for? And perhaps most importantly, who kept score?
Historians do not approach the past free from the assumptions, questions, and circumstances of their own time. Each generation brings different perspectives to the historical record, asking new questions and sometimes reaching very different conclusions about the same events. Every account of American history keeps its own score, and every historian serves as a different scorekeeper, bringing their own rules, perspectives, and biases to the game. This account is no exception.
Historiography is the lifeblood of academic history, yet the general reader may know little of the competing interpretations and ongoing disputes that shape our current understanding. Virtually every major period in the American experience has been the subject of fierce intellectual combat: the motives behind European colonization, the consequences of westward expansion, the origins of the Civil War, the meaning of Reconstruction, the rise of industrial capitalism, and the struggle for civil rights. Few subjects, however, illustrate the persistence of historical disagreement better than the American Revolution. Almost as soon as the Republic came into existence, Americans began arguing over what had brought it about, who deserved credit for its success, and what it was supposed to accomplish. Those questions have never entirely disappeared.
What follows is an examination of the principal ways historians have interpreted the American past, the arguments that have shaped those interpretations, and the ways in which each generation has reconsidered the history of the nation.
For more than a century after independence, American historians largely agreed on what the story meant. David Ramsay wrote the Revolution as a triumph of liberty over tyranny, and George Bancroft carried that triumph across ten volumes, adding the hand of Providence. In 1913 Charles Beard broke the agreement. His Economic Interpretation of the Constitution argued that the framers had acted on their own economic interests, and for a generation the Progressive historians read American history as a contest between the propertied and everyone else. After the Second World War, Daniel Boorstin and other consensus historians pushed back, arguing instead that the ideas, values, and experiences Americans shared mattered more than the divisions and conflicts between them. The debate between these interpretations centered on questions of motive, class, and the forces that shaped American society. Despite their differences, both schools of thought shared an antiquated axiom: the story belonged to the powerful.
Beginning in the 1960s, historians challenged that assumption. A generation of social historians turned to the people the older narratives had left at the edges: women, workers, the enslaved, and Native nations. Eugene Genovese’s Roll, Jordan, Roll recovered the world enslaved people made for themselves within slavery’s limits. In the West, Patricia Nelson Limerick’s The Legacy of Conquest replaced Turner’s frontier with a continuous history of conquest, and Richard White’s The Middle Ground showed Native peoples and Europeans negotiating a shared world in which neither side could simply dictate terms. More recently, Ned Blackhawk has argued that American history cannot be understood at all without Native peoples at its center. The historical narrative did more than add new teams and expand its rosters. It began to ask different questions. Historians came to see that an outcome means little on its own; it has to be set in context, explained by its causes, and followed forward through what continued and what changed. A final score, in other words, says nothing about how the game was played.
But the inclusive narrative carried with it a new set of challenges, beyond simply merging the old with the new. How do historians include not only the existence of those long excluded, but their merits, their accomplishments, and their influence, without diminishment or dismissal? And how do they do it without rewriting the record, or the rulebook, as an act of retribution: vacating wins and taking down championship banners?
It is a difficult job, made harder by the historian’s own purpose and bias, and the attempts have tended toward three extremes. The first gave the new actors a place in the story the way the Washington Generals took the court against the Harlem Globetrotters. They had the privilege of competing against the best, occupying the same court, but solely because the game requires two teams to play. Real opponents, predestined to lose, who proved ineffectual against the inevitable. Those accounts acknowledged that Native nations, the enslaved, and women had been present, but they kept the older Euro-American narrative and the heroes of Manifest Destiny at center stage.
The second cast them as victims. They entered the story the way Robert Griffin III is often remembered: not as the 2012 Offensive Rookie of the Year who seemed ready to remake the quarterback position, but as a knee buckling on a January afternoon against Seattle, while the coaches who should have pulled him watched from the sideline. Here the systems themselves (racism, sexism, discrimination, exploitation) took the center of the story, and the people living under them became an injury report, evidence of what had been done to them. Systems and institutions did shape the experiences and opportunities of those long relegated to the margins of American history, but a history told only through them leaves little room for the people.
The third runs in the opposite direction, pivoting from the injury report to the highlight reel. Writing history from the bottom up, these historians searched for the ways ordinary people resisted, negotiated, carved out spaces of their own, and shaped American culture and politics. At its best, the approach restored people to the story. At its extreme, it turns them into the hero of an underdog movie. The Blind Side told Michael Oher’s rise from poverty to the NFL as a story of grit rewarded and a family’s generosity. In 2023, after Oher alleged that the arrangement he had lived under had been misrepresented to him as an adoption, a claim the Tuohys disputed, a Tennessee judge ended the conservatorship that had governed his affairs. The heartwarming version had left the hardest questions outside the frame: who controlled his story, and who profited from it. Romanticized history makes the same mistake. It turns every life into a triumph and, in doing so, understates how restrictive, violent, and crushing the systems around those lives actually were.
None of the three mirrors the full record. Rather than focusing solely on victors and victims, the best history examines the relationships among power, opportunity, resistance, and individual agency. It asks who made the decisions, whose interests those decisions served, how ordinary people responded to forces beyond their control, and how those responses shaped both immediate outcomes and the course of later events. It recognizes the importance of individuals as well as institutions, considers societies alongside the systems that shaped them, and refuses to reduce the people who lived within those structures to mere beneficiaries or casualties of historical change. Complexity and nuance often obscure the patterns we find most obvious and complicate the endings that make us comfortable. But they do not erase them; nor do they absolve historical actors of responsibility for their choices and actions. Knowing more about our past does not require us to abandon judgment. It asks us to make that judgment with greater care, a fuller understanding of circumstances, and a clearer appreciation of the choices people made and the consequences they produced.
The people who survived conquest and slavery, those who persevered through exclusion and discrimination were, as I have argued in my own research, neither powerless, nor passive subjects, but autonomous agents who actively engaged with the world. The historian’s responsibility is to account for both realities: the forces that constrained an individual’s life and the agency that individual exercised within them.
That is why the book carries two questions in its title, and why they return throughout its chapters: Who belonged? Who decided? Each chapter examines those questions in its own time and place: who was considered part of the community, who was not, and who possessed the power to make those distinctions?
### How to read this book
Every chapter includes four tools.
The first tool is a set of three questions to ask of any conflict: What does the law say? What do the morals mandate? Who holds the power? When the Fugitive Slave Law passed in 1850, Daniel Webster stood by the law, Henry David Thoreau stood on conscience, and in 1854 William Lloyd Garrison literally set the law on fire, when he burned a replica of the Constitution itself.
The second tool makes a clear distinction: explaining is not excusing. Cultural legacies are real, they are observable patterns of the past. They are not mandates for behavior, nor are they predestined pathways into the future; we all have free will. When this book traces a sequence of similar behaviors, either by individuals or institutions, which span across generations, it is provided solely to explain the past, not to excuse it.
Footnotes are the third tool. Most textbooks hide their doubts; this one puts them at the bottom of the page. The notes tell you how strong the evidence is behind each claim: which numbers are counts and which are estimates, which famous lines were said and which were invented later. “Fifty-four forty or fight!” has echoed through textbooks as a slogan of Polk’s 1844 campaign; the notes show you the evidence that it wasn’t.
The fourth tool is a question: so what? If a student can identify why history matters, they can transform that knowledge into a material gain or a real, actionable benefit. Each chapter concludes with a contemporary parallel, inviting you to consider where those connections hold, where they break down, and what they reveal about the society we have inherited. The purpose is not to suggest that history repeats itself or that the present can be understood simply by looking backward. It is to give you the opportunity to examine those connections for yourself, question the comparisons, and decide whether or not the past can provide sensible answers for today and solutions for our future.
