INTRODUCTION
Thinking Like a Historian
~
“Ancora imparo.” I am still learning.
— Attributed to Michelangelo
Most history textbooks tell the story of human existence by arranging a few notable events into an insipid chronology of names and dates. Students regularly learn what happened, who was involved, where and when it took place. But rarely do they understand why it happened, whose interests shaped the outcome, or how historians arrived at their conclusions. That first question matters most: without the why, history becomes a record of the past rather than a way to understand it.
Even the way textbooks are organized reinforces the problem. Bolded terms, sidebar definitions, chapter summaries, and multiple-choice review questions help students remember what is likely to appear on a standardized test, but they also teach students to treat history as a closed case file: find the term, memorize the definition, select the correct answer, and move on. That is not how historians work. Historians begin with questions, not answers. They weigh evidence, compare competing accounts, argue about motives, and revise their conclusions when the evidence demands it. Students deserve the same experience: evidence that does not always point in the same direction, and the discovery that understanding the past requires judgment, not simply recall.
Understanding the causes, reasoning, and justifications behind human behavior unlocks our innate curiosity and our desire to explore the unknown. Children know this instinctively, which is why they see learning as fun. Somewhere along the way, history turns into something we absorb rather than something we explore. True crime proves the instinct never left us.
When confronted with a horrific crime, we struggle to reconcile the act with the person who committed it. How could someone who looks like us, lives next door, or works beside us cross a line we regard as unthinkable?
True crime works like a psychological autopsy. Its audience combs through a killer’s childhood, relationships, and breaking points, trying to reverse-engineer how an ordinary person becomes a monster. Historians perform the same autopsy on the past. How did ordinary people come to own other people, drive their neighbors off ancestral land, or march off to kill their own countrymen? The answer rarely turns out to be monsters. More often, it turns out to be people much like us. Understanding is not justifying.
The resemblance ends at the verdict. True crime almost always delivers a clean moral arc: a transgression, a hunt, an unmasking, a resolution. History rarely offers that comfort. Justice is often delivered too little, too late, or not at all, and the guilty frequently die in their own beds instead of on a prisoner’s cot. That leaves the historian with the why, and the why leads to the questions that still matter today.
True crime offers the most dramatic hunt for the why, but the most common form of historical analysis happens every day in break rooms, barbershops, social media posts, or wherever people argue about last night’s game. History, like sports, often comes with a list of winners and losers, defined solely by the outcomes they produced. But an outcome is only the final score, and anyone who watches sports knows the scoreboard does not tell the whole story. That is why I often use sports as a metaphor for history: knowing who won the Super Bowl is neither as truthful nor as useful as understanding how or why they won.
When sports fans debate the greatest of all time, compare players across eras, or try to explain a team’s collapse, they are doing more than arguing over statistics. They are practicing the historian’s craft in a form most people already understand, on a gridiron instead of an empire. Sports leave behind a record of performance, decisions, changing conditions, and consequences. The challenge is to sort through that record, separate the causes from the circumstances, and explain why one player, team, or era turned out differently from another.
None of those arguments runs hotter than the debate over the greatest of all time, and none better exposes a habit historians call presentism: judging the past through the lens of the present. Many of the fans who argue loudest about Michael Jordan never saw him play. They know him through highlight reels, box scores, and documentaries, and they measure him against a game that now runs faster and shoots far more three-pointers than his ever did. For sports fans, presentism results in endless and harmless arguments over hypotheticals. Applied to history, it is far more consequential, because it skews our understanding of both past and present.
Textbooks also leave out the reader’s freedom to remain uncertain or unconvinced. History is full of moments when the outcome was anything but certain. People made decisions in the moment, not knowing whether they would succeed, or even survive. We have the luxury of hindsight; they didn’t, and that difference matters. Looking backward, it is easy to arrange events into a logical sequence and mistake the outcome for the inevitable conclusion. Textbooks often present the past as a straight line from one event to the next, as though the people involved were simply carrying out a script whose ending had already been written. But history was not inevitable to those who lived it. Revolutions could fail. Armies could lose. Political coalitions could collapse. Decisions that seem obvious in retrospect were often uncertain, dangerous, and deeply contested at the time. When a textbook removes that uncertainty, it removes the stakes. The reader knows what happened but never feels what it meant to face the possibility that everything might turn out differently.
Sportscasters often say a game was closer than the score would indicate, and that is the closest thing to real analysis most people ever hear. In other words, scoreboards can be misleading. The final score shows who had the better night, not who has the better team. The National Football League has a saying for this: “on any given Sunday.” A scoreboard does not show whether a team played without its injured superstar, if the head coach got ejected, or that a sudden gust of wind pushed the game-winning field goal wide right.
The casual fan may read the headlines, watch the highlights, even listen to the postgame interviews, and still not know how the game was won. The diehard, the stat junkie, can name the last twenty Super Bowls, the final scores, the MVPs, and perhaps even the color of the Gatorade dumped on the head coach. But what the diehard often cannot explain is why those players and teams succeeded where others failed. The diehard and the history buff are two sides of the same coin, and they prove the same point. Stats and records, like names and dates, can help win a game of Trivial Pursuit or impress guests at a dinner party; they cannot explain why the game, or the past, turned out the way it did, or, more importantly, why it matters today. Even the diehard is a fan in the front row: the seat is great, but the place to understand the game is up in the booth with the coaches, where the whole field comes into view. That is the difference between surface-level knowledge and deeper understanding, and it is the whole ballgame.
In sports, as in history, context matters. Scoreboards, snapshots, and highlight reels often oversimplify, exaggerate, even twist the truth about a player or a team, whereas the game film, known in the coaching world as “the eye in the sky,” does not lie. Some figures benefit from a nostalgic, forgiving record. Jerry West, “the Logo,” remains one of the most respected and revered players of all time, even though he lost eight of the nine NBA Finals he played in. George Washington, the undisputed father of the country, lost more battles than he won, by Mount Vernon’s own account. At Long Island in 1776 he misjudged the British movements and let them trap the Continental Army against the East River, a failure that could have ended the Revolution in its first summer. Washington ordered a silent evacuation across the river by night, and when dawn broke with part of the army still on the Brooklyn shore, a heavy fog settled over the river and covered the last crossings. The scoreboard records a defeat. The game film shows an army that survived to fight again.
Others suffer the cruelty of being captured by their worst moments. Bill Buckner collected 2,715 hits and is remembered for a single ground ball through his legs in the 1986 World Series. The scoreboard leaves out that his ankles were so worn down he began treating them five hours before game time, that his manager had replaced him on defense late in every one of the team’s previous postseason wins, and that the Red Sox still had an entire Game 7 to win the series. Leon Lett won three championships with the Dallas Cowboys and made two Pro Bowls, yet he is immortalized for a showboat fumble that turned a Super Bowl highlight into an all-time blooper. Herbert Hoover is remembered as the president who could have prevented the Great Depression but refused to use federal power. In truth, however, Hoover did not sit idly by; he approved the Reconstruction Finance Corporation and signed the Emergency Relief and Construction Act. Both proved too little, too late, and neither appears on the scoreboard. Richard Nixon opened relations with China, established the Environmental Protection Agency, and signed Title IX into law, yet a single word, Watergate, defines him. A narrative can grow so powerful that it distorts historical facts or downplays them.
Sports fans may never agree on who is the best, but no serious fan would argue that the Lakers of the 1960s were a bad team or that Jim Kelly was a horrible quarterback. Those Lakers lost six NBA Finals to the Boston Celtics in eight years, and Kelly’s Buffalo Bills lost four straight Super Bowls. Yet history is often told as a simplistic binary of good and evil, of winner and loser, without any room for nuance or context. Consider Christopher Columbus, Thomas Jefferson, Henry Ford, and Robert Oppenheimer. Columbus is either the daring navigator who connected two worlds or the man who opened them to conquest and enslavement. Jefferson wrote that all men are created equal and enslaved more than six hundred people over his lifetime. Ford put the nation on wheels with the moving assembly line and the five-dollar day, and he used his own newspaper to spread antisemitism. Oppenheimer led the scientists who built the atomic bomb, then warned against the weapons that followed it, until the government stripped him of his security clearance. Each belongs on both sides of the ledger, and a scoreboard has room for only one.
A ledger can record the history of a whole society, and a society can cook its own books. After the Civil War, white Southerners built a new account of why they had fought. The war, this version claimed, concerned states’ rights, not slavery. The men who led secession had left little room for that interpretation. Mississippi’s declaration of secession stated plainly, “Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery.” In March 1861, Confederate vice president Alexander Stephens called slavery the cornerstone of the new government and declared that Black people could never stand as the equals of white people. The Lost Cause rewrote that record anyway, and its defenders organized to make the new version stick.
The United Daughters of the Confederacy, founded in 1894, helped carry the story into public memory. The organization raised monuments, influenced school curricula, and policed textbooks. In 1919, its former historian-general, Mildred Rutherford, wrote a textbook guide that condemned books identifying slavery as the cause of the war. The monuments rose in two major waves: the first between 1900 and the 1920s, as segregation took hold across the South; the second from the mid-1950s to the late 1960s, amid the struggle over desegregation and voting rights. Their placement carried a message beyond remembrance. The first wave helped give Jim Crow a heroic past. The second answered the movement challenging its survival.
The consequences reached beyond monuments and classrooms. The language of states’ rights became a familiar defense of segregation, while the Lost Cause presented the Confederacy as a noble struggle for constitutional liberty rather than a government founded to preserve slavery. A century after the war, the false account still served a political purpose. The war delivered emancipation. The story told about the war helped defend and legitimize the racial order and institutions that replaced it.
Reconstruction suffered a similar fate, and this time historians helped write the wrong story. For the first time, Black men voted, held public office, established schools, and built institutions of their own. William Dunning of Columbia University and his students recast the period as a corrupt failure: ignorant Black officeholders manipulated by scheming carpetbaggers, while vindictive Northerners punished a defeated South. Their interpretation dominated textbooks for decades. W. E. B. Du Bois challenged it in Black Reconstruction (1935), but his account received little attention from the historical establishment for a generation.
The Dunning School did more than discredit Reconstruction. It supplied an argument for dismantling its achievements. If Black political participation had produced corruption and misgovernment, the reasoning went, then restricting the Black vote could be presented as a necessary correction. Poll taxes, literacy tests, and white primaries helped accomplish that exclusion. In 2015, historian Eric Foner described the Dunning interpretation as part of the “edifice of the Jim Crow System.” The false history turned the brief experiment in interracial democracy into evidence against democracy itself. It gave Jim Crow a past to blame and a justification to continue.
The West was not empty, but the story said it was. In 1845, New York editor John O’Sullivan gave the claim its name: manifest destiny — America’s God-given right to spread across the continent, as if Providence had already assigned the land. Textbooks repeated the phrase until a political argument began to sound like a historical fact. If God had allotted the continent to the United States, then taking Native land could be called settlement, and the nations already living there became obstacles to expansion rather than peoples with claims of their own.
The policy followed the logic. In the nineteenth century, the United States used treaties, warfare, and removal to dispossess Native nations. Later, the Dawes Act of 1887 divided tribal lands into individual allotments and opened the remainder to non-Native settlement. Tribal landholdings fell from approximately 138 million acres to 48 million by 1934. The boarding schools pursued the same project of remaking Native life through a different means. Carlisle opened in 1879, and its founder, Richard Henry Pratt, stated the purpose in 1892: “Kill the Indian in him, and save the man.” Land could be taken from a people; their children could be separated from their families and taught to abandon their languages and cultures. Manifest Destiny did more than explain expansion. It made dispossession sound like a national mission. The story supplied the permission; government policy carried it out.
Who gets to write history? For most of the human record the answer is whoever got the final say — the literate, the powerful, the victorious — and overwhelmingly men. Women were there. They were rarely the ones writing it down.
Historians work from sources. A primary source comes from someone who was there: a diary, a letter, a newspaper story filed that week, a photograph. But a source by itself is not an answer, because a source has to be placed. Every Star Wars film opens with a block of yellow words crawling up into the dark, telling the audience what has already happened; without them, Darth Vader striding through a captured ship is just noise. Historical context is the opening crawl. So a historian reading a document asks what was going on around it, who wrote it and what he meant to accomplish, what argument he was driving at, what came of it, and whether he can be trusted at all. Columbus, writing to the monarchs who had paid for the voyage, had every reason to describe a paradise worth paying for again. And no single witness settles anything: a fight breaks out at school and thirty students give thirty different accounts, which is why historians collect as many versions as they can find. Out of that work come interpretations, and two historians can read the same record and reach opposite conclusions — two books about the Vietnam War can describe two different wars. As I tell my students, there are three sides to every story: mine, yours, and what actually happened. The truth is often more complicated than we think, or want to believe.
How will history remember me? Will it tell my whole story? Will it be accurate? Will it be fair? I often find myself asking those questions when I write about historical figures, and wondering whether I have done them justice. If they could speak to us today, what would they say? What would they want us to know about their lives that has been overlooked, misrepresented, or misunderstood? We cannot give them back their voices, but we can tell their stories with the care and honesty they deserve, mindful that we are speaking for those who can no longer speak for themselves.
The first place to put that lesson to work is the story Americans have long told about the continent before European arrival. North America, the story goes, stood as a pristine wilderness: forests untouched, landscapes unaltered by human hands. That image shaped how later generations understood discovery and settlement, while practically erasing any evidence that people already here ever existed. They were not a few scattered bands moving across an empty continent, but millions of people farming, building, trading, and governing. The story took shape in the hands of those who arrived later, and it served their purposes. An empty continent invited discovery; a wilderness justified settlement. Look beyond that familiar account, and a different history comes into view. The Pristine Myth is where we begin.
