UNIT VI
The Market and the Majority
An Economy Nobody Designed, a Politics Ordinary Men Captured, and the Circle They Drew Together
~
Between 1815 and 1840 the Constitution did not change a word, and the country became unrecognizable. Two forces reshaped it, neither directed by a single policy: an economy that arrived like weather, and a majority of ordinary white men who walked in and took the vote.
“The Market Revolution” is the one no one planned. For most of the colonial era and for years after independence, the American economy ran on a simple rule: grow what you eat, make what you need, trade what is left. As late as 1820, more than seven in ten American workers farmed, most of them to feed their own families. The farther west they pushed, the more it cost to haul their surplus to a buyer. Then, within a single lifetime, the rule broke. Roads, canals, steamboats, railroads, credit, and factories carried Americans out of subsistence and into an economy of wages and markets. As I tell my students: when you farm to feed your family, that is your job. There is little else to buy and no money to buy it with. When you work for a wage, you have cash, and cash can be spent on anything. A business class emerged: Americans who made their living selling things to other Americans. The Market Revolution stitched North and South into one economy while turning them into strangers. It left the country bound, paradoxically, to economic liberty and slavery at once. No convention debated that. No party ran on it. It happened to a generation that mostly experienced it as weather.
“The Age of Jackson” is the change ordinary men made themselves, loudly, in the same years. One revolution ran through the ballot box and the camp meeting. Ordinary white men captured American politics. They tore down property tests, built the mass party, elected one of their own, and wrecked the White House furniture celebrating it. Ordinary Americans transformed American religion, as revival preachers challenged an educated clergy just as voters had challenged an educated elite. The two movements drew their circle in the same ink: wider than ever for white men, and closed explicitly by race and sex against everyone else. Democracy became the emblem of American freedom, and the age took the name of the man who embodied its promises and its cruelties.
Neither transformation came from the Constitution. It did not dig the canals or lay the rails. It did not set the price of cotton or decide that a man working for wages would be paid in money he could spend. It did not strike down property tests, or decide that in New York a white man needed no property to vote while a Black man needed land worth $250. Those decisions came from somewhere else: counting houses, state conventions, and camp meetings, shaped by people the document never named.
The market that brought North and South into one economy made the South’s institution more valuable than ever. The religion that democratized heaven armed both sides: Southern preachers defended slavery from Scripture, and Northern converts set out to destroy it. That leaves a question: when the country changed this much and the Constitution did not, where did power actually reside?
