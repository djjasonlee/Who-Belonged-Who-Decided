UNIT III
Making British America
The Price, the Law, and the Four Societies That Grew Where One Was Planned
~
This unit explains how English colonies, planted for wealth and virtue, became four distinct societies bound to one crown — and how one of them wrote race into law. Chapter 5 begins with a question that has a lazy answer and a more complicated one. The lazy answer to why British America turned to African slavery is that racism arrived fully formed and drove the process by itself. The more complicated answer begins with economics: tobacco planters in the Chesapeake and sugar planters in the Caribbean wanted a large, controllable labor force they could hold for life and pass on to their heirs. As the supply of English indentured servants tightened and the cost of their labor rose, enslaved Africans became more affordable. Disease also factored into the equation. Malaria made the Chesapeake deadly for many Europeans, while some Africans carried inherited traits that served as a natural protection against the disease. To the planter elite, Africans became the better return on investment: durability equaled profitability. The Chesapeake chose its labor force within this landscape of price and disease. Virginia’s assembly then built the legal structure to protect those choices. Beginning with statutes in the 1660s and culminating in the comprehensive slave code of 1705, the colony made enslavement increasingly racial, hereditary, and enforceable. Economic interests helped create the system; law hardened it; racial ideology justified and perpetuated it. The law did not simply recognize a natural division. It manufactured a hereditary caste.
Chapter 6 widens the frame to everything else the colonies became while the law was doing its work. Not one colony turned out as its planners intended: the Bible commonwealth became a merchant republic, the Quaker refuge turned on itself over land, the planter colonies hardened into oligarchies, Carolina on the Barbados model, and by 1750 four regions with four social orders shared a crown, a language, and a taste for English goods — but almost nothing else. Then two transatlantic movements arrived in the same generation, one of the head and one of the heart. The Enlightenment taught that authority must answer to reason; the Great Awakening taught that it must answer to conscience. Between them, they gave four divided societies a common vocabulary for questioning power. Penn had written that governments, like clocks, go from the motion men give them. The wealth, divergence, and autonomy that allowed colonists to put that idea into practice rested on the Crown’s long neglect, just as the Native leverage of Unit II rested on rivalry between empires. Neglect is not a right, and it can end.
Unit IV begins when the neglect ends.
### In This Unit
Chapter 5, “Slavery in British North America,” follows slavery from the Gold Coast and the sugar islands to the Chesapeake, the statutes from 1662 to 1705, and the resistance that answered them. Chapter 6, “Colonial Society and Culture,” traces the four regions, the dissenters and the witch trials, the Enlightenment and the Awakening — and closes with the balance turned inward.
