UNIT IX
From Slavery to Salvation
Four Million Freed, but Free to Do What?
~
The war that began in April 1861 to restore the Union ended in April 1865 having destroyed slavery, and almost nobody who fired the first shots intended that outcome. It killed somewhere between 620,000 and 750,000 Americans and, by its end, had carried four million people from property to freedom. Two chapters cover it: the first eighteen months, when the government tried to fight for the Union without touching slavery, and the twenty-seven months after it stopped trying.
“The War for the Union” is the attempt. The North went to war to save the Union and the South to save slavery, and for a year and a half Lincoln held to the position he had run on: slavery could continue where it already existed, and the war concerned the Union and nothing else. The people at the center of the war did not wait for him. On the night of May 23, 1861, three enslaved men rowed to Fort Monroe and told the fort’s new commander they had been building Confederate fortifications. Major General Benjamin Butler had no statute to work from. He called them contraband of war and refused to send them back. Every camp after that one faced the same arrivals and the same missing law. Congress wrote two Confiscation Acts to catch up. By the time Lincoln read a draft proclamation to his cabinet in July 1862, the question he had been keeping in a drawer had already been answered in the field for more than a year, and he needed a victory before he could say so out loud. Antietam, which most military historians count a draw, had to do.
“A New Birth of Freedom” is what the answer cost and what it bought. In twenty-seven months the Union changed what it fought for, put nearly two hundred thousand Black men in uniform, turned Lee back at Gettysburg and split the Confederacy at Vicksburg, bled both armies in Virginia, burned a path to the sea, re-elected its president, and sent the end of slavery to the states as an amendment to the Constitution. Alexander Stephens had declared that the Confederacy rested on the claim that some Americans could never climb past the first rung of the ladder. The war destroyed the claim and the government together, and it moved the country up two rungs: from union to emancipation, and from emancipation to the edge of citizenship.
No proclamation moved the country on its own. The runaways at Fort Monroe made the law catch up to them; the men who put on the blue suit made the country owe them something it could not easily refuse; and the twenty Black ministers and church leaders in Savannah, asked by Sherman and the Secretary of War on January 12, 1865, what freedom meant to them, answered land.
These two chapters close the first half of the sequence, and they close it mid-argument. On April 11, 1865, Lincoln said he would prefer that Louisiana give the vote to its “very intelligent” Black men and to “those who serve our cause as soldiers.” No president had ever said as much in public, and Lincoln never spoke in public again. Three days later, John Wilkes Booth shot him. The rung above the one Lincoln had pointed toward remained unsettled: whether four million people would belong to the nation as citizens, as voters, and as owners of the land the Savannah ministers had asked for. That question is the whole subject of the half that follows.
