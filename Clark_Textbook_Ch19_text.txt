CHAPTER 19
The War for the Union
From Fort Sumter to Emancipation, 1861–1862
“Let me tell you what is coming. After the sacrifice of countless millions of treasure and hundreds of thousands of lives, you may win Southern independence if God be not against you, but I doubt it.”
— Sam Houston, 1861
By 1861 the federal government the founders had built could no longer hold itself together. Designed to exercise limited control over the states, it now faced two sections that read the same Constitution in opposite directions: Northern arguments for due process collided with Southern claims of protected property, and the declaration that “all men are created equal” had driven both regions beyond the point of compromise. Secession appealed to the South for two reasons. Northern insistence on free soil led the planter elite to see non-slaveholding farmers and the growth of industry as threats to the Cotton Kingdom, and white Southerners held an inflated faith in the superiority of their race and their slave-based economy, which they believed would thrive once freed from Northern interference. Lincoln’s election ended decades in which the South could count on an advocate in the White House, and paranoid rhetoric across the region painted an apocalyptic picture of a North bent on destroying slavery. By his inauguration, seven Deep South states had formed the Confederate States of America, and Lincoln declared secession illegal. Most seceding states justified themselves as resisting federal overreach, but their own declarations, as A House Divided showed, moved quickly from the language of state sovereignty to the protection of property in slaves. Terms like “Southern honor,” “states’ rights,” and “federal coercion” dressed the cause; the preservation of slavery and white supremacy was its substance.[[1]]
This chapter follows the war’s first eighteen months, from the guns at Charleston to the bloodiest single day in American history, and its argument is this. The North went to war to save the Union and the South to save slavery, and for a year and a half Lincoln tried to fight the first war without touching the second — until the enslaved, fleeing into Union lines at Fort Monroe and every camp after it, forced the question the President had been holding in a drawer. The chapter ends where Lincoln finally opened it. Who the war was for, and who had the right to say so, was a question Washington meant to answer alone. The enslaved who walked into Union lines answered it first, and left the government no choice but to reckon with what it had tried to postpone.
### Seventy-Five Thousand for Ninety Days
Fort Sumter, a brick fortress on an island in Charleston Harbor, offered a test neither side could avoid. South Carolina insisted the fort now belonged to the Confederacy; the United States insisted it did not. Major Robert Anderson, a Kentuckian and a former slaveholder who stayed loyal to the Union, had moved his small garrison of eighty-five men into it the day after Christmas 1860, and by spring his provisions were running out. President Buchanan had authorized an attempt to resupply the fort, and it had failed. Lincoln chose a different course. On April 6, 1861, he notified South Carolina’s governor that “an attempt will be made to supply Fort Sumter with provisions only” — food for hungry soldiers, not reinforcements — which placed the decision to fire squarely on the Confederacy. Jefferson Davis made it. At 4:30 in the morning on April 12, Confederate batteries under General P. G. T. Beauregard opened fire on the fort. Beauregard had once been Anderson’s student: Anderson had taught him artillery at West Point. After thirty-four hours of bombardment, Anderson surrendered, and on April 14 he marched his men out with the flag he would carry home.[[2]]
It is worth asking who built the batteries. Confederate payrolls for the harbor forts, which run from 1861 through 1864, list thousands of enslaved laborers — 1,210 at Fort Sumter itself after its capture, 3,072 on Sullivan’s Island, 920 at Fort Johnson, and 562 on Morris Island — with the wages for their labor paid not to them but to the people who claimed to own them. The same rolls record those who ran: 248 from Sullivan’s Island, 72 from Fort Johnson. The enslaved understood what the war might mean before either government admitted it. In March 1861, weeks before the bombardment, a teenage slave crossed Charleston Harbor to Fort Sumter, convinced that the new president meant to free the slaves and that Major Anderson would protect him. Anderson turned him over to the authorities in Charleston.[[3]]
On April 15, Lincoln called 75,000 state militiamen into federal service for ninety days to suppress an insurrection “too powerful to be suppressed by the ordinary course of judicial proceedings,” and he proclaimed a naval blockade of the Southern coast. The North answered with enthusiasm. Republican governors offered more regiments than the call required, and even Northern Democrats rallied to the flag. “Every man must be for the United States or against it,” Stephen Douglas declared in Chicago, weeks before his death. “There can be no neutrals in this war, only patriots — or traitors.” The ninety-day term revealed how short a war Lincoln expected.[[4]]
The call also forced the Upper South to choose, and it confirmed white Southerners’ fears of federal “coercion.” Virginia, the most populous Southern state and home to many of the nation’s best soldiers, was divided. Its convention voted for secession on April 17, 1861, though many of its northwestern counties stood with the Union. The next day Colonel Robert E. Lee, recommended by the veteran General Winfield Scott, was offered command of the Union’s field army. Lee declined, and on April 20 he resigned his commission rather than draw his sword against his native state. Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina soon followed Virginia out, bringing the Confederacy to eleven states, and the Confederate capital moved from Montgomery to Richmond. Davis arrived there on May 29, 1861, to cannon salutes, brass bands, and bouquets from the crowd — a scene that contrasted sharply with Lincoln’s own arrival in Washington three months earlier, when, warned of a plot against his life, he had slipped into the capital at dawn in a curtained sleeping car.[[5]]
### The Whole Game
Lincoln moved at once to hold the slave states that had not yet left: Maryland, Delaware, Missouri, and Kentucky, the state of his birth. The border states were geographically and militarily strategic, and each side looked to them as critical to winning the war. Kentucky, the most divided of them and the birthplace of both Lincoln and Davis, held the Ohio River, a line far easier to defend than the mountains of Tennessee. Lincoln’s view of what was at stake survives in a letter to Senator Orville Browning in September 1861: “I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game. Kentucky gone, we can not hold Missouri, nor, as I think, Maryland. These all against us, and the job on our hands is too large for us.” He kept Union troops out of Kentucky as long as he could, hoping to win the loyalty of its people rather than compel it.[[6]]
Missouri mattered almost as much, because whoever held it could command the Mississippi River valley. Missouri had a head start on the war: it had supplied many of the Border Ruffians who fought the Jayhawkers in Bleeding Kansas, and like Kentucky it now endured a vicious guerrilla war of bushwhacking and terror that would produce outlaws like Frank and Jesse James. Maryland posed the most immediate problem of all, for if Maryland seceded, the national capital would be surrounded by the Confederacy. In April 1861 a mob in Baltimore attacked federal troops passing through the city, and Marylanders cut the rail and telegraph lines north, forcing regiments bound for Washington to detour by water down the Chesapeake Bay. The border states made the Civil War a true brothers’ war, splitting families as well as counties. And the war remade the map. The Unionist counties of northwestern Virginia organized a loyal government of their own at Wheeling in June 1861, and that government supplied the consent the Constitution requires for dividing a state. West Virginia entered the Union on June 20, 1863.[[7]]
### Contraband
The role African Americans played in the Civil War, enslaved and free, is the part of its story most often left out. On the eve of the war, the South held nearly four million people in slavery, and close to half a million free people of color lived in the United States, about half of them in the North. Far from being passive figures, these men and women shaped political and military policy from the war’s first weeks. In the North, Black abolitionists and community leaders contributed to the war effort by raising funds, supplying goods and labor, and eventually served in the Union Army. In the South, the people the war was being fought over took matters into their own hands. Runaways had been driving the sections apart for years before Sumter — the Fugitive Slave Act was the nation’s Rubicon — and Lincoln’s election only emboldened more of them to flee. When the fighting began, Lincoln clung to his prior positions: slavery could continue where it already existed, the Constitution protected property, and he would enforce the Fugitive Slave Act as a show of good faith to the border states. The enslaved did not wait for him to change his mind.[[8]]
They had been testing the Union’s intentions since before the first shot — the boy who crossed the harbor at Sumter, and the runaways who reached Fort Pickens on the Florida coast and were returned to their owners. Decades of abolitionist agitation had fed the hope that emancipation was near, and Lincoln’s election seemed to confirm it, sparking a surge in escapes. By May 1861, as the Union army gathered to protect Washington, fugitives were already making their way to Union lines and Union camps. At first the army was ordered to respect private property and to return runaways under the Fugitive Slave Law, to secure the loyalty of slaveholding elites in the border states. The flight should not have surprised anyone; the enslaved had done the same during the Revolution and the War of 1812. And as Northern soldiers watched the intelligence and skill with which people pursued their own freedom, many of the stereotypes the South’s propaganda had spread began to crumble.[[9]]
As the war went on, the trickle of runaways became a flood, and it forced both governments to change their legal and military strategies. The most significant effect fell on the South’s labor. Slavery had freed the Southern elite to pursue politics, business, and now war; as the labor force slipped away toward Union lines, fields went untilled and crops unharvested, with no one to carry them to market. Planters moved the people they enslaved deep into the interior, away from the armies. Only a fraction of the enslaved could ever reach Union lines, but every escape fed resistance among those left behind, and productivity and order on the plantations declined month by month. The Confederacy responded by impressing enslaved men into military labor, both to get the work done and to keep them under watch. For the Union, the fugitives brought problems of a different kind. Camps filled with men, women, and children who needed food and shelter, while officers decided, without orders, what to do with them. They also brought something the generals had not expected: information about the roads, the terrain, and the movements of Confederate troops.[[10]]
On the night of May 23, 1861, three enslaved men — Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James Townsend — rowed to Fort Monroe, the Union stronghold at the tip of the Virginia peninsula, near Point Comfort, where “20 and odd” Africans had been brought ashore in 1619. They told the fort’s new commander, Major General Benjamin Butler, that they had been put to work building Confederate fortifications and were about to be sent south. Butler, who needed laborers himself, let them stay. When a Confederate officer arrived under a flag of truce to demand their return, Butler answered that the Fugitive Slave Law did not apply to a state that claimed to be a foreign country, and that slaves used to build enemy works were property that could be seized like any other military asset: “contraband of war.” Within weeks, hundreds of “contrabands” had found their way to Fort Monroe, and the word spread across the South.[[11]]
Congress followed the fugitives. After Bull Run, Northern officials confronted the painful reality that the war would be long, and that slavery let the South send most of its white men of military age into the army while the enslaved fed it, built its fortifications, and hauled its supplies. On July 22, 1861, Senator Lyman Trumbull of Illinois, exhausted from having ridden out to watch the battle, told the Senate he had seen enslaved men at work for the Confederate army, and his testimony helped carry a bill to confiscate any slaves used to aid the rebellion. Democrats and the proslavery men of the border states opposed it, wanting slavery left untouched, and Lincoln shared their fear of losing the border. Despite Lincoln’s apprehension, support for the Confiscation Act continued to grow with each Confederate victory. On August 6, Lincoln reluctantly signed the First Confiscation Act, which authorized the seizure of any slaves used to aid the Confederate war effort. It did not legally free them, but it was enforced more broadly than its words, because Secretary of War Simon Cameron instructed officers to receive fugitives from loyal and disloyal masters alike and leave compensation for Congress to sort out later. Some generals went further than Lincoln would allow. In August 1861 John C. Frémont declared free the slaves of rebels in Missouri, and in May 1862 David Hunter declared free all the enslaved in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida; Lincoln revoked both orders. But the clashes between Republicans in Congress and conservative generals over what the law required led to a Second Confiscation Act on July 17, 1862, which went far beyond the first. It made confiscation a punishment for treason and declared the slaves of anyone in rebellion “captives of war” who would be “forever free.” Without the actions of thousands of runaways, the North might well have ignored slavery as part of its strategy for winning the war. Rather than wait for deliverance, these freedom seekers effectively initiated new political and military strategies for the North and South, which completely changed the trajectory of the Civil War. Controversial as they were, the Confiscation Acts gave former slaves a chance to prove their loyalty to the Union while weakening the Confederacy’s economy, and they set in motion the arguments that would lead Lincoln, within a year, to open the Union army to Black soldiers.[[12]]
### Complex Characters: Harriet Tubman
Harriet Tubman arrives in most classrooms as a legend: Moses, the conductor who never lost a passenger. The woman behind the legend did more varied work than the legend allows, and some of it makes for harder reading.
Born Araminta Ross in Dorchester County, Maryland, around 1822, she grew up enslaved on the Eastern Shore. As a girl of twelve or thirteen, she stood in a village store when an overseer threw a two-pound weight at another enslaved man, and it struck her in the head. For the rest of her life she suffered sudden sleeping spells, headaches, and vivid visions. Some modern scholars suggest temporal lobe epilepsy, a hypothesis no one can confirm. Tubman understood the visions as messages from God, and that faith shaped how she weighed danger. In 1849, fearing sale, she escaped alone to Philadelphia.
Over the next eleven years she returned to Maryland about thirteen times and personally led about seventy people out, among them her brothers and her aged parents, while giving directions to dozens more who escaped on their own. A reward notice published in October 1849 offered one hundred dollars for her capture; the forty-thousand-dollar bounty of later legend has no documentary support.[[13]]
The work required authority, and she exercised it. She carried a pistol, and by her own account, recorded by her biographer Sarah Bradford, when a frightened fugitive wanted to turn back, she pointed it at him and told him, “you go on or die!” A man who returned could betray the entire party to the slave catchers. The episode shows a woman responsible for many lives at once, making a decision no one else in the party would make.[[14]]
The war gave her a wider field. In South Carolina she nursed sick soldiers and freedpeople, cooked, and organized eight or nine scouts who gathered intelligence from the enslaved along the coast. The raid up the Combahee River in June 1863, commanded by Colonel James Montgomery, ran on what those scouts learned. Tubman went upriver with the expedition herself, and more than seven hundred enslaved people left the Combahee plantations for the Union boats.[[15]]
Recognition came slowly, and on other people’s terms. In 1869 she married Nelson Davis, a Union veteran, and settled in Auburn, New York. Some years after his death in 1888, she began receiving a widow’s pension of eight dollars a month. She also petitioned Congress for pay for her own three years of service as nurse, cook, and leader of scouts, asking $1,800. In 1899 Congress raised her pension to twenty dollars a month; the House had proposed twenty-five, and the Senate cut it. The law raised her widow’s pension in recognition of her service as a nurse.[[16]]
She kept working. In Auburn she took in the poor, the old, and the sick, raised money, bought land beside her home in 1896, and opened a home for aged and indigent Black Americans in 1908. She spoke for women’s suffrage in her last decades. She died on March 10, 1913, having deeded the home to the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church.[[17]]
The legend grew from real sources. Sarah Bradford’s Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman (1869), written partly to raise money for her, preserved testimony that would otherwise have vanished, and it also put nineteen trips and some three hundred rescues into print, numbers later historians could not confirm. In 2016 the Treasury announced plans to put her portrait on the twenty-dollar bill. The record shows a woman who kept working after the country had celebrated her, and who still had to petition for pay for what she had done.[[18]]
### Advantages, Objectives, and Strategy
Each side went to war with strengths and weaknesses, and each army represented a cross section of its society. The Union army drew mainly young farm boys, artisans, and factory workers; the Confederate army was made up mostly of non-slaveholding farmers led by a slaveholding elite. For both, military service promised glory and a path to advancement, and fifteen years after the Mexican War many young men saw the conflict as a life-changing adventure. In hindsight the North looks like the overwhelming favorite. The eleven Confederate states held about nine million people, three and a half million of them enslaved; the Union states held more than twenty-two million. The North held a four-to-one advantage in white men of military age, about 110,000 manufacturing establishments to the South’s 18,000, more than twice the South’s railroad mileage, twice its draft animals, and a standing army and navy. But the South possessed strengths of its own. It did not have to conquer the United States to win. Like Washington’s Continental Army, it needed only to survive, defending its own ground and wearing down its opponent’s will to fight. Its territory — three-quarters of a million square miles and thousands of miles of coastline — was a daunting space to invade and occupy. Both armies were led by West Point men who had learned their trade together in Mexico, and on the question of Britain and France, the war’s wild card, the South rested many of its hopes.[[19]]
Confederate leaders compared themselves to the patriots of 1776. Like their grandfathers, Jefferson Davis told his congress, white Southerners were fighting for the “sacred right of self-government”: “We seek no conquest, no aggrandizement … all we ask is to be let alone.” Among the South’s sacred rights was the freedom to deny freedom to others; the Confederate Constitution and Alexander Stephens’s Cornerstone Speech, examined in A House Divided, made that plain. The South’s strategy followed from its aims. It needed only to defend its borders and outlast the Union in a war of attrition until Northern support collapsed into division, stalemate, and Confederate independence — ideally with the help of Britain and France, despite their antislavery sentiments.[[20]]
Lincoln answered Davis in a message to Congress on July 4, 1861, which framed secession as an attack on representative government itself. The issue, he said, was whether a constitutional republic could “maintain its territorial integrity, against its own domestic foes.” He asked for “at least four hundred thousand men, and four hundred millions of dollars” to make “this contest a short, and a decisive one.” The chief architect of Northern strategy was the seventy-four-year-old General Winfield Scott, whose plan the newspapers mocked as the “Anaconda”: push down the Mississippi to cut the Confederacy in two, blockade its coast to strangle its trade and block foreign aid, and only then, if necessary, strike at Richmond. Scott thought it might take two or three years and hundreds of thousands of men, and it did. But Lincoln was under pressure for a quick victory, and newspapers across the North cried “Forward to Richmond!” — only a hundred miles from Washington. His generals warned that their troops were too green. Lincoln answered that the Confederates were green too.[[21]]
Lincoln went to war to restore the Union, not to destroy slavery, and no one imagined a conflict that would last four years and kill somewhere between 620,000 and 750,000 Americans — a range, not a number, because so many of the dead were never identified or counted. Both sides expected a short war, and both were wrong. The war would bring wild swings in military fortune, in public support, and, above all, in purpose. And Union soldiers, sure of their own patriotic duty, quickly discovered that their Southern counterparts held equal, if not greater, conviction in the nobility of theirs. What began for the Union as a war to reunite the country would become a struggle over human freedom, as thousands of Union soldiers, most of whom had never supported abolition, came face to face with the people their enemies held in bondage.[[22]]
### The First Modern War
Before the Civil War, Americans fought in the European style. From the French and Indian War through Mexico, armies of infantry, cavalry, and artillery met on open fields and exchanged choreographed volleys at a hundred yards or less. The standard infantry weapon of the Revolution was the smoothbore flintlock musket: a soldier rammed a ball down the barrel, poured powder into the pan, and pulled the trigger to strike a spark. Because each charge of powder varied, so did the velocity and accuracy of each shot, and every volley wrapped the field in thick white smoke. Even a skilled soldier could fire only about three rounds a minute, and after a volley troops often charged with the bayonet into gruesome hand-to-hand fighting. Battles of that kind usually cost hundreds of casualties, not thousands.[[23]]
By the 1850s, new weapons were making war far deadlier. Rifling — spiral grooves cut inside the barrel — spun the bullet for distance and accuracy, as frontier riflemen had shown during the Revolution. In 1849 a French officer, Claude-Étienne Minié, designed a soft lead bullet with a hollow base that expanded into the rifling when fired, so that a rifle could be loaded as quickly as a musket. Soldiers tore open a paper cartridge with their teeth, poured in the powder, and rammed the bullet home. The “minié ball” was accurate at several hundred yards, and it was devastating: where the old round ball often lodged in the flesh, the heavy conical bullet tore straight through, shattering bone and leaving surgeons with little choice but amputation. The Springfield Model 1855 was the first American rifle-musket built for it. Artillery grew deadlier too. Cannon fired solid shot that ripped through advancing ranks, exploding shells, and canisters of iron balls that turned a field piece into a giant shotgun at close range.
Weapons advanced faster than tactics. Commanders on both sides still massed infantry in long, deep formations and marched them across open ground to within fifty yards of an enemy armed with rifles, while cavalrymen and infantrymen alike carried revolvers and, later in the war, repeating rifles. The results were carnage on a scale no American had seen. Generals chose strategy over survival, hoping that numbers or sheer determination would carry the position. A cultural argument has been offered for why the Confederate armies, though often outnumbered, suffered a higher proportion of killed and wounded. It comes from the historians Grady McWhiney and Perry Jamieson, whose Attack and Die (1982) traced Confederate frontal assaults — and the Rebel Yell that accompanied them — to the Celtic, Scots-Irish ancestry of much of the white South, a warrior tradition of the charge. Many historians have challenged the thesis. Confederate soldiers did display extraordinary courage on the battlefield, and their frequent, desperate charges did produce a disproportionate share of casualties; the bold assaults of the rebel armies would become entwined with the South’s memory of the war. But the Celtic explanation needs the brake every cultural legacy needs: a legacy can help explain a pattern, but it does not command behavior. The men who charged at Gaines’ Mill and Malvern Hill were following orders and tactics, not bloodlines.[[24]]
The war was modern in one more way, and Lincoln was its first beneficiary. The telegraph let a president follow a battle almost as it happened. Through the war Lincoln spent long hours in the War Department’s telegraph office, reading dispatches as they came off the wire and sending orders back — urging pursuit, demanding action — to generals who did not always obey. I put that image at the center of the war’s command problem: a president who could see what needed doing, and generals who would not do it.[[25]]
### Bull Run
In the early summer of 1861, skirmishes in Missouri and western Virginia set the stage, and both sides were eager for a quick and glorious victory. General Scott, too old and too heavy to take the field, urged patience and was overruled. Command passed to General Irvin McDowell, a cautious staff officer who had never led an army, and on July 16 McDowell marched about 35,000 men out of Washington toward the railroad junction at Manassas, Virginia. Confidence ran high across the North. “What a picnic,” one New York volunteer recalled thinking, “to go down South for three months and clean up the whole business.” Congressmen and civilians followed the army in carriages with picnic baskets, expecting to watch a battle. Dozens of them were members of Congress, and the crowd included the British correspondent William Howard Russell and the photographer Mathew Brady. Almost as soon as the battle ended, Northern newspapers turned the crowd into a joke. The Boston Herald ran a comic poem by H. R. Tracy about careless picnickers who rode out to watch the fighting and then fled in their carriages over the dead and wounded, and the “picnic battle,” with its party mood, entered the record. But Centreville lay a seven-hour carriage ride from Washington, through country whose people now belonged to a rival nation, and nobody along that road owed a Union sightseer a meal. Those baskets carried necessities.[[26]]
The Confederates already knew they were coming. Rose O’Neal Greenhow, a Washington widow and society hostess with Southern sympathies, entertained officers and congressmen in her home near the White House and listened carefully. A young courier, Betty Duvall, carried one of Greenhow’s ciphered messages across the Potomac in a silk purse wound into the bun of her hair, warning General Beauregard that McDowell was on the march. Beauregard drew his roughly 22,000 men up along Bull Run, a stream a few miles from Manassas Junction, and called for reinforcements.[[27]]
On July 21, McDowell, unwilling to send green troops straight at the enemy line, maneuvered to strike the Confederate flank instead, and for the first two hours the Union flank attack drove back the outnumbered Confederates, who gave ground grudgingly. Then he hesitated, reluctant to extend his forces, and Confederate reinforcements arrived by road and by rail, among them a brigade under Thomas J. Jackson, whose stand on Henry House Hill earned him the name “Stonewall.” In the afternoon heat the Confederates counterattacked, and Jackson urged his men to “yell like furies.” “The peculiar corkscrew sensation that it sends down your backbone … can never be told,” one Union veteran later wrote of the Rebel Yell. “You have to feel it.” The Union line broke. Exhausted soldiers threw down their muskets, cut horses from their harnesses, and fled, overrunning the spectators’ carriages, and for two days stragglers wandered back into Washington. The Union lost 460 killed, 1,124 wounded, and 1,312 missing or captured; the Confederates lost 387 killed and 1,582 wounded. It was the largest and bloodiest battle in American history to that time. Davis reached the field after the fighting was over and set off celebrations in Richmond with a telegram: “We have won a glorious though dear-bought victory. Night closed on the enemy in full flight and closely pursued.” In the North, by contrast, early dispatches filed while McDowell was still advancing meant that much of the country woke on Monday morning to read that the Union had won. Lincoln, at first encouraged, was struck hard by the reports that followed.[[28]]
After days of alarm in the capital, and of public drunkenness among its beaten soldiers, calm returned and the North looked ahead. The defeat energized Davis and his armies. It sobered Lincoln, revealing the strength of the South, the caution of his own generals, and the length of the war ahead. Lincoln replaced McDowell with General George B. McClellan, a gifted organizer who would build the Army of the Potomac into a formidable force — and then hesitate to use it.[[29]]
### Two Theaters
The war ran in two theaters. In the East — Virginia, Maryland, and Pennsylvania — the Union aimed at Richmond. In the West, it aimed at the Mississippi River valley and the division of the Confederacy in two. (The two sides often named the same battle differently: the North tended to name battles after rivers and streams, the South after nearby towns, so that Bull Run was Manassas and Antietam was Sharpsburg. This book uses the most common names.) As I tell my students, in the East the Union “gets cooked,” while in the West it is winning — “until they put Bobby Lee in charge.”
The West came first. In February 1862, Ulysses S. Grant captured Forts Henry and Donelson on the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers, and on February 25 Nashville became the first Confederate state capital to fall. Grant pushed south, and the Confederate general Albert Sidney Johnston, whom Davis considered his finest officer, gathered some 44,000 men to destroy Grant’s army before reinforcements could reach it. On April 6, 1862, Johnston’s army struck Grant’s camps at Pittsburg Landing, Tennessee, near a log meetinghouse called Shiloh Church. The Union troops were caught unprepared; William Tecumseh Sherman had dismissed warnings of a Confederate advance. They gave ground all day, and in the afternoon Johnston, leading from the front, was struck behind the knee by a bullet that severed an artery. He bled to death within minutes. Reinforced overnight, Grant counterattacked on April 7 and drove the Confederates, now under Beauregard, from the field. The carnage was unprecedented: 23,746 casualties in two days, more than the combined casualties of the Revolution, the War of 1812, and the Mexican War. After Shiloh, Grant later wrote, he “gave up all idea of saving the Union except by complete conquest.”[[30]]
In the East, McClellan moved slowly and carefully. In the spring of 1862 he carried his army by water to the Virginia peninsula and advanced up it toward Richmond, until more than 100,000 men stood within a few miles of the Confederate capital. At Seven Pines, on May 31 and June 1, the Confederate commander, Joseph E. Johnston, was badly wounded, and Davis knew the moment of crisis had arrived: Richmond was not only the Confederacy’s political capital but its chief industrial city, and the Confederacy could not survive its loss. He turned to a dignified Virginian who had spent three decades in the old army, earning the respect of Northerners and Southerners alike, but had never commanded an army in the field. On June 1, 1862, Robert E. Lee took command of the force he would name the Army of Northern Virginia, and the course of the war changed.[[31]]
Lee was aggressive where his opponents were cautious, and he saw a weakness in the Union army’s very size: McClellan’s army needed hundreds of tons of supplies a day, carried along a vulnerable railroad line. Rejecting both retreat and passive defense, Lee attacked. In a week of battles beginning June 25 — the Seven Days — he drove McClellan away from Richmond. At Gaines’ Mill on June 27, more than 90,000 men clashed, and some 15,500 became casualties in six hours of fighting, more of them Confederate than Union. The campaign reversed the war. For a year, Northern armies had seemed to roll toward victory; the New York Times had predicted the war would be over by Independence Day. Instead, within a month, Lee had turned back the largest army the United States had ever put in the field, and in the North the shock was immense. Late in August Lee followed with a second victory at Bull Run, and he prepared to carry the war north.[[32]]
The first phase of the war had settled one thing: the conflict would be long and terribly bloody. In the West the Union had won the upper hand, and Grant’s victory at Shiloh had opened the Mississippi valley. In the East, McClellan’s caution and Lee’s daring had swung the pendulum of confidence toward the South. The South was galvanized by the heroic reputations of its generals, while Lincoln was forced to change commanders again and again, as his generals squandered opportunities to pursue and destroy retreating rebel armies. The carnage of 1862 convinced Lincoln, as it had convinced Grant at Shiloh, that the war could not be won halfway. During that summer he gave up hope of a negotiated reunion and committed the nation to a war that would mobilize all of the North’s resources — economic, political, and, in time, moral.
### Antietam
“We cannot afford to be idle,” Lee wrote to Davis on September 3, 1862, “and though weaker than our opponents in men and military equipments, must endeavor to harass, if we cannot destroy them.” He had several aims. Lee believed in always seizing the initiative and never letting an opponent dictate the terms of battle, and he wanted to keep the Union army on the defensive. He needed provisions for his ragged troops, and Northern farms could supply them. Meanwhile, alarmed by Confederate victories so close to the capital, the Union was building new fortifications around Washington. A victory in Maryland might draw that border state toward the Confederacy and help Democrats in the approaching Northern elections. And Britain and France, watching the war’s trajectory, might be persuaded to recognize the Confederacy. In the first week of September his army crossed the Potomac into Maryland and gathered near Frederick.[[33]]
There Lee faced a problem in his rear: a Union garrison of some 14,000 men at Harpers Ferry threatened his supply line to Virginia. He divided his army to capture it, sending Stonewall Jackson to surround the town, and set the plan down in Special Orders No. 191. The gamble paid off. On September 15 the garrison surrendered — some 12,500 prisoners, 73 cannon, 13,000 small arms, and 200 wagons. “Through God’s blessing,” Jackson informed Lee, “Harper’s Ferry and its garrison are to be surrendered.”[[34]]
But two days earlier, around noon on September 13, at a campsite near Frederick the Confederates had just left, Corporal Barton W. Mitchell of the 27th Indiana found three cigars wrapped in a sheet of paper. It was a copy of Special Orders No. 191, laying out the positions of Lee’s divided army. The paper went up the chain of command to McClellan, who exulted to General John Gibbon, “Here is a paper with which, if I cannot whip Bobbie Lee, I will be willing to go home.” Then he waited. His army did not begin to move for roughly eighteen hours, giving Lee time to learn that something had gone wrong and to fight delaying actions at the passes of South Mountain on September 14. When word came of Jackson’s success at Harpers Ferry, Lee gathered his army behind Antietam Creek, near the town of Sharpsburg.[[35]]
At dawn on September 17, 1862, McClellan attacked. His army outnumbered Lee’s by roughly two to one. Lee’s men were exhausted, hungry, and many of them sick, and they watched McClellan’s army assemble along the east side of the creek. The fighting began in a cornfield, where Union troops fired first and rebel regiments repulsed attack after attack, turning the field into a killing ground. Men fought at close range for hours, often hand to hand with bayonets. The fighting then moved to a sunken farm lane later called Bloody Lane, and finally to a stone bridge over Antietam Creek. By nightfall the two armies had suffered 22,717 casualties, including 3,654 killed. Antietam remains the deadliest single day of battle in American history. The next day McClellan, holding fresh reserves and the knowledge that Lee’s army was spent, let the Confederates withdraw across the Potomac without a fight. From his point of view, he had accomplished his mission of driving the invaders from Union soil. Lee withdrew into Virginia without accomplishing any of his objectives, having lost much of his army to casualties and desertion. In the North, few saw it as a victory. Lincoln wanted Lee’s army destroyed, and he would remove McClellan from command within two months. What became of the general who had promised to whip Bobby Lee or go home? I give my students four words: “He ended up home.”[[36]]
A month later the dead of Antietam reached New York. Mathew Brady hung a placard reading “The Dead of Antietam” at the door of his gallery and put up the photographs Alexander Gardner had taken on the field, and the New York Times wrote that if Brady “has not brought bodies and laid them in our door-yards and along the streets, he has done something very like it.”[[37]]
### Calling the W
Most military historians count Antietam a draw. Lincoln counted it a victory, because Lee had retreated, and because he desperately needed one. After eighteen months of war with victory nowhere in sight, casualties mounted and support for the war sagged. Midterm elections were approaching, and a promise to preserve the Union by force no longer seemed enough to justify more bloodshed. By September 1862, opposition was growing among Northern Democrats and even within Lincoln’s own party, and some Northern newspapers and businessmen were urging him to let the South go. I put the President’s problem to my students in political terms: “The enemy list is growing. The friend list is shrinking.” How did Lincoln change his enemies’ list? He needed a game changer.
Lincoln had one in his desk. On July 22, 1862, he had read his cabinet a draft proclamation freeing the slaves in the rebel states, and Secretary of State William Seward had advised him to wait for a military victory, so that it would not look like the desperate cry of a losing government. Antietam would have to do. As I tell my students: “It wasn’t really a clear-cut victory, but Lincoln didn’t care. It looked good enough. So let me call the W.” On September 22, 1862, just five days after Antietam, Lincoln issued a preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. Unless the rebellion ended by January 1, 1863, it declared, all persons held as slaves in any state or part of a state still in rebellion “shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free.” The proclamation spared the slave states loyal to the Union, whose slaveholders Lincoln could not afford to lose. But it changed the war. By committing the Union to emancipation, Lincoln made it far riskier for Britain and France, with their antislavery publics, to side with the Confederacy. The Republicans lost seats in the midterm elections that fall but kept control of the House.[[38]]
For eighteen months, the Union had fought a war to restore the nation as it was. The preliminary Proclamation announced that the nation would not be restored as it was. The enslaved had pressed the question from the first night at Fort Monroe; now the President had answered it, on a timetable of his own. The next chapter begins on January 1, 1863, when he kept his word.
## Questions for Consideration
1. Identify the advantages, ideologies, and strategies of the North and South. Which of the South’s advantages proved most durable, and which proved an illusion?
2. Identify the significant victories of the Union and Confederate armies in 1861–62, and explain the weaknesses and strengths of each side’s military leadership.
3. Who forced the question of slavery onto the Union’s war agenda: Lincoln, Congress, the generals, or the enslaved themselves? Use Fort Monroe and the Confiscation Acts as evidence.
4. The chapter applies a brake to the “Celtic” explanation of Confederate tactics: a legacy can explain, but it does not command. What would it take to prove that a cultural legacy, rather than tactics and orders, shaped how soldiers fought?
5. Lincoln held the Proclamation until a victory gave him cover, and then called a draw a win. Was that statesmanship or politics — and is there a difference?
## Then and Now
1. Butler had no authority to free anyone. The Fugitive Slave Law was still on the books, and his answer to the officer under the flag of truce was not that the law was wrong but that it did not reach a state claiming to be a foreign country — and that men set to work on enemy fortifications were property that could be seized like any other military asset. Contraband of war was a category that had never been applied to human beings until a general on the ground needed one. Congress followed him rather than the reverse; the confiscation bill came two months later. The target group changes after that, and so do the political language and the legal mechanism; the dynamic does not. In 1985 a fifteen-year-old girl who had fled the civil war in El Salvador was taken into federal immigration custody and held in a converted Pasadena motel, sharing a sleeping room with seven other children and five unrelated adult women, and the suit brought on her behalf produced a settlement in 1997 that still governs how the United States may hold a child: released to a parent, a relative, or an approved sponsor; if no sponsor can be found, a licensed child-care facility rather than a jail; food, water, medical care, education, recreation, and access to a lawyer. That settlement is the wall. Every administration since, of both parties, has run into the same legal and logistical limit, improvised a classification to get past it, and been sued over the improvisation. The law defines the process. It does not resolve the moral question.[[39]]
2. Test it. Is that the same mechanism — an official reaching for a classification the statute does not provide, because the people are already there and will not wait for Congress — or does the comparison collapse the difference between seizing property and holding a child? Notice what it does not settle. The Confederate officer at Fort Monroe considered Butler’s category a plain evasion of federal law, and so did Unionists in the border states who read it as emancipation by staff memo. Whichever case you would call a lawful improvisation and whichever an abuse, name what distinguishes them besides which outcome you would have preferred. Or set it aside and bring your own: find a classification an official has had to invent recently because the people in front of them arrived before the law did.
